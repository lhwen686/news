Research highlights long-term health risks of gestational diabetes

深度解析

妊娠期糖尿病（GDM）常被误认为分娩后即告终止的暂时性并发症。然而，这篇综述深入揭示了GDM作为一种重要的临床预警信号，其深远的健康影响可横跨母婴两代。从医学机制上看，GDM的发生是妊娠期特有的胰岛素抵抗和/或胰岛素分泌不足所致，其本质是母体胰岛B细胞功能对妊娠期增加的代谢需求代偿不足。即使分娩后血糖恢复正常，这种潜在的代谢脆弱性仍可能持续存在，使GDM女性未来患2型糖尿病（T2DM）和心血管疾病的风险显著增高，这体现了代谢记忆和疾病进程的延续性。对于子代，母体在宫内高血糖环境中的暴露，被认为是“胎儿编程”效应的关键机制之一。高血糖会影响胎儿的胰腺发育和代谢基因表达，从而增加其出生后肥胖和心血管代谢疾病的易感性。行业影响方面，该研究强调了GDM筛查和产后持续监测的重要性，将其从单一的妊娠并发症提升为一种跨生命周期的风险预测工具。这促使医疗机构需加强产后血糖检测和心血管风险因素管理，并推广个性化的生活方式干预（如体重管理、健康饮食、体育活动，以及母乳喂养的潜在保护作用）。这不仅能有效预防母亲的T2DM和心血管疾病，也为儿童的远期健康奠定基础，具有重要的公共卫生和社会经济价值，推动了精准预防医学的发展。

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: Credit: Pixabay/CC0 Public Domain Gestational diabetes is often viewed as a complication of pregnancy that resolves after delivery, but a new review led by researchers from LSU's Pennington Biomedical Research Center shows that its health implications can extend for years, increasing cardiometabolic risks for mothers and potentially affecting the future health of their children. In "Gestational diabetes mellitus and risk of long-term cardiometabolic disease," published in Nature Reviews Endocrinology, Dr. Yun Shen and Dr. Gang Hu of Pennington Biomedical and their colleagues examine evidence linking gestational diabetes mellitus, or GDM, with long-term health risks across two generations. Women with a history of GDM have a seven- to 10-fold higher risk of developing type 2 diabetes than women without GDM, with studies also showing increased risks of cardiovascular disease and hypertension. The risk of type 2 diabetes is highest approximately three to six years after delivery. Hu has focused on gestational diabetes and its long-term consequences for more than 15 years, and the review builds on that body of work. "Gestational diabetes gives clinicians a window into future health risk at a relatively young age," said Hu, director of the Chronic Disease Epidemiology Laboratory at Pennington Biomedical and the review's corresponding author. "Rather than waiting until diabetes or cardiovascular disease develops years later, we have an opportunity to begin prevention much earlier, when interventions may benefit not only the mother but potentially her child as well." The implications may also extend to the next generation. Exposure to maternal hyperglycemia or GDM in the womb is consistently associated with greater adiposity and cardiometabolic risk in children. The researchers caution, however, that the effects of the intrauterine environment can be difficult to separate from shared genetic and postnatal factors, making continued research important. "Gestational diabetes does not develop the same way in every patient," said Shen, first author of the review and assistant professor in Pennington Biomedical's Chronic Disease Epidemiology Laboratory. "Understanding differences in insulin resistance, insulin secretion, glucose levels and other risk factors may eventually help us determine which mothers and children would benefit most from closer monitoring and more personalized prevention strategies." The review emphasizes that the postpartum period represents an important but often underused opportunity to reduce future disease risk. Women with a history of GDM should receive glucose testing four to 12 weeks after delivery and continued monitoring even when their glucose levels return to normal. If postpartum testing is normal, screening for prediabetes or type 2 diabetes should continue every one to three years throughout life, along with monitoring of blood pressure, weight and other cardiovascular risk factors. Lifestyle interventions can also make a difference. Among the studies highlighted in the review was the Tianjin Gestational Diabetes Mellitus Prevention Program, a randomized trial led by Hu involving 1,180 women with previous GDM. Participants who received individualized counseling focused on weight management, physical activity and healthy dietary intake had a 46% lower incidence of type 2 diabetes than women who received usual care over an average follow-up of 4.5 years. The review also highlights breastfeeding as another potentially protective factor. Studies have associated breastfeeding and lactation with improved glucose metabolism and cardiovascular risk factors, while longer breastfeeding duration has been associated with a reduced risk of type 2 diabetes among women with previous GDM. The researchers conclude that immediate priorities include ensuring reliable postpartum testing and sustained access to prevention programs, while continuing research to determine how prevention and monitoring can be better personalized for women with GDM and their children. "The long-term implications of gestational diabetes demonstrate why research across the lifespan is so important," said Dr. Jennifer Rood, interim senior vice chancellor and executive director of Pennington Biomedical. "The work of Hu, Shen and their colleagues is helping connect what happens during pregnancy with the health of mothers and children years later. That knowledge creates opportunities to move from identifying risk to preventing disease." In addition to Shen and Hu, the review was authored by Wei Perng and Dana Dabelea of the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus; Yeyi Zhu and Erica Gunderson of Kaiser Permanente Northern California; Liwei Chen of the University of California, Los Angeles; Xilin Yang of Tianjin Medical University; and Jaakko Tuomilehto of the University of Helsinki. Publication details Yun Shen et al., Gestational diabetes mellitus and risk of long-term cardiometabolic disease, Nature Reviews Endocrinology (2026).
