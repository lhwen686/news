3D printing method could expand options for personalized cardiovascular devices

深度解析

本研究通过增材制造技术，利用镍钛诺这种具有超弹性与形状记忆特性的镍钛合金，在心血管和神经血管介入领域实现了显著突破。传统上，微创经导管装置（MITDs）在制造复杂几何形状和实现个性化定制方面面临挑战。研究团队成功开发出能够根据患者具体解剖结构定制的三维打印编织结构植入物。 核心创新在于其超越了传统晶格结构的局限性。晶格结构在经导管应用所需的大变形下，易产生过高的慢性外向力（COF），增加血管损伤和血栓形成的风险。而新型编织结构通过内部细丝的移动和重新定向来适应压缩，展现出卓越的弹性恢复能力（超过90%）和显著降低的COF（比传统结构低五倍），从而大大降低了植入相关的并发症，预示着更安全、更有效的介入治疗。未来，该技术有望结合两光子聚合和磁功能化技术，开发更小、更精密的神经血管装置，为大脑偏远病变提供创新的远程磁导航治疗方案，极大地拓展了介入医学的治疗疆界。

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: 3D-printed nitinol tapered flow-diverter stent with variable fiber density, sectional size and shape. Credit: IMDEA Materials Institute An international research team led by IMDEA Materials Institute and Universidad Politécnica de Madrid (UPM) has developed a method for manufacturing personalized cardiovascular medical devices using three-dimensional printing of nitinol, a superelastic alloy. This research, published in npj Metamaterials, also involved researchers from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) and the University of Galway, and enables the design and manufacture of highly flexible braided structures in a single production step. The technology has already been patented, and its potential has been recognized with the National James Dyson Award. These structures mimic blood vessels and can be compressed without failure, enabling their delivery through a catheter. The research addresses one of the principal challenges in the design and manufacture of minimally invasive transcatheter devices (MITDs): the difficulty of developing implants with complex three-dimensional geometries, tailored to the specific needs of individual patients and capable of being compressed for implantation through a catheter. "The clinical impact of nitinol-based MITDs has been transformative for cardiovascular interventions," explained Dr. Carlos Aguilar Vega, one of the authors of the publication. 3D-printed nitinol customized aortic valve frame prototype reconstructed from patient CT scans. Credit: IMDEA Materials Institute Limits of conventional implant structures "However, despite their widespread clinical adoption, current manufacturing methods impose fundamental geometric limitations that restrict their optimization and personalization. "While recent advances in powder metallurgy-based techniques, such as spark plasma sintering, have expanded the manufacturing possibilities for these devices, it remains challenging to develop implants that combine complex geometries, mechanical flexibility and patient-specific anatomical adaptation," he added. Most metallic medical devices produced through additive manufacturing are based on lattice structures, consisting of networks of interconnected elements arranged in repeating patterns. While these architectures are relatively straightforward to design and manufacture, their mechanical behavior relies primarily on the bending of rigid struts. This represents a major limitation for transcatheter applications, where implants must be compressed to fit inside small-diameter catheters, navigate through blood vessels, and subsequently recover their original shape after deployment, all without suffering permanent deformation or fracture. The researchers found that conventional lattice structures are unable to withstand the large deformations required by these procedures. As they are compressed, they become excessively stiff, exert high forces on arterial walls and face a significant risk of fracture at strains approaching 25%. 3D-printed nitinol prototype of a structurally closed, fenestrated bifurcation stent. Credit: IMDEA Materials Institute Braided filaments absorb greater compression Braided architectures, by contrast, behave very differently. Rather than deforming through the bending of rigid elements, they accommodate deformation through the movement and reorientation of interwoven filaments, in a manner similar to conventional braided stents or textile meshes. To achieve the biomedical-grade surface finish required for implantation, the team applied a combined chemical etching and electropolishing treatment. Testing showed that this post-processing step reduced fiber thickness by 47.7% and cross-sectional area by 71.1% compared with the as-printed structures. As a result, the new braided stents were able to withstand compressions of up to 50% of their original diameter while maintaining more than 90% elastic recovery. In addition, the chronic outward force (COF) was up to five times lower than that observed in conventional lattice structures, significantly reducing the risk of vascular injury and thrombosis. "Lattice structures manufactured using conventional additive manufacturing techniques are unable to achieve the high degree of reversible deformation required for transcatheter applications," said Aguilar Vega. "Braided architectures, on the other hand, can readily accommodate the large deformations required while maintaining both structural integrity and elastic recovery." Further testing before clinical investigations The research team successfully validated several prototypes from a mechanical standpoint using widely accepted performance indicators and a single compression-expansion cycle that replicates the actual insertion of the device into a catheter and its subsequent deployment. Although these results are promising, the next steps include in vitro biocompatibility testing and animal studies before clinical investigations in patients can begin. Smaller devices for hard-to-reach brain vessels The researchers also identify the development of neurovascular devices capable of addressing diseases in remote and difficult-to-access regions of the brain as one of the next frontiers in the field. Achieving this goal will require further miniaturization of complex NiTi structures, exploring technologies such as ultra-high-precision three-dimensional printing based on two-photon polymerization using metallic suspensions, as well as magnetic functionalization to enable remote magnetic navigation. These are among the research directions that IMDEA Materials Institute intends to pursue in the coming months. More information Carlos Aguilar Vega et al., Additive manufacturing of superelastic NiTi braided metamaterials: an innovative strategy for engineering minimally invasive vascular medical devices, npj Metamaterials (2026).
