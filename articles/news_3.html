野生动物进口与人畜共患病暴发风险增高相关

Wildlife import linked to higher risk of animal-human disease outbreaks

深度解析

本研究通过严谨的统计学方法，揭示了全球野生动物进口量与人畜共患新兴传染病（EID）暴发风险之间存在显著的、进口驱动的关联。核心专业概念在于“人畜共患溢出效应”，即野生动物携带的病原体跨越物种屏障感染人类。文章强调，高野生动物进口量增加了“新型病原体”进入并传播至当地人群的几率，尤其当本地人群对这些外来病原体缺乏“免疫力”时，极易引发疾病暴发。 研究采用两种统计路径，包括对发生几率和数量的常规分析以及对极端事件的专门评估，通过“优势比”（1.23）量化了风险增量，并巧妙地通过纳入非人畜共患新兴传染病作为对照，排除了“报告偏差”的影响，使结论更具说服力。这一发现对全球公共卫生和“大流行病预防”具有深远影响。它明确指出，未来政策应聚焦于加强“进口检查点”的风险管理，包括对活体野生动物实施严格的“检疫”、前瞻性“病原体筛查”以及强化“公共卫生监测”。此举旨在从源头阻断潜在的病原体传播链，从而有效降低人畜共患病疫情的发生概率及极端暴发事件的破坏性，对于构建全球健康安全屏障至关重要。

DOI: 10.1016/j.onehlt.2026.101565 In recent research published in the journal One Health, "Exploring the Impacts of Wildlife Trade on Zoonotic Spillover and Disease Outbreaks: A Multiple-perspective Study," researchers from Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University (XJTLU) have discovered that wildlife imports strongly influence the likelihood of zoonotic emerging infectious disease (EID) outbreaks. This marks the first global measurement of how wildlife trade is linked to outbreaks of animal-borne diseases, across countries and over time. "While previous papers have identified the spatial link between EID occurrences and other factors such as population growth rate, GDP and climatic conditions, wildlife trade volumes remain untested," says Dr. Lingyun Xiao, one of the corresponding authors of the paper from the Academy of Life and Natural Sciences at XJTLU. A global multiyear study The researchers analyzed data from 214 countries over 39 years using two statistical approaches: The first tested whether countries that traded more wildlife in a given year were also more likely to report a zoonotic EID occurrence, and whether they reported more of them. The second focused on extremes, testing whether years of unusually high wildlife trade coincide with unusually severe disease outbreaks. In both cases, the researchers looked at imports and exports separately. While in theory wildlife trade is expected to be linked mainly to zoonotic pathogens, the study also included nonzoonotic EIDs as a comparison check, testing whether reporting bias or overall trade volume could explain the link. For instance, countries with strong surveillance might detect more occurrences of both zoonotic and nonzoonotic EIDs. "If wildlife trade were simply a proxy for general trade or reporting bias, we would expect it to be associated with nonzoonotic EIDs too," Xiao says. "The fact that the strongest and most specific signal was for zoonotic EIDs strengthens the case that this is not just reporting noise." The asymmetry between wildlife imports and exports Their findings showed that higher wildlife import volume was associated with higher odds of zoonotic EID occurrence. The odds ratio was 1.23, meaning that a one-unit increase in wildlife import volume was associated with about 23% higher odds of zoonotic EID occurrence. In contrast, wildlife exports showed no significant association. Kai Wang, a Ph.D. student from the Academy of Life and Natural Sciences at XJTLU, explains that wildlife imports may increase the chance of a novel pathogen entering a country and affecting humans, which may lead a country to record an outbreak in a year it otherwise would not have. It is important to note, however, that while wildlife import volume significantly increases the chance of zoonotic EID emergence, it does not affect the frequency. Once an EID has occurred, additional factors beyond wildlife imports contribute to the number of cases in the country, such as the effectiveness of containment, surveillance intensity and public health response. Extreme cases The researchers discovered a striking finding in their analysis of extreme cases: Countries with very high wildlife imports also tend to record a very high number of zoonotic outbreaks. Dr. Chengxiu Ling, a corresponding author from the Academy of Life and Natural Sciences at XJTLU, says this means that extreme zoonotic EID is 40% more likely to co-occur with high wildlife import volumes. Conversely, the link was far weaker for exports. Additionally, the analysis identified other factors, such as mammalian richness, population density and population growth, which elevate the risk of EID. "This reveals a critical import-driven risk profile: Wildlife importation is associated with both the probability of zoonotic EID occurrence and extreme outbreak events," says Ling. This is especially significant for pandemic prevention since events that pose the highest public health impact are often rare, extreme outbreaks instead of average conditions. Future pandemic prevention recommendations These findings and examples emphasize the need for stronger risk management at import checkpoints. Strengthening quarantine, pathogen screening and public health surveillance for imported live wildlife are among the recommended practices to prevent future outbreaks. Ling explains that imported wildlife can carry new pathogens that local populations have never encountered and to which they have no immunity. "Exportation may reflect broader wildlife exploitation and increased human–wildlife encounters. But our findings suggest that zoonotic risk is more likely associated with the intensity of wildlife importation than with overall trade activity. That is why the policy focus should be on importation points." More information Kai Wang et al., Exploring the impacts of wildlife trade on zoonotic spillover and disease outbreaks: A multiple-perspective study, One Health (2026).
