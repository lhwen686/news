Normal-looking sleep can mask memory problems, study suggests

深度解析

这项研究揭示了非快速眼动（non-REM）睡眠期间特定海马体中间神经元在记忆巩固中的关键作用及其精细的分子机制。核心概念是Chrm2基因高表达的中间神经元通过乙酰胆碱（ACh）水平的波动来调控海马波纹（hippocampal ripples）的产生。在非REM睡眠期间，脑内的ACh水平自然降低，解除了对这些Chrm2受体表达神经元的抑制性“化学制动”，使其变得活跃。这些活跃的中间神经元进而促进了海马波纹的生成，而海马波纹被认为是记忆痕迹重放和强化的神经基础。 这项发现具有深远的行业影响。首先，它提供了一个针对认知障碍的潜在基因靶点。许多神经退行性疾病（如阿尔茨海默病）和精神疾病都伴随记忆障碍。通过调控这些Chrm2神经元的活动或ACh信号通路，未来可能开发出增强记忆巩固的药物或基因疗法。其次，研究强调了标准睡眠评估的局限性，即表观正常的睡眠模式下，记忆巩固可能已经受损，这提示临床诊断需更深入地探究睡眠相关的神经回路功能。最后，它深化了我们对睡眠在认知功能中作用的理解，为开发基于神经科学原理的睡眠干预措施以改善学习和记忆提供了新的理论基础。

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: Credit: SHVETS production from Pexels A new mouse study that set out to test how sleep helps stabilize new memories has identified cells that may offer a genetic target for accessing circuits implicated in cognitive disorders. Researchers led by Min Xu and Wanling Peng from the Institute of Neuroscience at the Chinese Academy of Sciences focused on interneurons that gate the generation of ripples, which are brief bursts of electrical activity that are critical for memory consolidation. The targeted cells lie in the hippocampus, a brain region important for learning and memory. When researchers quieted these brain cells, it impaired the animals' ability to remember where objects had been or recognize a previously encountered mouse. The experiments suggest that sleep can look normal on standard measurements while the work of strengthening memories falters. The study, published in Neuron, connects chemical changes during sleep to the activity that helps new memories persist. Remembering after rest The team genetically modified a group of mice so that they could monitor and selectively control a population of neurons with high expression of the Chrm2 gene. These cells become particularly active during non-rapid-eye-movement, or non-REM, sleep and were also active during quiet wakefulness. To test the role of these neurons in memory, the researchers let adult male mice explore two identical objects. The animals then returned to their home cages for five hours, during which they mostly slept. Before they returned to the arena, the researchers moved one object. Credit: Neuron (2026). DOI: 10.1016/j.neuron.2026.09.031 A group of control mice spent more time investigating the relocated object, indicating that they remembered the earlier arrangement. Mice whose targeted neurons had been suppressed after learning showed little preference. Yet standard measurements detected no changes in their sleep-wake patterns or broad brain-wave activity between the two groups. To pinpoint when the cells mattered, the team used light to suppress the neurons specifically during non-REM sleep after learning. This also impaired later memory performance. Applying the suppression during wakefulness and REM sleep did not produce the same deficit. A separate experiment extended the finding to social memory. Suppressing corresponding cells in another part of the hippocampus after a social encounter disrupted the mice's usual preference for investigating an unfamiliar mouse over one they had already met. A chemical brake comes off Why do these cells become especially active during sleep? The researchers investigated acetylcholine, a chemical messenger that can be measured at relatively high levels during active wakefulness and REM sleep but low levels during non-REM sleep. The Chrm2 gene encodes a receptor through which acetylcholine can suppress neuronal activity. As acetylcholine levels fall, that chemical brake eases, allowing the targeted cells to become more active. The team linked this activity to hippocampal ripples associated with replaying and strengthening memories. A 2022 study had identified cells that fired rapidly around these events. The new genetic tools allowed researchers to test how a sleep-active population of neurons contributes to both ripples and memory. Stimulating the targeted cells during non-REM sleep increased ripple occurrence, while suppressing them reduced it. The authors call the neurons "a critical node in the ripple generation network," yet exactly how they coordinate with neighboring cells to produce those bursts remains unresolved. Analysis of existing human brain data revealed a similar population expressing CHRM2, although its role in human sleep and memory has yet to be established. The mouse experiments do however give researchers a way to investigate an important distinction: Memory consolidation can falter even when standard sleep measurements appear normal.
