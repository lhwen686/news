上消化道内窥镜检查患者，吸入大麻者可能比食用大麻者需要更多麻醉。

Upper endoscopy patients who inhale cannabis may need more anesthesia than edible users

深度解析

该研究深入揭示了大麻使用途径对麻醉需求，特别是异丙酚剂量在进行上消化道内窥镜检查（EGD）时的差异性影响。核心专业概念在于大麻中主要精神活性成分四氢大麻酚（THC）的药代动力学在不同给药途径下的显著差异。吸烟或吸食电子烟形式的THC通过肺部迅速吸收，作用起效快，且长期吸入可能导致上气道黏膜刺激，增加气道反应性，进而可能需要更深度的镇静以确保患者在内窥镜通过时的舒适度和安全性。相比之下，口服THC需经过肝脏首过代谢，产生不同的活性代谢产物（如11-羟基-THC），其吸收更慢，起效时间更长，且可能不会引起相同的局部气道刺激。 这项研究对临床麻醉和胃肠病学领域具有重要影响。它促使麻醉师在术前访视时，超越简单的“是否使用大麻”询问，转而详细了解患者的大麻使用方式、频率和末次使用时间。这种精细化的信息收集有助于麻醉师更准确地预判镇静需求，调整麻醉药物剂量（如异丙酚），实现个性化的麻醉方案规划，从而优化患者体验，提高手术安全性，避免镇静不足或过度镇静的风险。此外，它也为解释此前关于大麻与麻醉相互矛盾的研究结果提供了新的视角，强调未来研究应区分大麻使用途径，以获得更具临床指导意义的结论。

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: Credit: Unsplash/CC0 Public Domain Patients who smoke or vape cannabis may need more anesthesia during an upper endoscopy than those who use edibles or do not use cannabis, according to a new study presented at the Anesthesiology 2026 annual meeting. The study suggests "not all cannabis is equal" when it comes to sedation during procedures and that the route of cannabis use may matter for anesthetic planning. "The most important message for patients is to be honest with their anesthesiologist about cannabis, including how they use it—smoking, vaping or edible products—how often they use it and when they last used it," said Basavana Goudra, M.D., FCARCSI, FRCA, FASA, lead author of the study and an anesthesiologist at Sidney Kimmel Medical College in Philadelphia. "For physicians, simply asking, 'Do you use cannabis?' may no longer be sufficient." The study authors, who included Katherine Green from Johns Hopkins University in Baltimore and Ankitha Jagadeesh from Rutgers University in New Brunswick, New Jersey, reviewed 280 gastrointestinal endoscopic procedures (i.e., colonoscopies and upper endoscopies) at a single center. The analysis included 264 patients with complete information about their cannabis use: 217 nonusers, 38 who reported inhaled use and nine who reported oral use, such as gummies or other edibles. When the researchers looked at all cannabis users together, they found no significant difference in the initial propofol dose compared with nonusers. But when they separated patients by how they used cannabis, a sharper pattern emerged. Overall, the average initial propofol dose was 138.7 mg among nonusers, 168.5 mg among those who inhaled cannabis and 114.2 mg among those who consumed it orally. This suggests that, unlike those who smoked or vaped cannabis, those who used edibles did not appear to have the same increased need for propofol, although the edible group was small. The difference was most substantial among patients undergoing upper endoscopy (EGD). Patients who inhaled cannabis required substantially higher doses during EGD, a procedure in which an endoscope, a thin tube with a camera used to look inside the body, passes through the patient's mouth, throat and upper airway. In EGD patients, inhaled cannabis users required 192.8 mg of propofol, compared with 151.4 mg among nonusers and 145.0 mg among oral cannabis users. No meaningful differences were observed among patients undergoing colonoscopy. The findings may help explain conflicting results in earlier research on cannabis and anesthesia, which often grouped cannabis users together without distinguishing how they consumed it. Inhaled and edible cannabis products are processed differently by the body. Smoking or vaping delivers tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), the main psychoactive component in cannabis, rapidly through the lungs, while edible THC is processed through the liver, producing different metabolites and changing how quickly the effects begin and how long they last. The researchers also noted that regular inhalation of cannabis may irritate the upper airway, potentially requiring deeper anesthesia to help patients tolerate the endoscope. The authors said the results do not suggest patients who inhale cannabis should delay or avoid medically necessary endoscopy. Instead, the findings underscore the importance of sharing complete and accurate information before a procedure so anesthesiologists can anticipate sedation needs and tailor care to the patient.
