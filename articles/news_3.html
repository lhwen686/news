运动是否弊大于利？微心脏研究正在揭示新答案。

Can exercise do more harm than good? Mini-heart research is uncovering new answers

深度解析

“微型运动员”平台的核心在于其“芯片上的心脏”技术，它利用微流控设备模拟人类心脏组织的生理功能。关键技术原理包括：首先，从患者的皮肤或肌肉细胞中提取，通过细胞重编程技术将其转化为诱导多能干细胞（iPSCs），随后将这些iPSCs定向分化为患者特异性的心肌细胞。其次，研究人员通过精确的电脉冲刺激，在体外环境中模拟高强度耐力运动时心脏的跳动频率（如每分钟150次），从而观察心肌组织在生理压力下的反应。通过测量心肌的收缩强度变化和信号物质的释放，该平台能有效地反映真实心脏对运动负荷的适应性与损伤机制。 该技术的行业影响深远：在临床应用层面，它为心血管疾病的个性化诊疗提供了前所未有的工具，能够识别个体对运动相关心脏损伤的易感性，从而制定更为精准的运动康复或训练方案。在药物研发方面，该平台有望成为药物筛选和毒性评估的更高效、更具人体相关性的模型，减少对动物实验的依赖。此外，它还极大地推动了心血管疾病病理机制的研究，为运动心脏病学和临床实践提供了深入理解心脏功能的创新途径。这种高保真的人体模型正引领心血管研究进入一个更精准、更个性化的新时代。

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: Credit: Rikkert Harink, University of Twente "Exercise is medicine" is a commonly quoted phrase. But as is so often the case with medications, more is not always better. What counts as too much exercise, and how can it affect the heart? Does running a marathon or competing in an Ironman triathlon boost your health, or might it do the opposite? The development of the Micro-Athlete mini-heart (a heart-on-a-chip that mimics the human heart) is giving researchers valuable new insights into cardiovascular disease. Micro-Athlete "Picture a miniature heart running a marathon inside a laboratory," says Thijs Eijsvogels, an associate professor in the Medical BioSciences department of the Radboud University Medical Center. He is a member of the HealthTech Nexus team, which is developing an in vitro platform based on cultured human cardiac tissue. Electrical pulses are used to stimulate the tissue, replicating the conditions of sustained endurance exercise. Researchers can tune the stimulation, varying its intensity from low to high and its duration from one to four hours. "We can simulate sports conditions by making the heart tissue beat at a frequency of 150 beats per minute," Eijsvogels explains. But how accurately can this heart-on-a-chip platform predict how a real human heart behaves? "Our results are promising. The stimulated heart tissue shows the same change in squeezing strength and release of signaling substances as in people who exercise intensively," says Eijsvogels. "This means the platform mirrors how the human heart reacts, making it a reliable starting point for follow-up studies." Technology based on patients' own cells The cultured cardiac tissue is derived from human stem cells—versatile cells capable of developing into a wide range of cell types, from muscle cells to neurons. "We harvest skin or muscle cells from patients with a heart condition and reprogram them into stem cells," says Robert Passier, a University of Twente professor specializing in stem cells and cardiovascular diseases. "We can then grow cardiac tissue cultures from those stem cells. By studying how that tissue behaves, we can identify the cause of the patient's heart disease." The researchers see the platform as an important milestone on the path to advanced human-based models for cardiovascular disease research. As an added benefit, it also reduces the need for animal experiments. Eijsvogels explains, "Being forced to run or swim can put considerable stress on an animal's heart. As a result, the key question is whether the effects on their heart are due to stress or exertion." Impact Ultimately, this platform could help researchers understand why extreme exertion can be harmful to the heart and why some people are more susceptible to these risks than others. It may also pave the way for more personalized training and treatment plans, benefiting patients recovering from surgery as well as recreational and top athletes. "A platform like this can help identify those individuals who are most vulnerable to exertion-related heart damage and those who are less at risk," says Passier. "That information can help doctors recommend the best strategy for each patient, such as determining how intensely someone can safely exercise given their personal risk level." What's next The current platform is still a fairly simple representation of a human heart, since it consists mainly of heart muscle cells. The human heart is much more intricate and includes many other cell types that have not yet been integrated into the model. Passier notes, "One of the next steps is to integrate those other cell types into the model and find out whether that alters the outcomes. We are constantly working to refine and improve the platform." The initial findings are highly promising, and the research team is now preparing its first publication. The researchers have already identified several promising avenues for using the technology to study other heart conditions. To that end, close collaboration is indispensable. Eijsvogels states, "It is important that we carry out this research together. In that way, we will be able to deepen our understanding of how the heart functions. The Micro-Athlete platform can also provide answers to other questions in sports cardiology and clinical practice."
