深度解析

这项研究深刻揭示了长期体力活动模式与神经系统疾病风险之间的重要关联。核心机制在于，规律的体育锻炼能够显著改善心血管健康，这通过有效管理体重、优化血糖水平及控制血压得以实现。肥胖、糖尿病和高血压是公认的心血管疾病危险因素，它们不仅直接损害血管内皮功能，增加动脉粥样硬化风险，进而诱发缺血性脑卒中及短暂性脑缺血发作（TIA），还可能通过慢性炎症、氧化应激和微血管病变等途径，加速神经退行性病变进程，从而增加痴呆症和帕金森病的风险。文章特别指出抑郁症作为后期神经系统疾病的强预测因子，可能提示其与神经炎症、神经递质失衡或早期神经退行性变存在共同的病理生理学基础。 从行业影响来看，此项研究强调了可穿戴设备在公共卫生和预防医学领域的巨大潜力。通过长期、客观地收集个体活动数据，健身追踪器不再仅仅是健身辅助工具，而是成为了早期风险识别和健康干预的强大平台。这种大数据驱动的、个性化健康监测模式，能够帮助临床医生和公共卫生机构更精准地识别高危人群，实现早期生活方式干预和疾病管理，从而有望延缓甚至预防神经系统疾病的发生，减轻个人和社会医疗负担。它预示着未来医疗将更加注重预防和个性化干预，将日常行为数据转化为有价值的临床洞察。

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: Credit: Unsplash/CC0 Public Domain Adults who averaged fewer than 5,000 steps a day were later diagnosed with dementia, Parkinson's disease or stroke at almost twice the rate of those averaging more than 12,000 steps, according to a new study presented at the 151st Annual Meeting of the American Neurological Association (ANA). The study, which analyzed a year of fitness-tracker data from more than 22,000 people, suggests that long-term activity patterns tracked by these popular devices may offer clues about future brain health. People in the least-active group were more likely to have obesity, diabetes, hypertension and depression than those in the more active groups. The findings reflect the close connection between cardiovascular health and brain health. Physical activity can help people manage their weight, blood pressure and blood sugar, factors that are also linked to stroke and cognitive decline. "Low activity and conditions such as obesity, diabetes, high blood pressure and depression are a package deal, part of a broader health picture. Data from fitness trackers could help identify those who need earlier attention to their weight, blood sugar, blood pressure or mental health—all health concerns that we know how to address," said Dr. Meet Popatbhai Kachhadia, lead author of the study and a neurology resident at Florida Atlantic University's Charles E. Schmidt College of Medicine in Boca Raton, Florida. "The takeaway is that how much you move over a year may offer a window into your overall brain-and-body health." The study is likely the first to use a year of wearable-device data from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) All of Us Research Program, which focuses on diversity, including recruiting people who have historically been left out of medical research. Researchers used the data to determine how sustained, yearlong activity patterns related to newly diagnosed dementia, Parkinson's disease or stroke. They assessed the average daily step counts of 22,517 American adults ages 40 and older and then followed them for five years, identifying diagnoses of neurological disease in their electronic health records. Participants were classified into one of three groups based on their median daily activity level—low activity (4,800 steps a day), moderate activity (around 8,300 steps a day) and high activity (roughly 12,600 steps a day). Researchers found that, unlike information provided by a single fitness tracker reading or questionnaire responses, examining the pattern of physical activity over an extended period can better predict who is likely to develop neurological disease. Over an average of about five years of follow-up, 343 participants received a new diagnosis of dementia, Parkinson's disease, stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA, sometimes called a ministroke). The rates increased as activity declined: about 3.8 neurological diagnoses for every 1,000 people followed for one year in the least-active group, compared with 2.7 in the moderately active group and 2.1 in the most-active group—about an 80% higher rate in the least-active group. More active participants also had a lower average body mass index (BMI) and lower rates of diabetes, high blood pressure and depression. Specifically, researchers observed the following associations: Average BMI (from 18.5 to 24.9 is considered healthy in adults) Average BMI of participants was 32.1 in the low-activity group, 28.4 in the moderate-activity group and 26.5 in the high-activity group. Rate of diabetes Low-activity group: 5.6%; moderate-activity group: 2.5%; high-activity group: 2.0%. Rate of high blood pressure Low-activity group: 13.4%; moderate-activity group: 9.5%; high-activity group: 9.2%. Rate of depression (which researchers said was the single strongest predictor of later neurological disease, although they noted that depression can be an early symptom of dementia and Parkinson's disease) Low-activity group: 8.6%; moderate-activity group: 5.1%; high-activity group: 3.1%. The activity level of each group remained consistent from the first to the last month of the study, which is why someone with a pattern of low step counts over a 12-month period should view it as a prompt to have their health checked rather than a verdict about their brain, said Kachhadia. He notes that more research is needed, including factoring in activity type and intensity. "Everybody should develop a regular habit of physical activity, setting realistic, sustainable fitness goals to improve their health," said Dr. Marc A. Swerdloff, ABPN, FANA, co-author and clinical affiliate associate professor of neurology at Florida Atlantic University and Marcus Neuroscience Institute in Boca Raton, Florida. "For example, people averaging around 8,000 steps a day had meaningfully lower rates of neurologic disease than the least-active group. That's a realistic target, and it aligns with federal guidance of roughly 150 minutes of moderate activity per week. Nobody needs to reach 12,000." Who's behind this story?
