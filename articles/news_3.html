脑损伤后膀胱感染加剧认知障碍，研究发现

Bladder infection after brain injury worsens cognitive problems, finds study

深度解析

本研究揭示了创伤性脑损伤（TBI）后发生的泌尿道感染（UTI）对认知功能具有显著的负面影响，这涉及复杂的神经免疫机制。核心专业概念在于，TBI不仅直接损害大脑，还可能通过改变机体对周围感染的反应，进一步加重神经炎症和认知障碍。研究发现，TBI本身会导致膀胱免疫细胞增加，提示脑损伤可能引起外周器官的免疫功能改变，从而增加UTI的易感性。更重要的是，TBI后UTI会引发大脑多个区域炎症相关蛋白的延迟性升高，表明外周感染通过“脑-膀胱轴”激活了中枢神经系统（CNS）的神经炎症，从而加剧了空间记忆等认知功能的损害。细胞因子IL-6在TBI后升高而UTI清除后减弱，暗示了炎症反应与抗炎活性的复杂动态平衡。 这项研究对临床实践具有重要影响。鉴于UTI在TBI患者中普遍存在并与谵妄及再入院率升高相关，本发现强调了在TBI患者管理中，对UTI进行早期识别、有效治疗及预防的重要性。它提示临床医生应将外周感染视为TBI长期预后不可忽视的因素，促使TBI康复方案考虑免疫调节和感染控制策略，以减轻神经炎症并保护认知功能。未来，这可能催生针对TBI患者的创新性感染预防措施、免疫调节疗法，甚至开发新的生物标志物来预测和监测TBI后的认知恢复。

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: Credit: Pixabay/CC0 Public Domain A urinary tract infection that follows a moderate brain injury can worsen cognitive problems caused by the injury, new research in mice shows. People who have experienced a traumatic brain injury (TBI) are susceptible to many types of infections, especially in the lungs. Yet previous studies have shown that bladder infections in this population are not uncommon: 15% of adult brain injury patients experience a urinary tract infection (UTI) within a year, and UTIs are responsible for up to 20% of hospital readmissions that occur within a year after a TBI. In addition, UTIs are known to cause delirium-like symptoms in medically vulnerable people. This new study, led by researchers at The Ohio State University and published recently in the Journal of Neurotrauma, is the first publication to examine neuroimmune and cognitive effects of a post-brain injury bladder infection in an animal model. "This very common infection is happening in TBI survivors and probably those who don't even report it or go to the hospital, and it's associated with observed and perceived cognitive changes. We thought UTI could be something that's having a much bigger impact on recovery after brain injury than we currently appreciate," said senior author Olga Kokiko-Cochran, an investigator in the Chronic Brain Injury Program and the Institute of Brain, Behavior, and Immunology (IBBI) at Ohio State and associate professor of neuroscience in the College of Medicine. "Let's not overlook UTI," she said. "Sometimes these common experiences that are overlooked are important, and I think we can't presume that an infection of any sort is no big deal." Injuries in this work are moderate in severity and resemble the type of TBI that could result from a fall—the most common cause of traumatic brain injury. The experiments involved four groups of female mice: those with a TBI (performed surgically under anesthesia) and "sham" mice, which had surgery but no brain injury. Three days later (again, under anesthesia), either uropathogenic E. coli bacteria, the main cause of UTIs, or a control treatment was injected into their urethras. Bladder infection amplified memory loss Results from behavioral tests six days after injury showed that the TBI impaired the animals' spatial memory, and a UTI following the injury "greatly exaggerated those cognitive impairments," said first author Zachary Zimomra, a neuroscience research associate and manager of Kokiko-Cochran's lab. That UTI-induced cognitive impairment was associated with delayed increases in two inflammation-related proteins in two different cell types in several regions of the injured brains compared with controls. This finding suggested that brain immune cells were responding not just to the TBI, but to the bladder infection as well. And bladder immune cells changed after brain injury alone—an intriguing clue that could help explain why infections follow a TBI. "Maybe there's some susceptibility we don't even appreciate," Kokiko-Cochran said. "Because we do see, post-TBI, a higher immune cell presence in the bladder." Clues to a brain-bladder link The team looked further into changes in inflammatory protein signaling linked to the injury and infection, finding that one cytokine, IL-6, was elevated in the blood after TBI and then muted by the UTI—suggesting that clearance of the infection prompted anti-inflammatory activity. Cellular changes seen in the bladder and brain also offer hints at the biology behind post-injury infection that researchers plan to investigate further. A better understanding of how the brain and bladder communicate—beyond their known neural connection related to urination—could lead to infection prevention strategies that improve long-term TBI outcomes, the researchers say. "Clinically, there is something about UTI that causes a behavioral change in cognition that you don't necessarily see from influenza or a cold or some other infection. It may be something with the bladder itself," Zimomra said. "We want to learn what happens within the brain, but also how changes in the bladder following TBI exaggerate or enhance these behavioral effects." Next studies will broaden the scope UTIs affect 400 million people worldwide each year, and females are 30 times more likely than males to experience a bladder infection—which is why this initial study was done in female mice. But UTI after a brain injury is not restricted to females, meaning future research will include animals of both sexes, Kokiko-Cochran said. "This has been an opportunity for our lab to contribute something new that has, we think, important translational relevance and could raise awareness and help us think about how this type of infection is layered on top of a brain injury," she said. "There's a lot more we can do to understand the brain-bladder connection." Publication details Zachary Zimomra et al, Urinary Tract Infection Exaggerates Cognitive Deficits and Region-Specific Neuroinflammation Following Traumatic Brain Injury, Journal of Neurotrauma (2026).
