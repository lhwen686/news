Your genes might boost risk for high cholesterol

深度解析

这篇医学新闻深入探讨了基因与饮食在胆固醇管理和心血管疾病风险中的复杂交互作用。文章核心在于揭示个体对饱和脂肪摄入的反应存在显著的基因差异性。低密度脂蛋白（LDL）胆固醇，即俗称的“坏胆固醇”，是动脉粥样硬化及后续心血管事件（如卒中和心肌梗死）的关键驱动因素。饱和脂肪摄入会影响肝脏的胆固醇代谢，进而升高LDL水平。本研究强调，并非所有人对饱和脂肪的代谢反应都相同，某些基因型会使个体对高饱和脂肪饮食更为敏感，导致LDL水平显著升高，并大幅增加心血管疾病风险。 这项发现对临床实践和公共卫生具有深远影响。它推动了“精准营养”或“个性化医学”理念的发展，预示着未来的饮食干预将不再是“一刀切”的普适性建议，而是可能基于个体的基因图谱进行定制。通过多基因风险评分等工具，医生可以更早、更精准地识别那些对饱和脂肪特别敏感的高风险人群，从而制定更具针对性的预防策略，包括更严格的饮食控制或早期药物干预。这有助于优化资源分配，提高干预效果，并最终更有效地降低人群的心血管疾病负担。

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: Credit: Pixabay/CC0 Public Domain Frustrated that your cholesterol levels aren't budging for the better? You may have your genes to blame, a new study finds. Researchers at Stanford University found certain genes influenced, for good or ill, how women's bodies dealt with cholesterol. It's an issue plaguing millions of health-conscious Americans. "We have people who come into clinic and say, 'I changed my diet and it made no difference in my cholesterol.' Or they say, 'I changed my diet and it had a huge effect on my cholesterol,'" noted senior author Dr. Shoa Clarke, a cardiologist and assistant professor of medicine and pediatrics at Stanford Medicine. His team's findings are not meant to deter folks from fighting cholesterol, however. "Sometimes people think, 'Oh, if I'm genetically prone to something, then it doesn't really matter what my diet is or what the environment is like; it's genetically predetermined.' But what we're saying here is the opposite," Clarke said in a news release. "The people who had this genetic predisposition for higher LDL ['bad' cholesterol] were actually the ones with the most sensitivity to diet, where diet has the best chance of helping them—or hurting them, if the diet is not good." The findings were published Nov. 18 in the journal JACC: Advances. The new study looked at data collected for decades among more than 21,000 American women, as part of the ongoing Women's Health Initiative. At the study's beginning in 1991, the women's median age was 65, meaning half were younger and half were older. About half were White, a third were Black and 15% were Hispanic. All checked in regularly with detailed reports on their daily diet, and all of the women got regular cholesterol checks. Most importantly for this study, the women also underwent complex genetic analysis. The Stanford team found that certain genes seem to predispose women to higher levels of cholesterol, and that was especially true for women who consumed high levels of unhealthy saturated fats. For women lacking these genes, no such association was found. Further research showed that women who ranked in the top 25% in terms of a genetic propensity for high LDL cholesterol and saturated fat intake were also much more prone to suffer a stroke or heart attack. The other 75% (with a less pronounced genetic predisposition) did not seem as affected by saturated fat intake, the team noted. "We found the same thing—that the association between saturated fat intake and risk for having cardiovascular events was amplified for those people at the upper end of the genetic spectrum," Clarke said. "This variation in response to saturated fat may be one of the reasons there is so much controversy and disagreement over how much is OK in a diet." The study is in line with guidelines from the World Health Organization urging people to limit their intake of red meat, because it contains high levels of saturated fat. Clarke's team found that—despite what fans of high-fat ketogenic diets might claim—higher saturated fat intake offered no protective effect for the heart, even among those at relatively low genetic risk for higher LDL cholesterol. "The effects of saturated fat may not be the same for everyone, but there isn't any evidence of a cardiovascular benefit to eating more saturated fat," Clarke said. "The harms might vary from small to large depending on who you are." In prior research, the Stanford group created a "polygenic risk score" assessing an individual's relative odds for poor heart health based on their genetics. "We don't yet have evidence that polygenic risk scores should be used to guide diet recommendations," Clarke said. "But that certainly is a future area of study, and from what our results in this new study suggest, could indeed be a fruitful one." More information Alexa Barad et al., Association Between Saturated Fat Intake and Low-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol Across the Genetic Spectrum, JACC: Advances (2026).
