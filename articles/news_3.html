深度解析

本次新闻报道聚焦于南非研究人员探索治疗复发或难治性淋巴瘤的新方法，即使用通用型（“off-the-shelf”）基因工程自然杀伤（NK）细胞。这与目前主流的自体嵌合抗原受体T细胞（CAR-T）疗法形成鲜明对比，后者需要从患者自身收集T细胞进行基因改造和扩增，过程复杂且成本高昂。 文章中的核心专业概念在于通用型CAR-NK细胞疗法：这些NK细胞并非来自患者自身，而是从共享的实验室细胞系中培养而来，并经过基因工程改造表达CAR，以特异性识别B细胞淋巴瘤表面的CD19抗原。NK细胞本身具有天然的抗肿瘤活性，其在人体内的存活时间相对较短，通常认为相比CAR-T细胞，其引发细胞因子释放综合征（CRS）和免疫效应细胞相关神经毒性综合征（ICANS）等严重副作用的风险较低，且发生移植物抗宿主病（GVHD）的几率也大大降低。 这种“通用型”策略在行业内具有深远影响。首先，它简化了制备流程，避免了每位患者复杂的个体化生产，有望大幅缩短治疗等待时间，降低生产成本，提升治疗可及性。这对于医疗资源相对有限的地区，如南非，尤其重要。其次，NK细胞作为细胞治疗平台，拓宽了肿瘤免疫治疗的路径，为那些不适合或对CAR-T疗法无反应的患者提供了新的选择。尽管目前仍处于1期临床研究阶段，主要评估安全性和耐受性，且初期疗效存在差异，但其潜在的物流优势、成本效益和安全性，预示着细胞治疗未来可能向标准化、规模化生产迈进，从而实现更广泛的临床应用。然而，这种疗法的长期有效性和最佳适用人群仍需通过后续更大规模的临床试验来进一步确立。

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: Credit: Unsplash/CC0 Public Domain South African researchers are testing "off-the-shelf" immune cells against lymphoma that has returned or resisted treatment, investigating an approach that avoids having to manufacture a separate treatment from each patient's own cells. The trial uses genetically engineered natural killer (NK) cells grown from a shared laboratory cell line. Researchers are assessing their safety and looking for evidence that they can help patients with certain B-cell lymphomas. Dr. Jackie Thomson, a clinical hematologist and stem cell transplant specialist at Wits Donald Gordon Medical Center (WDGMC), combines this research with teaching doctors to deliver complex blood cancer care. The Donald Gordon Medical Institute (DGMI) is the center's research and training arm. Thomson has seen treatment options expand considerably over her career. "We have so many options for these patients. It really doesn't feel like a death sentence anymore," she says. The study involving her team, QUILT-106, focuses on selected B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphomas. Its engineered cells carry a chimeric antigen receptor, or CAR, designed to recognize CD19, a protein found on certain B cells. Researchers are testing the cells alone and with rituximab, an antibody treatment targeting another protein, CD20. As a phase 1 study, it focuses on safety and tolerability while looking for early evidence of a response. Thomson describes varied outcomes: "Some patients didn't respond, and other patients responded incredibly well and they went into remission very quickly." Further research and follow-up are needed to establish which patients might benefit and whether responses last. How the cells are produced is a central distinction. Conventional autologous CAR-T therapy uses a patient's own T cells, which must be collected, genetically modified and expanded before being returned to that person. Each patient requires an individual manufacturing process. The NK-cell product used in QUILT-106 comes from an established cell line and is manufactured abroad and imported for the South African study. This avoids having to collect and engineer each patient's own immune cells. Thomson sees the potential to simplify logistics and reduce costs. "I think in the long run they will be cheaper," she says. The trial has not established those savings; affordability will depend on the eventual price and cost of delivering treatment. The need for blood cancer care is substantial. South Africa's National Cancer Registry recorded 2,342 new cases in its non-Hodgkin lymphoma category and 1,428 cases of leukemia in 2024. These are recorded diagnoses, rather than everyone living with these diseases. Delivering advanced treatments requires services that can assess patients, handle cell products correctly and monitor recovery. Thomson established the transplant unit in 2019. In 2025, the Wits Donald Gordon Cellular and Immunotherapy Center achieved JACIE accreditation, recognizing compliance with international standards. JACIE (an international certification body for stem cell transplantation and cellular therapy) assesses clinical and laboratory practice and the quality systems supporting them. "You need infrastructure to improve the results," Thomson says. Residents rotate through the unit, and at the time of the interview the team was training a fellow, an already qualified specialist developing further expertise. Supervised ward rounds and weekly academic meetings allow doctors to work through treatment decisions with experienced clinicians. "That's why it's so important for us to have the residents rotating through the unit now," she says. Trainees learn to interpret bone marrow findings, select donors and plan transplants. They must understand whether enough stem cells have been collected, whether those cells are viable and how they are preserved. The teaching extends through recovery, when complications must be recognized and managed. The work connects what happens in the laboratory with what happens at the bedside, including monitoring blood counts and immune recovery in the weeks after a transplant. A transplant can restore blood-cell production after intensive treatment. Donor immune cells may also attack remaining cancer, but can attack healthy tissue too, causing graft-versus-host disease. Specialists must weigh these risks and benefits. Thomson is clear that newer cell therapies have not removed the need for transplantation. Research requires careful records and sustained follow-up to establish whether responses last and complications emerge. Thomson describes the demands of meeting research standards alongside clinical care and accreditation. Nationally, a 2025 South African Medical Journal paper from the BloodSA Cell and Gene Therapy working party identified training in transplantation and cell and gene therapies as a priority, alongside specialized facilities and funding for personnel. Thomson also worries about what happens after training. She describes doctors struggling to find funded posts despite the need for their expertise. Expanding access requires places to use those skills, supported by diagnostic services and timely referrals. She would like more time to concentrate on the science itself. "I've got a lot of dreams of just spending time with the natural killer cell," she says. For now, her days also involve teaching doctors and maintaining the clinical and laboratory systems that allow that research to happen. Clinical categories Oncology
