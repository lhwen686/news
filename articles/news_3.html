一身两制：整合口腔与全身医疗，显著改善牙科随访

One body, two systems: Integrating dental and medical care dramatically improves dental follow-up

深度解析

这项研究揭示了医疗与牙科整合在提升儿科口腔健康方面的显著效能。其核心在于通过“整合医疗模式”，将传统上分离的护理服务在同一地点和共享电子健康记录系统下提供，从而显著降低了患者（特别是存在社会经济差异的群体）就诊的“结构性障碍”。这种模式使牙科复诊率提升了30%-60%，促使更频繁的“口腔预防保健”，最终改善了“口腔健康相关生活质量”和整体口腔健康“患者结局”。 从专业角度看，该研究证实了“连续性护理”的价值，并将口腔健康确立为“整体健康”的关键组成部分。对医疗行业而言，尽管整合面临统一电子健康记录系统等挑战，但其对减少健康不平等、优化资源配置及提升“公共卫生”效益具有深远意义。它呼吁医疗系统转向更全面的护理交付模式，以期实现疾病的早期预防和更优的长期健康管理。

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: Jeri Bullock, D.D.S., M.S., works with colleagues at the South Main Clinic. Credit: Charlie Ehlert / University of Utah Health In the United States, medical and dental care are traditionally separate. Care is provided in separate locations, recorded in separate systems and paid for via separate methods. But a new study has found that bringing the two systems together dramatically improves kids' odds of dental follow-up, creating new opportunities to prevent oral disease. Integrating dental care with medical care resulted in 30%–60% greater odds of continuing dental care, and patients at an integrated clinic had significantly better dental health. "We asked: Are kids more likely to return for dental care in this integrated environment?" says Jeri Bullock, D.D.S., M.S., associate dean for clinical affairs at the University of Utah School of Dentistry and first author on the study. "We found that yes, they were. Definitely." The results are published in the Journal of Dental Research. Care under one roof increases dental checkups and improves health Researchers compared dental care follow-up between two dental clinics in Salt Lake City: Liberty Elementary Clinic, where medical and dental care are provided by two different organizations with separate health records, and South Main Clinic, where medical and dental care are fully integrated. By tracking over 1,600 pediatric visits to the two clinics, the team found that kids who visited the fully integrated South Main Clinic were 30%–60% more likely to return for a dental appointment within two years. Importantly, kids who got dental care at South Main tended to have healthier teeth despite demographic differences—such as a trend toward lower socioeconomic status—that often correlate with worse health outcomes. This suggests that an integrated system may lower structural barriers to dental care, helping all children get the care they need. Researchers suspect that the better oral health of South Main patients is driven by their more frequent dentist appointments. "The oral health-related quality of life is much higher for patients visiting that site," says Brenda Heaton, Ph.D., associate dean for research at the University of Utah School of Dentistry and senior author on the study. "They're getting the same kind of care at both sites, but because of the model of care delivery, they have better outcomes." For patients and families used to separate dental care, full medical-dental integration is a big change. "It's a very unique experience for a patient to be able to get both their medical care and their dental care under the same roof," Heaton says. "Imagine it's a primary care visit for pediatrics, and the kid shares with the physician that they have pain in their mouth. When they check out, the front desk can schedule a dental appointment for them right there at the clinic." Medical and dental health records are also shared on the same electronic system. And if needed, a dentist can walk right down the hall to do a quick consultation during a general checkup. Dentists and dental researchers have advocated for increased integration into health care systems for years, but the rigorous statistics of the new study make its results especially compelling, the team says. Researchers used multiple parallel methods of data analysis to facilitate direct comparisons between demographically similar patients, allowing them to isolate the effect of medical-dental integration. "It allowed us to really compare apples to apples," Bullock says. Seeing results in the real world Bullock adds that the results bear out what she's seen in her own clinical practice at South Main. "I see this day to day," Bullock says. "I know this works; I'm seeing these kids back. I just felt that there was something special. But to actually see it come out in the numbers was really gratifying. I was really excited and proud and felt good as a clinician that what I felt and what I was seeing was true to what the data was saying." The team warns that the barriers to full medical-dental integration are significant. Bullock notes that effective integration needs a unified electronic health record system, so that dentists and pediatricians can quickly understand a patient's medical needs and provide seamless care. Transitioning to such a system represents a major hurdle for most medical systems. Still, the researchers are hopeful that their results might help drive greater integration and better patient care, both at University of Utah clinics and more broadly. "For more than a century, we've treated one body through two disconnected systems," Heaton says. "When medical and dental care work together, we reduce barriers, simplify care for families and create more opportunities to prevent disease before it becomes a larger health problem. My hope is that studies like this help move health care toward models that recognize oral health as an integral part of overall health." Publication details J.L. Bullock et al., Integrated Care Improves Continuity of Oral Health Care in Pediatrics, Journal of Dental Research (2026).
