街道外观、安全感与睡眠之间的关系

The relationship between the look of streets, feelings of safety, and sleep

深度解析

该研究通过创新性地将人工智能与城市规划及公共卫生领域相结合，揭示了城市街景特征对居民睡眠健康的深远影响。其核心专业概念在于，研究团队利用先进的机器学习技术，对海量的谷歌街景图像进行像素级分类和识别，将无结构化的视觉数据转换为可量化的城市环境特征。这一技术原理的突破性在于，它实现了对大范围地理区域环境要素的客观、精细化评估，显著提高了研究的效率和成本效益，超越了传统实地考察或主观问卷调查的局限性。 在行业影响方面，这项研究为城市规划和公共卫生政策提供了重要的循证依据。它强调了被动式环境设计在促进居民健康中的作用。研究结果表明，绿色植被丰富和具有高围合度的街道环境能增强居民安全感并延长睡眠时间，而过高的适步性可能因人流密集而降低安全感，影响睡眠。这提示城市规划者在设计社区时，应更深入地考虑感官体验和心理舒适度，而非仅仅功能性。未来，AI驱动的环境分析有望成为城市健康规划的标准工具，助力构建更宜居、更有利于居民身心健康的城市空间，从而在宏观层面改善公众睡眠质量，降低相关健康风险。

The AI identifies every pixel in an image as a specific object, such as buildings, trees, roads, sidewalks, vehicles, or people, allowing the researchers to automatically quantify the visual characteristics of neighborhoods. These measurements were then used to examine how different street environments relate to sleep health. Credit: Osaka Metropolitan University and Map Data Google Your neighborhood's "streetscape"—the physical environment of your street—is something you have probably never consciously thought about, despite seeing it every day. But what if the streetscape was affecting you on a subconscious level and disrupting your sleep? This question is posed by the findings of a study led by Professor Daisuke Matsushita of the Graduate School of Human Life and Ecology at Osaka Metropolitan University. The research team used AI to analyze more than 200,000 Google Street View images to identify visual neighborhood characteristics. They then linked these characteristics to the self-reported sleep of 1,089 working adults living on the lower floors of apartment buildings in Tokyo, who were most likely to be affected by the streetscape. The findings were published in Building and Environment. Greener, enclosed streets linked to sleep They found that those living in neighborhoods rated as having a stronger sense of safety tended to sleep longer and that this rating appeared to be shaped by the streetscape. Generally, people slept longer in areas with lots of greenery, such as leafy trees, along the street. Similarly, high "enclosure"—meaning many tall vertical buildings and few wide-open spaces—was associated with longer sleep and fewer insomnia symptoms. Walkability carried an unexpected downside However, the study also made a surprising finding. The researchers found that highly walkable streetscapes, such as those with more sidewalks and traffic signs, were associated with a reduced sense of safety and shorter sleep duration. This suggests that walkability does not always represent a reassuring environment. Instead, a possible explanation is that streets with extensive pedestrian infrastructure are often busier, more crowded and used by more strangers, which may be perceived as less safe or less relaxing than quieter residential streets. A tool for healthier neighborhood design "This study demonstrates the potential to evaluate streetscape characteristics across large geographic areas in a cost-effective manner," Dr. Matsushita said. "Based on the technique used in this study, cities could potentially measure perceived safety, beauty, liveliness and enclosure, as well as pollution and noise." "We hope that opening up this new perspective creates further possibilities for designing healthier neighborhoods," he concluded. Publication details Xiaorui Wang et al, Urban eye-level landscapes and sleep health: Cross-sectional evidence from a megacity using machine learning for objective and perceptual assessment, Building and Environment (2026).
