本文深度剖析了美国初级保健医生短缺的医疗系统问题，并提出从本科医学教育（UME）阶段进行干预的解决方案，核心在于重塑“初级保健人才输送渠道”。当前，医学教育的临床通用准则在研究生医学教育（GME）中强制要求门诊培训，但在UME中缺乏对特定门诊实践的规定，这构成了影响医学生职业选择的关键“教育鸿沟”。早期且充分的门诊临床体验对医学生职业认同的塑造至关重要，因为专业选择往往在住院医师阶段前已基本固化。 文章提出的策略具有深远的行业影响。通过减轻行政负担、利用人工智能辅助及优化团队学习，可提升初级保健岗位的吸引力。设立教育相对价值单位（RVUs）等激励机制以吸引和留住优秀的初级保健教学师资至关重要。最重要的是，医学院和认证机构强制实施最低门诊轮转要求，将直接影响医学课程设置，促使更多学生认识并选择初级保健领域。这将有助于构建一个更健全的初级保健体系，改善患者的医疗服务可及性、促进疾病预防与慢性病管理，最终提升国家整体医疗健康水平。

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: A new paper led by a faculty member at the Hackensack Meridian School of Medicine (HMSOM) points to why there is a shortage of primary-care doctors—and outlines strategies to boost recruitment, starting in undergraduate medical education. Credit: Hackensack Meridian Health A primary care physician is hard to find. The thousands needed nationwide are even tougher to discover. A new paper led by a faculty member at the Hackensack Meridian School of Medicine (HMSOM) points to why—and outlines strategies to boost recruitment, starting in undergraduate medical education. The perspective piece in the Journal of General Internal Medicine identifies what the authors call a critical gap in "med ed": While graduate medical education mandates ambulatory training, neither the Liaison Committee on Medical Education (LCME) nor the Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC) requires specific outpatient exposure during medical school. This is a missed opportunity, according to the experts. The paper was authored by medical education experts on behalf of the Society of General Internal Medicine (SGIM) and Association of Chiefs and Leaders of General Internal Medicine (ACLGIM) Hess Initiative Work Group. The writers were led by Tovah Tripp, MD, associate professor of internal medicine at HMSOM, the school's internal medicine clerkship director and a practicing primary care doctor at Hackensack Meridian Health. "Investing in (undergraduate medical education) experiences is essential to rebuild the primary care pipeline and create a generation of physicians who understand the value and role of primary care within the health care system regardless of their ultimate specialty," the authors write. The paper synthesizes existing literature into interrelated pathways to boost primary care's importance to doctors-in-training. First, stimulating the "ecosystem" of primary care through mitigating administrative burdens and debt, while leveraging artificial intelligence and team learning models, will boost the value of the clinical role to learners. Faculty can also be developed through incentives, including educational relative value units (RVUs) or tax credits, as well as protected time and flexible schedules, among other givebacks. Finally, enriching students' experiences of primary care during clerkships and mentorships would heighten the value of primary care, they write. Specialty choices are often solidified long before residency, making early interventions critical, the authors argue. Medical schools and accrediting bodies should mandate minimum ambulatory rotation requirements to ensure primary care choices when the time comes, the authors add. "There is not a single 'fix' for this kind of systemic problem, but well-thought-out strategies can nudge the trend in a positive direction," said Tripp. Publication details Tovah Tripp et al, Improving the Ambulatory General Internal Medicine Experience in Undergraduate Medical Education: a Call for Action, Journal of General Internal Medicine (2026).
