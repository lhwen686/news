温暖夜晚与新生儿重症监护室中患病早产儿相关联

Warm nights linked to ill premature babies in NICUs

深度解析

本研究深入探讨了新生儿重症监护室（NICU）中早产儿肺炎克雷伯菌感染的流行病学特征与环境关联。肺炎克雷伯菌作为一种常见的机会性病原体，对极低出生体重儿构成严重威胁，可引发败血症和坏死性小肠结肠炎等致命性感染。文章的核心专业概念在于其结合了前瞻性基因组监测与机器学习技术。通过对拭子样本进行基因组测序和遗传簇分类，研究人员能够精准追踪病原体的传播路径，及时识别潜在的暴发，这相较于传统微生物培养方法具有更高的分辨率和时效性。最引人注目的发现是，机器学习揭示了气候条件（如温暖的夜晚和湿度）与细菌簇流行率之间的显著关联，提示环境因素可能通过影响病房表面的细菌负荷来促进传播。这一洞见对感染控制领域具有深远影响：它提示医疗机构可依据气候数据预测高风险期，进而实施有针对性的干预措施，如加强消毒、提高筛查频率或进行员工再培训，从而实现更精准、更具前瞻性的感染预防策略，有效降低NICU脆弱患儿的感染风险。

DOI: 10.1186/s13756-026-01753-4 Premature babies in the intensive care unit are particularly at risk of becoming infected with potentially pathogenic bacteria. A team of doctors in Hanover has now investigated which external factors play a role in this. They concluded that climatic conditions have an influence. The study was led and designed by Dr Leonard Knegendorf, a clinician-scientist at TWINCORE, the Centre for Experimental and Clinical Infection Research. The findings were published in the journal Antimicrobial Resistance & Infection Control. The bacterium Klebsiella pneumoniae is part of the normal microbial flora of babies from birth. Under certain circumstances, however, it can also cause disease. Premature babies, in particular, are at high risk of severe intestinal inflammation and bloodstream infections. For this reason, young patients in the neonatal intensive care unit are regularly tested for the bacterium. "When Klebsiella was detected in these swabs, we sequenced it and classified it into genetic clusters," says Dr. Leonard Knegendorf. "This enables us to identify whether any feared outbreaks are occurring on the ward." Knegendorf is a specialist in microbiology at Hannover Medical School (MHH) and, since May 2024, has also led the junior clinical research group "Data-Driven Clinical Microbiology" at TWINCORE. Over 30 months, the team tested all 936 patients in the neonatal intensive care unit weekly for Klebsiella pneumoniae, regardless of whether an outbreak was suspected. Around 8.7 percent of the children carried the bacteria; in more than two-thirds of these cases, the bacteria were detected only during treatment. So-called "very low birth weight" infants—that is, premature babies weighing less than 1,500 grams (3.3 pounds) at birth—were found to be particularly at risk. They had more than three times the risk of acquiring Klebsiella in the ward. However, only three of the affected children fell ill; all had additional serious risk factors, such as a weakened immune system or having undergone surgery shortly beforehand. One finding was particularly surprising and was uncovered only through machine learning. The frequency with which Klebsiella clusters occurred on the ward fluctuated over the year, and this pattern could be explained by weather data. "We found that weeks with particularly warm nights in the preceding four weeks were associated with a higher frequency of clusters," Knegendorf says. Humidity also played a role. A statistical model based on climate data and information on the patient population was able to explain up to 80 percent of the fluctuations in cluster frequency. "We suspect that outdoor temperatures influence the bacterial load in the ward environment, for example via surfaces, drains or other damp areas," Knegendorf says. "However, these are still hypotheses that we need to test in further studies." The findings show that Klebsiella bacteria are continuously transmitted in intensive care units even outside classic outbreaks and that climatic conditions may play an underestimated role. During warmer periods, more targeted measures, such as increased disinfection, more frequent screening or staff training, could help better protect children who are particularly at risk. As a next step, the team plans to speed up genomic analysis. Until now, it has taken an average of just over three weeks for sequencing results to become available. With the help of Oxford Nanopore technology, this time is set to be significantly reduced, enabling transmission chains to be identified and interrupted more quickly in the future. More information Carolin Böhne et al, Prospective genomic and epidemiologic surveillance of Klebsiella pneumoniae in a tertiary NICU, Antimicrobial Resistance & Infection Control (2026).
