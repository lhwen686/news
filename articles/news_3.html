LSD microdosing shows potential sustained attention gains in women, but none in men

深度解析

本研究深入探讨了迷幻药微剂量，特别是LSD，对人类认知功能，尤其是持续注意力的性别差异影响。从神经药理学角度看，LSD主要通过作用于大脑中的5-HT2A血清素受体来发挥其精神活性作用。血清素系统在调节情绪、认知和感知方面扮演关键角色。尽管微剂量LSD不引发完整迷幻体验，但其可能通过对前额叶皮层等关键脑区血清素受体的微调，影响神经递质平衡和神经回路活动，从而改善或改变认知表现。文章中观察到女性持续注意力显著改善而男性无此效果，提示可能存在性别特异性的生物学机制差异，例如雌激素等性激素对血清素系统功能和LSD药代动力学/药效学的潜在调节作用。 这项研究对精神病学和药物开发领域具有重要影响。它强调了在未来迷幻药辅助治疗的临床试验中，将生物性别作为一个关键变量纳入考量的重要性。理解这些性别差异有助于推动个性化医疗的发展，未来可能针对不同性别的患者制定更精准的剂量方案或治疗策略，从而提高治疗效果、降低潜在副作用。这一发现不仅为迷幻药作为新型精神药物的研发提供了新的方向，也凸显了在神经科学研究中整合性别维度的必要性，以实现更全面、更有效的治疗方案。

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: Credit: Unsplash/CC0 Public Domain A pilot study suggests that low doses of LSD may have different effects on sustained attention in women and men. The findings, presented at the ECNP annual congress in Munich, are preliminary, but they add to growing evidence that people may not all respond to psychedelic microdosing in the same way. Microdosing means taking a very small amount of a psychedelic drug, below the level usually associated with a full psychedelic experience. Some people report using low-dose LSD in the hope of improving focus, concentration, productivity or mood, and there are also anecdotal reports of people using it to manage problems with attention. However, controlled scientific studies have produced mixed results, making it important to test these claims carefully. Testing attention across four low doses A group of researchers from the University of Maastricht tested the responses of volunteers to microdosing. The randomized controlled study involved 48 healthy volunteers, including 24 women, with an average age of 36.9 years. Participants were randomly assigned to receive either placebo or four low doses of LSD, each containing 15 micrograms, over a two-week period (typical psychedelic effects tend to appear with doses greater than 60 mcg). Neither the participants nor the researchers knew who had received LSD and who had received placebo until after the study was completed. Researchers looked at three areas of mental performance: sustained attention, information processing and working memory. Participants completed tests at the start of the study, after the first dose, after the fourth dose and at follow-up. The most notable result was seen in a test of sustained attention, where people had to watch a screen and respond as quickly as possible when a signal appeared. Attention gains seen in women, not men Women given the placebo in the study tended to have slower reaction times and more attention lapses than men given the same task. Under low-dose LSD, however, women responded about 8% faster to the stimulus than in the placebo condition (335 ms with placebo, compared with 308 ms under LSD) and had an average of 2.3 sustained attention lapses, compared with the 4.7 lapses among women who received the placebo. For attentional lapses, this pattern was seen after the first dose, after repeated dosing and at follow-up. No comparable improvement was observed in men given the microdoses. Lead researcher Iva Totomanova (of the University of Maastricht) said, "We examined the effect of LSD on three different cognitive tasks. The key finding was that LSD improves how women can sustain attention in certain tasks. With the information processing and working memory cognitive tasks, the results were not as clear-cut, which may have led to sex-related effects being missed in previous studies. "It's important to note that while our work suggests that microdosed LSD improves sustained attention in women, it does not show that low-dose LSD generally improves cognition, nor that women are generally more responsive to LSD than men. "Instead, the results point to a more cautious message: effects may depend on the person, the cognitive task being measured and the circumstances of use. A better question may be: Who responds to microdosing, and under what circumstances?" Sex differences need targeted follow-up The work is a reanalysis of an earlier completed study, so the authors say the results need to be confirmed in research designed specifically to examine sex differences (for example, by looking at hormone concentrations in the blood and taking into account menstrual phase, contraceptive use). Totomanova added, "This is a small pilot study, so it does need to be confirmed in a larger population. If we can do that, it may help us understand why some people respond to LSD in a particular way, when others don't. We are beginning to see that people respond to psychedelics in different ways. With psychedelic pharmaceuticals, it looks like personalized medicine is important." Commenting, Dr. Mikkel Højlund (of the University of Southern Denmark) said, "Microdosing of psychedelics has received much attention in the media, so testing this in a proper research context is important to advance our knowledge on the subject. Even though it is a double-blind, placebo-controlled study, the researchers are cautious: as they say, this is a small study, with several variables, but it merits further work on a bigger scale." This is an independent comment; Højlund was not involved in this work.
