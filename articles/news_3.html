Overuse of inhalers found to increase heart attack risk in asthma and COPD patients

深度解析

这项研究揭示了哮喘和慢阻肺患者过度使用缓解吸入器（特别是短效β2受体激动剂SABA和短效M受体拮抗剂SAMA）所带来的严重心血管风险和死亡率增加。其核心机制在于：首先，缓解吸入器的过度使用本身就是疾病控制不佳的标志，表明潜在气道炎症未得到有效控制，而慢性气道炎症已被证实是心血管疾病的独立危险因素。其次，SABA和SAMA作为短效支气管扩张剂，虽然能迅速缓解症状，但其药理作用可能导致心率加快和心律不齐等全身性副作用，直接增加了心脏负担。长期过度暴露于这些药物的全身效应，无疑会加重心血管系统的压力，尤其是在已有或潜在心血管风险的患者中。 这一发现对行业产生了深远影响。它警示临床医生和患者，缓解吸入器的使用应被视为疾病管理策略中的辅助手段，而非主要治疗。过度使用是需要立即进行医学干预的“警示信号”，促使医疗专业人员重新评估患者的维持治疗方案、检查用药依从性和吸入技术，并考虑整合心血管风险评估。对于制药行业而言，这可能推动更安全、更有效的长效维持疗法的研发，以减少对缓解药物的依赖。同时，加强患者教育，使其理解预防性治疗的重要性及过度使用缓解吸入器的潜在危害，是改善患者预后、降低并发症的关键。

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: Credit: Cnordic Nordic from Pexels Overuse of reliever inhalers is associated with higher risks of developing and dying from cardiovascular disease, including heart failure, heart attack and ischemic stroke, in patients with asthma and COPD, according to a study published today in ERJ Open Research. These "reliever" inhalers can help people breathe more easily when their symptoms are particularly bad. However, it is possible to overuse them if a patient's asthma or COPD is not being managed well by "preventer" treatments. Professor Lies Lahousse, a lead author of the study from Ghent University in Belgium and Erasmus MC Rotterdam in the Netherlands, explains, "Short-acting bronchodilator overuse is common. We know that almost one in five asthma patients and one in three COPD patients are overusing these inhalers. In both asthma and COPD, we found that increased use of these inhalers was associated with higher risks of cardiovascular disease and death, and the risk continued to rise the more inhalers patients used per year. "Overuse of short-acting bronchodilators is usually the result of poor disease control. Both COPD and uncontrolled asthma are linked to an increased risk of cardiovascular disease and death." Higher risks across both diseases The study assessed real-world data from primary and secondary care for more than 280,000 COPD and asthma patients in Belgium who used either SABA (short-acting beta-agonist), SAMA (short-acting muscarinic antagonist), both or no short-acting bronchodilators to manage their condition. Researchers took into account differences in age, sex, socioeconomic status, smoking history, nursing home residency, disease severity and other conditions or medications. Both SABA and SAMA bronchodilators are often used as reliever therapies by asthma and COPD patients. SABA bronchodilators increased the risk of death by 12% and 14% in asthma and COPD patients, respectively, compared with no use. The risk of death from SAMA bronchodilators increased by 73% and 36%, respectively. The researchers also assessed how the number of inhalers used per year affected cardiovascular health. They found that using three or more inhalers per year for either bronchodilator was associated with an increased risk of death compared with two or fewer inhalers per year. Patients who did not have any prior history of cardiovascular disease were found to be more vulnerable to cardiovascular problems resulting from inhaler overuse. Overuse as a warning sign Lahousse added, "Overuse of short-acting bronchodilators is an alarm signal that indicates uncontrolled airway inflammation and requires a medical review of treatment. It should prompt patients to have their lungs checked and health care providers to check adherence and inhaler technique against treatment guidelines. "Patients should be informed that short-acting bronchodilators only relax airway muscles briefly. Uncontrolled asthma and COPD are already linked to an increased risk of cardiovascular disease and death. When this is combined with overuse of SABA and SAMA bronchodilators—which both cause faster, irregular heart rates as a side effect—these risks become even more pronounced. Overreliance on this short relief may allow the underlying disease activity to progress unchecked. Our research should draw clinicians' attention to the fact that overuse is common and may be associated with adverse effects, even in patients without cardiac history." Calls for closer monitoring The team is now planning to investigate whether reliever overuse can be avoided by stricter adherence to maintenance treatment and whether cardiovascular risk assessment or specific cardiovascular therapies might decrease the risks for patients. They also hope to investigate whether short-acting bronchodilator overuse in primary care will trigger health care providers to check patients' lungs and monitor disease management more closely. Dr. Pavol Pobeha from the European Respiratory Society's expert group on monitoring airway diseases, based at Pavol Jozef Šafárik University in Košice, Slovakia, who was not involved in the research, said, "There are no cures for asthma or COPD. As a result, the primary goal of clinicians and patients is to manage symptoms and prevent the conditions from worsening. Relief from bronchodilators plays a key role in this management, helping patients take control of their own health and improve their quality of life, but it is important that they do not become reliant on these therapies. "We need improved monitoring systems that give a warning signal when patients are overusing short-acting bronchodilators, enabling doctors to support their patients with the best treatment and care to manage symptoms. This will help patients and their health care providers maximize the benefits of their medication while minimizing risks."
