Newly discovered pathway fuels production of cancer-driving proteins

深度解析

本文揭示了METTL13酶通过调控mRNA解码，在促进肿瘤发生和治疗抵抗中的关键作用。KRAS等癌基因会产生富含特定密码子的mRNA，指导驱动肿瘤生长的蛋白质合成。研究发现，METTL13通过对参与蛋白质合成的延伸因子eEF1A进行甲基化修饰，选择性地提高了癌细胞对这些特定密码子的解码效率，从而过度产生KRAS等促癌蛋白。这种机制赋予了癌细胞显著的生长优势，并导致了对现有KRAS抑制剂的耐药性。 这一发现具有重要的临床意义。目前的KRAS靶向药物主要在蛋白质产生后进行阻断，而METTL13通路则提供了一个更上游的干预点，即在翻译层面从源头限制KRAS等癌蛋白的合成。临床前研究已证实，抑制METTL13能有效降低KRAS水平，减缓肿瘤生长，并能恢复对KRAS抑制剂（如sotorasib）的敏感性，显著延长生存期。未来，针对METTL13的靶向策略有望克服KRAS突变型癌症的治疗瓶颈，为耐药患者提供新的治疗选择。此外，METTL13的活性水平或可作为预测生物标志物，指导患者选择最佳的KRAS靶向治疗方案，推动精准医疗的发展。

DOI: 10.1038/s41556-026-02085-6 A previously unknown mechanism helps cancer cells selectively produce proteins that drive tumor growth—such as KRAS—in common cancers, according to new research from The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center. The findings reveal that targeting a specific enzyme may improve responses to targeted therapies and help overcome treatment resistance. The study, published in Nature Cell Biology, was co-led by Pawel Mazur, Ph.D., associate professor of experimental radiation oncology, along with collaborators at other institutions. "Our findings show that, in cancer cells, the METTL13 enzyme facilitates the production of important growth-driving proteins," Mazur said. "By understanding the underlying mechanisms and removing that support, we may be able to make tumors more vulnerable to treatment and help improve patient outcomes." What did researchers discover about the mechanisms behind KRAS-driven cancers? KRAS is one of the most commonly mutated cancer genes, driving tumor growth in multiple types of cancers, including many lung and pancreatic cancers. Although KRAS-targeted therapies have improved outcomes for some patients, tumors often adapt and become resistant over time. Many cancer-driving genes, including KRAS, produce large amounts of messenger RNA rich in specific strings of genetic "words," called codons, that serve as instructions to build proteins. Structural, chemical and metabolic barriers sometimes make it harder for protein-producing machinery to read those codons. The researchers found that METTL13 adds a small chemical tag, known as methylation, to one of the proteins involved in protein production. This modification helps cancer cells read the codons more efficiently, giving them an advantage to produce larger amounts of proteins that promote tumor growth. Importantly, METTL13 did not broadly increase production of all proteins, but rather selectively enhanced production of only a subset of proteins, many of which are involved in cancer growth and survival. Can targeting METTL13 improve outcomes for KRAS-mutant cancers? Reducing or removing METTL13 lowered levels of KRAS and other cancer-driving proteins and slowed tumor growth in several preclinical cancer models. In KRAS-mutant lung cancer models, including tumors that had already developed resistance to the KRAS inhibitor sotorasib, METTL13 loss slowed tumor growth, restored treatment sensitivity and significantly extended survival. Additionally, researchers noticed that patients whose tumors had higher METTL13 activity tended to have poorer responses to KRAS inhibitors, further suggesting the pathway plays a role in treatment resistance. Why does this matter for patients? Current KRAS inhibitors work by blocking the KRAS protein after it has already been produced. These new findings suggest there may be a way to intervene earlier by targeting the METTL13 enzyme and limiting how much KRAS protein is produced by cancer cells in the first place. While these results are preclinical and require further validation before they can be translated into the clinic, researchers are working to better understand how METTL13 controls protein production and to develop strategies for safely targeting it. Future studies also will investigate whether the level of METTL13 could help predict which patients are most likely to benefit from KRAS-targeted therapy.
