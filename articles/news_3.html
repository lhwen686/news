Regular guava juice consumption may prove a readily accessible and affordable addition to helping lower the risk of anemia in women in low and middle income countries, suggests a synthesis of the available evidence, published in BMJ Nutrition Prevention & Health.

A combination of guava juice and iron supplements is more effective than the supplements alone for boosting hemoglobin levels, the pooled data indicate, prompting the researchers to suggest its inclusion in dietary counseling for anemia prevention in countries with a high prevalence of the condition.

Iron deficiency anemia is very common in teenage girls and pregnant women in low and middle income countries, putting them at heightened risk of ill health and death, note the researchers.

In many Asian regions, guava fruit is a rich, affordable source of vitamin C, which boosts uptake of dietary iron from plant sources, they add. The fruit contains up to four times as much vitamin C per 100 g as oranges, and also provides vitamin A, folate, dietary fiber, and modest amounts of iron, they point out.

While several small Indonesian studies have reported increases in hemoglobin after drinking the juice, the findings haven't been synthesized to establish its overall effectiveness, they explain.

The researchers therefore scoured research databases for relevant clinical trials and quasi-experimental studies published in English from 2000 onward.

They found 17 eligible studies, 15 of which were quasi-experimental trials, and two of which were randomized controlled trials. Six studies focused on teenage girls and 11 involved pregnant women. Most (nine) evaluated the effects of guava juice plus iron supplementation.

The results of 12 quantitative studies involving 235 women and teenage girls were synthesized. The pooled average difference in hemoglobin levels was 1.71 g/dl, overall, indicating a significant improvement after guava juice consumption.

Further detailed analysis showed average differences after drinking the juice of 1.52 g/dl among teenage girls and 1.84 g/dl among pregnant women.

In the five studies directly comparing guava juice and iron supplementation combined and iron supplementation alone (102 participants in each group), the average difference was 1.29 g/dl greater with the guava juice combination.

"An increase of 1–2 g/dl may shift individuals from mild or moderate anemia to non-anemic categories, improving fatigue, cognitive function, and productivity outcomes," suggest the researchers.

They highlight various limitations to their findings, including the fact that all the studies were carried out solely in Indonesia, and the wide variability in study design, intervention duration, guava variety, dose and participant characteristics.

The predominance of quasi-experimental designs also restricts causal inference, and the lack of long-term follow-up precludes assessment of the sustainability of any improvements in hemoglobin levels, they emphasize.

"Integrating guava juice into school nutrition programs, antenatal care packages, or community health initiatives could represent a feasible approach to address mild-to-moderate anemia, aligning with the United Nations' Decade of Action on Nutrition (2016–2025), which emphasizes dietary diversification and locally sourced nutrient-rich foods," they point out.

"Given its nutritional richness, affordability, and cultural acceptance across Asia, guava juice offers a promising low-cost intervention. Strengthening local supply chains, standardizing formulations and embedding such dietary approaches within public health nutrition programs could collectively contribute to more sustainable anemia control," they add.

"This study builds on the established role of dietary sources high in vitamin C to enhance iron absorption and improve the effectiveness of iron supplementation," comments Professor Sumantra Ray, chief scientist & executive director, NNEdPro Global Institute for Food, Nutrition and Health, which co-owns BMJ Nutrition Prevention & Health.

"But quasi-experimental research, the wide variation in study design, small sample sizes, and limited length of follow-up mean that caution is required when interpreting the findings. Without further rigorous research, defining the best therapeutic dose and period of use, guava juice can't be recommended as an alternative to conventional treatment in those at risk of iron deficiency anemia," he adds.

《英国医学杂志·营养预防与健康》发表的一项现有证据综合分析表明，定期饮用番石榴汁可能成为一种易于获取且经济实惠的补充措施，有助于降低中低收入国家女性的贫血风险。

汇总数据显示，番石榴汁与铁补充剂联合使用在提升血红蛋白水平方面比单独使用补充剂更有效，这促使研究人员建议在高发病率国家将其纳入贫血预防的膳食咨询中。

研究人员指出，在低收入和中等收入国家，缺铁性贫血在青少年女性和孕妇中非常普遍，使她们罹患疾病和死亡的风险增高。

他们补充说，在许多亚洲地区，番石榴富含维生素C且价格实惠，能够促进植物性膳食铁的吸收。他们指出，每100克番石榴所含维生素C是橙子的四倍，同时还提供维生素A、叶酸、膳食纤维以及适量的铁。

他们解释说，虽然几项小规模的印度尼西亚研究报告称饮用番石榴汁后血红蛋白有所增加，但这些发现尚未进行综合分析以确定其总体有效性。

因此，研究人员检索了2000年以来以英文发表的相关临床试验和准实验研究的数据库。

他们发现了17项符合条件的研究，其中15项是准实验性试验，2项是随机对照试验。有6项研究关注青少年女性，11项涉及孕妇。大多数（9项）评估了番石榴汁联合铁补充剂的效果。

对涉及235名女性和青少年女性的12项定量研究结果进行了综合分析。总体而言，血红蛋白水平的汇总平均差异为1.71克/分升，表明饮用番石榴汁后有显著改善。

进一步详细分析显示，饮用番石榴汁后，青少年女性的平均差异为1.52克/分升，孕妇的平均差异为1.84克/分升。

在直接比较番石榴汁联合铁补充剂与单独铁补充剂的五项研究中（每组102名参与者），番石榴汁联合组的平均差异高出1.29克/分升。

研究人员表示：“血红蛋白增加1-2克/分升可能使个体从轻度或中度贫血转变为非贫血状态，从而改善疲劳、认知功能和生产力结果。”

他们强调了研究结果的各种局限性，包括所有研究均仅在印度尼西亚进行，以及研究设计、干预持续时间、番石榴品种、剂量和参与者特征的广泛差异。

他们强调，准实验性设计的主导地位也限制了因果推断，而缺乏长期随访则无法评估血红蛋白水平改善的持续性。

他们指出：“将番石榴汁纳入学校营养计划、产前护理包或社区健康倡议中，可能代表一种解决轻中度贫血的可行方法，这与联合国营养行动十年（2016-2025）相符，该行动强调膳食多样化和本地采购的营养丰富食物。”

他们补充说：“鉴于其营养丰富、价格实惠以及在亚洲范围内的文化接受度，番石榴汁提供了一种有前景的低成本干预措施。加强当地供应链、规范配方并将此类膳食方法纳入公共卫生营养计划，可以共同促进更可持续的贫血控制。”

英国医学杂志·营养预防与健康》的共同所有者——NNEdPro全球食物、营养与健康研究所首席科学家兼执行董事Sumantra Ray教授评论道：“这项研究建立在富含维生素C的膳食来源在促进铁吸收和提高铁补充剂有效性方面的既定作用基础之上。”

他补充说：“然而，准实验性研究、研究设计差异大、样本量小以及随访时间有限等因素，意味着在解释研究结果时需要谨慎。在没有进一步严格研究来确定最佳治疗剂量和使用期限之前，不能将番石榴汁推荐为有缺铁性贫血风险者的常规治疗替代方案。”
