人类如何视觉辨别材料？

How do humans visually discriminate materials?

这项研究对多个行业具有深远影响。它为虚拟现实（VR）和增强现实（AR）环境中的材料渲染提供了更真实的模型，使其在不同视角下呈现出更符合人眼感知的真实感。对于机器人和人工智能的物体识别系统，引入人类主动探索性感知策略，将使其在复杂情境下具备更强大的适应性和识别能力，从被动图像处理转向主动信息获取。此外，基于人类感知特性的发现，也将指导未来人机界面的设计，使其更符合人类自然的认知模式和交互习惯，提升用户体验。

Researchers from the Cognitive Neurotechnology Unit, Vision and Action Laboratory, and Visual Perception and Cognition Laboratory in the Department of Computer Science and Engineering at Toyohashi University of Technology have conducted a psychophysical study using virtual reality (VR) to investigate how humans flexibly use exploratory behaviors—such as changing their viewpoint by moving their head and manipulating objects with their hands—when discriminating the material properties of objects.

The study was published in Journal of Vision

Humans identify the material properties of objects by using visual cues such as surface gloss, transparency, reflections and distortions of the background. For example, metals reflect the surrounding environment like a mirror, whereas glass transmits and refracts the background. However, depending on the surrounding environment and viewing direction, metal and glass can appear remarkably similar.

In such situations, it can be difficult to determine the material from a single frontal view. Just as a gemologist examines a gemstone from different angles, humans may obtain visual information useful for material discrimination by changing their viewpoint or manipulating the object.

To investigate this possibility, the researchers created computer-generated objects whose material appearance varied continuously from metal to glass in a VR environment and examined how participants observed and manipulated these objects while discriminating their materials.

Participants sat in a chair wearing a head-mounted display and were free to change their viewpoint by moving their head while translating and rotating virtual objects using handheld controllers. Throughout the experiment, the researchers recorded head position and orientation, object manipulations and exploration time.

In Experiment 1, participants freely manipulated a target object and continuously rated whether it appeared more metallic or more glasslike. The results showed that the more ambiguous the material was between metal and glass, the more extensively and frequently participants manipulated the object during observation.

Furthermore, individual differences in exploratory behavior were associated with a tendency to discriminate material properties more accurately.

In Experiment 2, the researchers investigated the relative importance of viewpoint changes (moving the head to change one's viewpoint) and object manipulation (moving the object by hand) for material discrimination. To this end, they created four experimental conditions in which viewpoint changes and object manipulation were independently enabled or disabled.

Participants compared one target object with two reference objects presented simultaneously and judged whether the target appeared to be metal or glass. The results showed that when multiple objects could be compared simultaneously, changing one's viewpoint by moving the head was more effective for material discrimination than manipulating the object by hand.

In Experiment 3, the reference objects were removed, and participants judged whether a single target object appeared to be metal or glass. In contrast to Experiment 2, the benefit of changing one's viewpoint by moving the head was reduced, whereas manipulating the object by hand became more effective for material discrimination.

These findings suggest that humans do not rely on a single exploration strategy but instead flexibly switch between viewpoint changes and object manipulation according to the visual information available in a given situation.

A more detailed analysis of participants' exploratory behaviors further revealed substantial individual differences in exploration strategies, such as bringing the object closer, moving closer to the object, and observing it from above or below.

These findings suggest that material perception is not merely a passive process of observing an object from a fixed viewpoint, but rather an active perceptual process in which observers move their head and hands to seek the visual information necessary for material discrimination.

The co-first author of the study, Ryu Nomachi, a second-year master's student in the Department of Computer Science and Engineering at Toyohashi University of Technology, commented, "When we look at an object, we are not simply processing the image that reaches our eyes. Whenever it is difficult to make a judgment, we naturally move our head or manipulate the object with our hands to seek the information we need.

"Our findings suggest that these everyday exploratory behaviors may play an important role in acquiring the visual information necessary for material discrimination."

Future directions

In this study, the researchers focused on the visual discrimination of metal and glass based solely on visual information. In the future, they plan to extend the research to a broader range of materials, such as fabric, stone and plastic, to better understand how humans select exploratory behaviors for different materials and acquire the visual information necessary for material perception.

In addition, although participants in the present study were not allowed to use tactile information directly for material discrimination, real-world material perception also relies on haptic cues such as texture, weight and temperature. By integrating active visual exploration with tactile information, future research is expected to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms underlying natural material perception in everyday life.

Furthermore, the concept of active perception demonstrated in this study—that humans actively seek the information they need rather than simply passively observing an object—may contribute to the development of more realistic material rendering in VR/AR, object recognition systems for robots and artificial intelligence that incorporate human-like exploratory behaviors, and human-centered interface designs based on the characteristics of human perception.

丰桥技术科学大学计算机科学与工程系认知神经技术单元、视觉与动作实验室以及视觉感知与认知实验室的研究人员，利用虚拟现实（VR）进行了一项心理物理学研究，旨在探究人类在辨别物体材料属性时，如何灵活运用探索性行为，例如通过移动头部改变视角和用手操纵物体。

该研究发表于《视觉杂志》(Journal of Vision)。

人类通过利用视觉线索来识别物体的材料属性，例如表面光泽、透明度、反射以及背景的扭曲。例如，金属像镜子一样反射周围环境，而玻璃则透射并折射背景。然而，根据周围环境和观察方向的不同，金属和玻璃可能看起来非常相似。

在这种情况下，仅凭单一的正面视角很难确定材料。就像宝石学家从不同角度检查宝石一样，人类可能会通过改变视角或操纵物体来获取有助于材料辨别的视觉信息。

为了探究这种可能性，研究人员在VR环境中创建了计算机生成的物体，其材料外观从金属到玻璃连续变化，并考察了参与者在辨别这些材料时如何观察和操纵这些物体。

参与者佩戴头戴式显示器坐在椅子上，可以自由移动头部以改变视角，同时使用手持控制器平移和旋转虚拟物体。在整个实验过程中，研究人员记录了头部位置和方向、物体操纵以及探索时间。

在实验 1 中，参与者自由操纵一个目标物体，并持续评定它看起来更像金属还是更像玻璃。结果表明，金属和玻璃之间的材料模糊性越大，参与者在观察过程中操纵物体的范围越广、频率越高。

此外，探索行为的个体差异与更准确地辨别材料属性的倾向相关联。

在实验 2 中，研究人员调查了视角改变（移动头部以改变视角）和物体操纵（用手移动物体）对于材料辨别的相对重要性。为此，他们创建了四种实验条件，其中视角改变和物体操纵被独立地启用或禁用。

参与者将一个目标物体与同时呈现的两个参照物体进行比较，并判断目标物体看起来更像金属还是玻璃。结果表明，当可以同时比较多个物体时，通过移动头部改变视角比用手操纵物体对材料辨别更有效。

在实验 3 中，参照物体被移除，参与者判断单个目标物体看起来更像金属还是玻璃。与实验 2 相反，通过移动头部改变视角的好处减小了，而用手操纵物体对材料辨别变得更有效。

这些发现表明，人类并不依赖单一的探索策略，而是根据特定情境中可用的视觉信息，灵活地在视角改变和物体操纵之间切换。

对参与者探索行为的更详细分析进一步揭示了探索策略中存在显著的个体差异，例如将物体移近、靠近物体以及从上方或下方观察物体。

这些发现表明，材料感知并非仅仅是一个从固定视角观察物体的被动过程，而是一个主动的感知过程，观察者通过移动头部和手来寻求材料辨别所需的视觉信息。

该研究的共同第一作者，丰桥技术科学大学计算机科学与工程系二年级硕士生Ryu Nomachi评论道：“当我们观察一个物体时，我们不仅仅是处理到达眼睛的图像。每当我们难以做出判断时，我们自然会移动头部或用手操纵物体，以寻求所需的信息。”

“我们的研究结果表明，这些日常探索行为可能在获取材料辨别所需的视觉信息方面发挥重要作用。”

未来方向

在这项研究中，研究人员专注于仅基于视觉信息的金属和玻璃的视觉辨别。未来，他们计划将研究扩展到更广泛的材料，例如织物、石头和塑料，以更好地理解人类如何为不同材料选择探索行为，并获取材料感知所需的视觉信息。

此外，尽管本研究中的参与者不被允许直接使用触觉信息进行材料辨别，但现实世界中的材料感知也依赖于触觉线索，例如纹理、重量和温度。通过将主动视觉探索与触觉信息相结合，未来的研究有望对日常生活中自然材料感知的潜在机制提供更全面的理解。

此外，本研究中展示的主动感知概念——即人类主动寻求所需信息而非仅仅被动观察物体——可能有助于在VR/AR中开发更真实的材料渲染、为机器人和人工智能开发融入类人探索行为的物体识别系统，以及基于人类感知特征的人机界面设计。
