一项新研究显示，你一生的性格变化幅度远超你所想。

Your personality changes more across your life than you think, according to new study

深度解析

这篇研究通过大规模纵向数据和先进的统计模型（如分层年龄-时期-世代交叉分类随机效应模型HAPC-CCREM）深入探讨了人格特质的动态性，颠覆了“人格一成不变”的传统观念。其核心概念是重新评估“大五人格特质”（外向性、宜人性、尽责性、神经质和开放性）在生命周期中的稳定性。研究明确指出，这些特质并非成年后就固定不变，而是与收入、健康等其他生活因素一样，甚至更剧烈地持续演变，尤其在尽责性和开放性方面表现显著。公众对此存在普遍误解，大幅低估了人格的实际变化。 这一发现对心理学、临床医学乃至社会科学领域具有深远影响。它挑战了人格发展的阶段理论，提示临床心理学和精神病学在评估和干预时，应更重视人格的可塑性，而非将其视为固化的诊断标签。在个人发展层面，它提供了积极的心理支持，鼓励人们相信通过努力可以实现自我转变和成长，而非受限于“我就是这样”的宿命论。对于组织行为学，这提示企业在人才选拔、团队建设和领导力培养中，应考虑员工人格特质的动态变化潜力，而非仅依据初始评估。最终，这项研究促进了对人类复杂性和适应能力的更全面理解，鼓励更灵活和以成长为导向的视角来看待自我与他人。

Credit: Pexels Personality traits have long been seen as set in stone, with many believing they stop changing once we reach adulthood. A recent study, however, challenges this long-held belief, suggesting that personality changes far more than most people realize. In the first part of the study, researchers surveyed 887 U.S. residents who rated the Big Five personality traits—extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, neuroticism and openness—as some of the most stable characteristics of a person's life. A follow-up analysis of data from eight large-scale longitudinal data sets involving 166,971 individuals across the United States, Germany, Australia and the Netherlands told a different story. It revealed that the Big Five traits changed just as much as other life factors, both year to year and over a lifetime. For instance, conscientiousness, which is the tendency to be careful, diligent and responsible, doesn't remain the same when someone is a teenager compared with when they are 90. In many cases, these changes were equal to or even greater than changes in things we think of as more flexible, like life satisfaction, income or health. People underestimated how much the Big Five personality traits actually change over time, getting it wrong 90% of the time for year-to-year change and 100% of the time for lifetime change. The full study is published in Communications Psychology. Meta-analytic change trajectories of standardized variables over the lifespan (ages 15 to 90). Credit: Communications Psychology (2026). DOI: 10.1038/s44271-026-00515-7 Testing the myth For a long time, both the general public and many psychologists believed personality traits were permanent, highly stable, largely inherited and unlikely to change much after childhood. This belief runs so deep that everyday English is full of phrases like "stay true to yourself," which assumes everyone has a fixed, unchanging inner essence. Even people actively working on self-improvement tend to frame their efforts this way. Instead of seeing change as a real shift in who they are, they describe it as uncovering their true self or simply outgrowing bad habits rather than actually becoming someone different. Decades of research have disproven this idea, yet two questions remain unanswered: How much do personality traits actually change compared with other parts of life, and do people's beliefs about that change match reality? The researchers used a two-part approach to compare what the public believes about personality change with what actually happens over a lifetime. First, they recruited 887 adults from across the United States who were given a list of 25 different characteristics, including the Big Five personality traits and 20 other variables like income, physical health, self-esteem and how often a person socializes. Participants were asked to rate how much they thought each one changed, on average, between the ages of 15 and 90. The scale went from not at all (0) to a lot (11). Relationship between lifespan net and cumulative changes from the panel data and laypeople's change ratings of the same variables. Credit: Communications Psychology (2026). DOI: 10.1038/s44271-026-00515-7 They then turned to massive amounts of existing personality data, drawing on eight large-scale longitudinal studies from four different countries that tracked the same people over many years to see how personalities actually evolve over decades. To separate real personality change from statistical noise, researchers used a mathematical model called Hierarchical Age-Period-Cohort Cross-Classified Random Effect Models (HAPC-CCREM). This let them measure change in two distinct ways: net change, which captured the overall shift in personality traits from age 15 to 90, and cumulative change, which accounted for every rise and fall along the way, even for people who ended up right back where they started. Even though people rated the Big Five personality traits low on the 11-point scale, these traits were far from set in plaster in adulthood and continued to evolve significantly throughout a person's life. Of the 32 life variables measured, extraversion and openness ranked among the top 10 for the largest overall declines across a lifetime. Conscientiousness ranked fifth in total change among all 32 variables, making it one of the most dynamic aspects of a person's life. The gap between public perception and reality was huge. People were fairly accurate when predicting changes in other parts of their lives, underestimating shifts in their own health only about 29% to 43% of the time. However, when it came to personality traits, people underestimated the total change 100% of the time. The findings push back on the familiar idea that "this is just who I am," offering a more realistic message that people can change over time, though usually gradually. The researchers, however, noted that the study focused on average change across large groups, missing how change actually plays out for individuals. Combining big-picture and individual-level approaches would give a clearer picture of perceived versus actual change over a lifetime.
