Research characterizes largely unexplored community of viruses within the human gut

英文原文

Original
The human gut microbiome is home to a vast and largely unexplored world of viruses, which could hold important clues to how the gut ecosystem works. New research, by King's College London, Helmholtz Munich and University of Nottingham, has characterized a huge and largely unexplored community of viruses, known as bacteriophages, within the human gut. The findings provide an integrated picture of the gut ecosystem, connecting its viral, bacterial and metabolic components, which influence health and conditions like IBS. Research into the human gut microbiome has traditionally focused on bacteria, but little is known about its enormous population of viruses, particularly bacteriophages – which are viruses that infect bacteria. In the study, published in Nature Communications today, scientists identified over 116,000 viral groups in the gut. More than 99% of these had no close match in the largest existing catalogue of gut viruses, highlighting how much of the viral world within the gut remains unexplored. By analysing genetic material from stool samples, researchers examined which viruses and bacteria were present in each person's gut and how their abundance varied together. They found that certain viruses were consistently more common alongside particular bacteria, while others tended to be less common. The team used data from over 1,000 TwinsUK participants, the largest UK Twin Registry with data on gut bacteria, health measurements and diet. By studying twins, the researchers also found that environmental factors play a major role in shaping the gut viral community, while genetics also contributes to certain aspects of viral diversity. Although genetic factors were estimated to explain around 47% of the variation in overall viral diversity, most individual viral populations appeared to be predominantly influenced by environmental factors. The researchers also identified a small group of viruses carrying genes linked to harmful bacterial behaviour. These viruses tended to occur alongside bacteria that can sometimes cause infections, and were less commonly found alongside bacteria involved in breaking down dietary fibre. Although this does not mean the viruses themselves cause disease, it suggests they may be associated with less favourable bacterial communities in the gut. Differences in the microbiome might influence conditions, such as irritable bowel syndrome (IBS). IBS affects around 10% of the population and can significantly affect quality of life, yet its underlying causes remain poorly understood. In a second study, published in Gut today, the research team identified 50 gut viral groups associated with IBS, most of which were less abundant in people with the condition. There were also differences in the patterns of relationships between bacteria and viruses, with many viruses infecting bacteria already linked to the condition, suggesting that IBS may involve changes in how the wider gut microbial community is organised, rather than changes in bacteria alone. This provides a new perspective on a common and poorly understood condition and could eventually contribute to research into better biomarkers and treatments. This study also used data from TwinsUK. The next challenge will be to understand whether these viruses actively influence bacterial metabolism and how their relationships with bacteria change in different states of health and disease. Future studies will investigate whether these findings can help explain individual differences in microbial function and ultimately contribute to more personalized approaches to gut health. Dr Panayiotis Louca, Research Associate at King's College London, who led the Nature study, said: "Gut viruses are difficult to study, so most of what we found simply isn't in existing catalogues. What stands out is how closely they track with gut bacteria and with the chemicals those bacteria produce. We can't yet say whether the viruses are driving that, and testing it is our next step." Senior author Dr Cristina Menni, who leads the metabolomics research group at the Department of Twin Research, Ageing and Genomic Epidemiology, King's College London, said: "Our research highlights that to fully understand the gut microbiome and its links to health and disease, we need to look beyond bacteria and consider the viruses that live alongside them. We reveal a fascinating and largely unexplored dimension of the gut microbiome – discovering that viruses are not only closely associated with bacterial communities, but also with the metabolites that connect the microbiome to our health. This opens exciting new avenues for understanding how the gut ecosystem works." The study is observational, so it cannot show that the viruses cause these effects, but it provides greater understanding of a largely unexplored area of the human gut. It would also be beneficial to replicate the study on a more diverse cohort, as participants were mostly older women of European ancestry." Dr. Panayiotis Louca, Research Associate, King's College London Professor Ana Valdes and Dr Afroditi Kouraki from the University of Nottingham said: "For a long time the viral part of the gut microbiome has been a kind of dark matter: we knew it was there but couldn't see it. Advances in sequencing and computational methods mean we can now start to map it properly, and this study shows just how much has been hidden." This research was funded by the Chronic Disease Research Foundation (CDRF) and in part by the Wellcome and Medical Research Council (MRC).
