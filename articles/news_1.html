Experimental cancer drug works differently than researchers originally thought

英文原文

Original
A cancer drug currently being tested in patients may have entered clinical trials based on an incorrect understanding of how it works, according to a new study led by the University of Sydney in collaboration with Goethe University, Oxford University and the Institute of Cancer Research, London. Published in Nature Chemical Biology, the study found the experimental drug zavondemstat and a closely related research compound, QC6352 – developed to treat cancers such as colorectal, pancreatic and prostate cancer – do not primarily target KDM4, a family of proteins which can help cancer cells grow and spread when it becomes overactive. Instead, both compounds largely work by blocking DHODH, an enzyme cancer cells rely on to produce the molecules needed for rapid growth. We can think of DHODH as a machine producing bricks needed to build new DNA. If you switch off the machine, the cell starts running out of bricks and can no longer efficiently copy its DNA and keep dividing." Lenka Munoz, Lead Author, Professor, University of Sydney School of Medical Sciences and Charles Perkins Center These findings could affect the interpretation of previous studies around the world that used the research compound (QC6352) to investigate the biology of the cancer protein and may have implications for the ongoing clinical development of the zavondemstat drug. The discovery emerged from research investigating whether zavondemstat and QC6352 could be repurposed for glioblastoma, the most common and aggressive form of brain cancer. "We tested these compounds to investigate whether they could potentially be repurposed for glioblastoma treatment," Professor Munoz said. "When we tested other KDM4 inhibitors, we found they did not reproduce the anti-cancer effects observed with QC6352. "If blocking KDM4 was driving those effects, we would have expected the other inhibitors to behave similarly. Instead, the results suggested QC6352 was acting through a different mechanism." Using patient-derived glioblastoma stem cells, tumour models and a series of genetic, mechanistic and molecular experiments, the researchers traced the compounds' response to DHODH rather than KDM4. "Researchers around the world have used QC6352 as a leading tool to study KDM4 biology, while the related drug zavondemstat progressed into clinical trials based on the same understanding," Professor Munoz said. "Our study found much of the anti-cancer activity of these compounds is driven by blocking DHODH rather than KDM4." Study highlights broader issue facing cancer drug development Professor Munoz said the findings highlight a broader challenge in cancer drug development. "Getting a drug's mechanism wrong can lead to poorly designed clinical trials, inappropriate patient selection and years of research focused on the wrong biological target," she said. "There are well-known examples of cancer drugs advancing through large clinical trials before researchers realised they were not working through the mechanism originally proposed. "Our study shows this is not just a historical problem but one still happening today. Greater rigour is needed to ensure we understand exactly how potential treatments work before they move into clinical testing. "Establishing a drug's true mechanism early can protect patients, prevent wasted research effort and help ensure what limited funding we have is directed towards genuinely promising treatments." The findings also point to DHODH inhibition as a potential new avenue for glioblastoma treatment. Several drugs targeting DHODH are already being investigated for other cancers, raising the possibility they could eventually be tested in patients with brain cancer. Beyond identifying the drug's true target, the researchers developed new compounds that inhibit KDM4 without affecting DHODH, which could help scientists study the role of KDM4 more accurately in future cancer research.

AI Translation
悉尼大学牵头，联合歌德大学、牛津大学和伦敦癌症研究所进行的一项新研究显示，一种目前正在患者身上进行测试的抗癌药物，其进入临床试验的依据可能基于对其作用机制的错误理解。 该研究发表在《自然化学生物学》期刊上，发现实验性药物zavondemstat及其密切相关的研究化合物QC6352（这些药物最初被开发用于治疗结直肠癌、胰腺癌和前列腺癌等癌症）并非主要靶向KDM4。KDM4是一种蛋白质家族，在过度活跃时可以帮助癌细胞生长和扩散。相反，这两种化合物主要通过阻断DHODH来发挥作用，DHODH是癌细胞赖以生产快速生长所需分子的关键酶。 “我们可以把DHODH看作是一台生产构建新DNA所需‘砖块’的机器。如果关闭这台机器，细胞就会缺乏‘砖块’，无法再有效地复制其DNA并持续分裂。”悉尼大学医学科学学院和查尔斯·珀金斯中心教授、首席作者Lenka Munoz表示。 这些发现可能会影响全球范围内利用研究化合物（QC6352）调查该癌症蛋白质生物学的先前研究的解释，并可能对zavondemstat药物正在进行的临床开发产生影响。 这一发现源于一项研究，该研究旨在调查zavondemstat和QC6352是否可以改作治疗胶质母细胞瘤（最常见且最具侵袭性的脑癌形式）的用途。 “我们测试了这些化合物，以调查它们是否可能被重新定位用于胶质母细胞瘤的治疗，”Munoz教授说。 “当我们测试其他KDM4抑制剂时，我们发现它们未能重现QC6352所观察到的抗癌效果。” “如果阻断KDM4是产生这些效果的原因，我们本应预期其他抑制剂也会有类似表现。然而，结果表明QC6352是通过不同的机制发挥作用的。” 研究人员利用患者来源的胶质母细胞瘤干细胞、肿瘤模型以及一系列遗传学、机制学和分子实验，追踪到这些化合物的作用靶点是DHODH而非KDM4。 “全球各地的研究人员都曾将QC6352作为研究KDM4生物学的主要工具，而相关药物zavondemstat也基于同样的理解进入了临床试验，”Munoz教授说。 “我们的研究发现，这些化合物的大部分抗癌活性是由阻断DHODH而非KDM4驱动的。” 研究凸显了癌症药物开发面临的更广泛问题 Munoz教授表示，这些发现凸显了癌症药物开发中一个更广泛的挑战。 她指出，“错误理解药物的作用机制可能导致临床试验设计不当、患者选择不当，以及数年研究都集中在错误的生物靶点上。” “癌症药物在大型临床试验中推进后，研究人员才意识到它们并非通过最初提出的机制发挥作用，这样的例子屡见不鲜。” “我们的研究表明，这不仅是一个历史问题，而是当今仍在发生的问题。我们需要更高的严谨性，以确保在潜在疗法进入临床测试之前，我们能确切了解其作用机制。” “及早确立药物的真实作用机制可以保护患者，避免浪费研究精力，并有助于确保我们有限的资金投入到真正有前景的治疗方案中。” 这些发现也指出DHODH抑制剂可能成为胶质母细胞瘤治疗的新途径。目前已有多种靶向DHODH的药物正在针对其他癌症进行研究，这使得它们最终有可能在脑癌患者中进行测试。 除了确定药物的真实靶点外，研究人员还开发了新的化合物，这些化合物可以在不影响DHODH的情况下抑制KDM4，这有助于科学家在未来的癌症研究中更准确地研究KDM4的作用。
