深度解析

萨姆·巴雷尔博士的访谈深刻阐释了以患者为中心的药物发现与转化研究的核心理念。她强调，科学进步的最终衡量标准是其对个体患者生命产生的实际影响，而非仅仅是实验室内的突破。这揭示了药物研发领域长期存在的“死亡之谷”挑战——即如何将有前景的实验室发现有效转化为临床可用的治疗方案。 文章详细阐述了解决这一挑战的专业机制，包括从疾病需求端出发，早期纳入患者视角，并全程关注从基础研究、证据生成、监管审批、生产制造到最终临床应用和可及性的全链条管理。同时，她指出了人工智能、基因组学、精准医疗、基因编辑以及免疫疗法等新兴技术协同作用的巨大潜力，这些技术不仅能揭示复杂的生物学机制，还能加速诊断与治疗的匹配，为罕见病和未满足医疗需求提供前所未有的解决方案。在行业影响方面，这种以患者为本的策略促使研发机构和企业重新定义成功，将可负担性和可及性纳入考量，并推动跨学科、跨机构的深度合作，从而降低研发风险，加速新疗法惠及患者。LifeArc等机构正是通过整合科学、资金和专业力量，在这一复杂生态系统中扮演着关键的桥梁角色。

Thought Leader Dr Sam Barrell CBE Chief Executive Office LifeArc In this interview, NewsMedical LifeSciences speaks with Dr Sam Barrell CBE, Chief Executive Officer of LifeArc, about the evolving landscape of drug discovery, the importance of patient-centred research and the challenges of translating scientific discoveries into meaningful improvements in patients' lives. Image credit: MMD Creative/Shutterstock.com Could you please introduce yourself and your role as CEO of LifeArc, and explain how your career in healthcare and medical research has shaped your perspective on drug discovery? My career has taken me from healthcare to life sciences. I trained as a doctor, then moved from frontline medicine into NHS leadership and major research organisations. That experience has shown me both the extraordinary potential of scientific discovery and how difficult it can be to turn that potential into something a patient can actually benefit from. It is why, at LifeArc, I focus on the quality of the science, as well as the whole route to impact; from understanding patient need and generating the right evidence to manufacturing, regulation, funding and adoption. Ultimately, I judge progress by whether it reaches the person who needs it. During your keynote at ELRIG Drug Discovery 2026, you spoke about how your mother, a district nurse, inspired your career in healthcare. How did her experiences influence your decision to pursue medicine, and how does that early inspiration continue to shape your work today? Healthcare was never an abstract idea in our family. Through my mum’s work, I saw the trust people place in those who care for patients in their own homes, and the difference compassion, judgement, and continuity can make. That inspired me to study medicine and gave me a deep appreciation for the realities of patients’ lives beyond a clinic or laboratory. It still shapes how I lead today. I try to begin with need, listen to patients and clinicians, and judge progress by whether it changes someone’s experience of illness. One of the most powerful messages from your keynote was that "progress is measured by the person in front of us." What does this mean to you, and how can the drug discovery community ensure that scientific progress translates into meaningful improvements in patients' lives? It means that scientific momentum must be measured in human terms. A new platform, target or dataset matters because it can give somebody an answer, more time, a better treatment or hope where none existed before. The drug discovery community can keep that connection by involving patients early, designing translation and access into programmes from the start, and bringing researchers, clinicians, regulators, funders and industry together around the same patient need. We should celebrate breakthroughs, but our real measure of success is whether they become safe, affordable and deliverable care. Discover the opportunities and challenges in developing treatments for rare diseases and unmet medical needs. Download the full interview. You shared moving examples of patients affected by conditions such as dementia and cancer to illustrate how far medical research has come. Looking back over the past 20 years, which advances in drug discovery do you believe have made the greatest difference to patients, and where are the most significant unmet needs? The clearest lesson is how one capability can open the door to another. Cancer immunotherapies such as CAR-T and checkpoint inhibitors have transformed outcomes for some patients; genomic sequencing has made precision diagnosis and treatment possible; gene therapies are beginning to treat previously untreatable conditions; and advances in technology and AI are helping us connect and use knowledge at unprecedented speed. Gardasil, the HPV vaccine, is another powerful example. By preventing infection with the types of HPV that cause most cervical cancers, it shows how extraordinary science can ultimately become part of ordinary life, with vaccination now a routine part of growing up for millions of young people. The greatest unmet needs remain where biology is complex, patient populations are small, incentives are weak or delivery systems are fragmented. Rare diseases, neurodegenerative conditions, childhood cancers and drug-resistant infections are important examples. We need to ensure advances don't remain exceptional cases, but become repeatable routes to impact. Using health data to power rare disease research Play Video credit: LifeArcOfficial/Youtube.com During your presentation, you highlighted that approximately one in 17 people lives with a rare condition, suggesting that rare diseases are collectively not so rare. Why do these conditions continue to face significant challenges in drug discovery, and what needs to change to accelerate the development of effective treatments? The challenge begins with small, geographically dispersed patient populations, which make diagnosis, natural-history studies, and conventional trials difficult. Data is fragmented, specialist capacity is limited, and the commercial model can be challenging. We need pathways designed for rare disease rather than forcing rare diseases into systems built for common conditions. That means earlier diagnosis, sustainable registries, responsible data sharing, platform and master-protocol trials, reusable evidence and manufacturing approaches, proportionate regulation, and funding that recognises both the human need and the value of learning across conditions. As CEO of LifeArc, you are focused on translating scientific discoveries into treatments that benefit patients. What are the biggest barriers preventing promising laboratory research from reaching the clinic, and how is LifeArc working to overcome them? LifeArc is itself a scientific organisation, so we’re not only translating discoveries made elsewhere. We do science ourselves and work with researchers and partners to help promising ideas progress to patients. Translation is too often treated as a handover that happens after a discovery, rather than a process designed from the beginning. Promising research can stall because the patient population or clinical pathway is poorly understood, the evidence is not aligned with regulatory needs, manufacturing is not scalable, funding falls between stages, or there is no clear route to adoption. LifeArc combines scientific and translational expertise, funding and partnership-building to help programmes cross those gaps. We work across preclinical research, clinical development and the wider ecosystem, focusing on areas of high unmet need where our independence and long-term perspective can help de-risk the pathway and bring the right partners together to deliver impact. Explore LifeArc’s Translational Research Your career has taken you from frontline medicine and NHS leadership to the Francis Crick Institute and now LifeArc. How has this combination of clinical and scientific leadership influenced your approach to connecting researchers, clinicians, industry partners, and patients to accelerate drug discovery? Those experiences have taught me to see the system from several perspectives at once: the urgency felt by patients, the practicalities of delivering care at scale and the possibilities created by ambitious science. They have also convinced me that no organisation sees everything and no single sector can translate discovery alone. My approach is to build a shared understanding of the need, involve patients and clinicians early, clarify the next evidence decision, and create partnerships in which each participant contributes what they do best. With advances in artificial intelligence, genomics, precision medicine, and other emerging technologies, which developments do you believe offer the greatest potential to address unmet medical needs? The greatest potential lies not in one technology, but in capabilities working together. Genomics can identify the biological cause of disease; AI can connect clinical, genetic and scientific evidence and reveal patterns that are otherwise hidden; precision medicine can match an intervention to the patient most likely to benefit; and emerging modalities such as gene editing, RNA medicines and cell therapies can address disease mechanisms that were previously beyond reach. But, as with any period of rapid technological change, we cannot know today exactly which of these advances will make the greatest difference to patients over the next 10 or 20 years. That makes agility important. At LifeArc, we need to respond as the science evolves, backing the approaches with the greatest potential and adapting as new opportunities emerge. The opportunity is to make successful approaches reusable and scalable, combining platforms, data and evidence so that each programme makes the next one faster, while maintaining rigorous standards of safety and patient protection. Collaboration is essential for translating research into new therapies. What does successful collaboration look like to you, and how can organisations such as LifeArc help bridge the gap between scientific innovation and patient access? Successful collaboration starts earlier than a conventional partnership agreement. It means shaping the programme together from the outset: agreeing the need, sharing relevant data responsibly, defining the evidence required and planning manufacturing, adoption and access. LifeArc can act as a bridge because we combine translational science, funding, development expertise and convening power, without being tied to a single institution or commercial model. Our role is to help align incentives, reduce duplication, preserve learning when one route closes and ensure that access is designed in rather than considered at the end. Image credit: DC Studio/Sutterstock.com Reflecting on the central message of your ELRIG Drug Discovery 2026 keynote, what would you most like researchers and industry leaders to take away from your presentation, and what advice would you give to the next generation of scientists working to improve patients' lives? The message I’d like people to take away is that scientific progress is cumulative. We learn from what came before, combine different capabilities, and use that knowledge to open up new possibilities. That means learning from what works, sharing what we know and, when something doesn’t work, understanding why and finding another route. For the next generation of scientists, my advice is to be ambitious, but always keep sight of the person you are ultimately trying to help. Ask not only whether something can be discovered, but what needs to happen for it to become a diagnosis, treatment or technology that people can actually receive. Seek out perspectives beyond your own discipline, share what you learn and remember that a failed route does not mean failed science. About Dr Sam Barrell CBE Dr Sam Barrell CBE is the Chief Executive Officer of LifeArc - a self-funded medical research organisation turning promising science into patient impact, with a focus on rare diseases and drug-resistant infections. LifeArc’s vision is a world where people living with these conditions can access the life-changing treatments they urgently need. Prior to this, she was the Deputy Chief Executive Officer of the Francis Crick Institute - a world-leading biomedical research organisation which she joined from a career in the NHS as a noted healthcare leader. Sam was CEO of the Taunton and Somerset NHS Foundation Trust and before that, established and led the South Devon and Torbay Clinical Commission Group. Sam is a Non-Executive Governor of Nuffield Health and sits on the innovation advisory board of British Land. She was awarded the CBE in 2014 for services to healthcare.
