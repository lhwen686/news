Breakthrough targeted therapy shows strong precision in treating chronic myeloid leukemia

深度解析

慢性髓性白血病（CML）的核心病理机制是BCR-ABL融合基因异常表达，产生一种持续活跃的酪氨酸激酶，驱动癌细胞增殖。酪氨酸激酶抑制剂（TKIs）通过抑制该激酶活性来治疗CML。传统TKI虽有效，但部分患者会产生耐药或不耐受。Asciminib作为一种新型TKI，其独特之处在于靶向BCR-ABL激酶的肉豆蔻酰口袋（STAMP抑制剂），而非直接竞争ATP结合位点。这种异位结合机制使其能更特异性、强效地抑制癌症驱动蛋白，即使在存在特定基因突变时也能有效作用，且具有更好的耐受性。 这项研究证实了asciminib在分子层面快速、持续抑制癌症驱动蛋白的强大能力。其行业影响深远：它为对现有TKI耐药或不耐受的CML患者提供了新的、更有效的一线治疗选择，有望显著提升患者的治疗反应率和生活质量。同时，它强力推动了CML治疗向精准医疗的迈进，强调结合基因组检测和治疗监测，根据患者的个体生物学特性定制治疗方案，而非采用“一刀切”模式。这为未来抗癌药物研发树立了新方向，即开发更具特异性、更低毒性的靶向疗法，最终实现“在正确的时间为正确的患者选择正确的治疗方案”的愿景。

英文原文

Original
A more effective and better-tolerated treatment for chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) could improve quality of life for people living with the disease. A new study led by Adelaide University and the South Australian Health and Medical Research Institute (SAHMRI) has provided the first direct evidence that a breakthrough targeted therapy can rapidly and consistently suppress the molecular signals that drive CML. The researchers compared the blocking ability of a new therapy called asciminib with commonly used tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) medications, imatinib and nilotinib. These existing medications have helped many CML patients manage their disease for the past two decades. We found asciminib was much more powerful at suppressing the cancer-driving protein that causes the leukemia cells to spread. This is a huge step forward in the search for better, frontline treatment options for patients who don't respond to current medications. Currently, 25% of patients will develop a resistance or intolerance to existing treatments." Chung Hoow Kok, Associate Professor, Adelaide University's Center for Cancer Biology and SA Pathology To test the potency of the drug, researchers developed a new way to measure how effectively the treatment switches off the cancer-driving protein inside patients' blood cells. "Within just one week, asciminib achieved substantially stronger suppression of the cancer-driving protein than the currently available treatments," said Adelaide University Professor Tim Hughes, who is the leader of SAHMRI's Chronic Myeloid Leukemia Group. "Importantly, none of the patients experienced the poorer responses sometimes seen with the existing drugs. "This new biological evidence from our research supports the excellent clinical outcomes from recent trials of asciminib." Researchers also investigated if asciminib worked in patients who had a specific genetic mutation, which is known to increase the risk of treatment failure. They found the medication was still able to block the main driver of abnormal cell growth, but noted other factors may still affect patient outcomes. There are more than 7,000 people living with CML in Australia. A type of blood cancer, it is caused by a genetic abnormality which fuels the growth of abnormal blood cells. Medical advances mean that many patients can now manage the potentially life-threatening condition through medication, but only 25% ever reach treatment-free remission. The findings have been published in the international journal Leukemia and could help doctors identify much earlier whether a patient is responding well to treatment, allowing therapy to be tailored sooner and moving CML care closer to truly personalized medicine. "This is an important step towards precision medicine, where treatment decisions are guided by the biology of each patient's cancer rather than a one-size-fits-all approach," said Associate Professor Kok. "Our findings reinforce the importance of combining genomic testing with treatment monitoring to work out which patients may benefit from additional therapies or more frequent reviews. "Our goal is to move beyond treating all patients in the same way. By understanding the biology of each person's CML, we can work towards selecting the right treatment, for the right patient, at the right time."
