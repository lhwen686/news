深度解析

这篇新闻报道揭示了多发性硬化症（MS）治疗领域的一个潜在突破，其核心在于髓鞘再生机制的探索与新型给药方式的应用。MS是一种慢性自身免疫性疾病，其病理特征是中枢神经系统髓鞘的脱失，导致神经传导障碍。目前多数疗法仅限于抑制炎症和预防复发，对已受损髓鞘的修复（即髓鞘再生）仍缺乏有效手段，这构成了巨大的临床未满足需求。 文章介绍了一种创新策略：利用低剂量茶碱激活表观遗传活性酶组蛋白去乙酰化酶2（HDAC2），从而促进髓鞘修复。HDAC2作为表观遗传调控因子，其活性可能影响髓鞘形成细胞的分化与成熟，是再生潜力的关键靶点。茶碱作为一种历史悠久、安全性良好的药物，以低剂量“老药新用”的方式被重新发掘，体现了药物再定位（drug repurposing）的巨大价值，能够显著缩短研发周期并降低成本。此外，为实现低浓度持续给药以维持药效并减少副作用，研究团队开发了透皮贴片，这代表了药物递送技术的进步，确保了药代动力学的稳定性和患者依从性。 从行业影响来看，该项目获得110万欧元资助，标志着其从基础研究向临床转化的关键一步。通过遵循良好生产规范（GMP）进行生产优化，并在健康志愿者中开展I期临床试验评估安全性、耐受性和药代动力学，是任何新药或新疗法上市前的必经之路。未来若多中心II期试验能验证其在MS患者中的髓鞘再生疗效，将有望彻底改变MS的治疗格局，从单纯的疾病管理转向积极的损伤修复，为患者带来实质性的神经功能改善。这不仅将为MS患者提供新的治疗选择，也将激发更多针对髓鞘再生的表观遗传调控机制研究和药物递送技术的创新。

英文原文

Original
A therapeutic approach pioneered in Mainz for the treatment of multiple sclerosis (MS) is taking an important step toward clinical application. The team led by Professor Claire Jacob at Johannes Gutenberg University Mainz (JGU) is further developing a drug patch that could promote the repair of damaged myelin sheaths. ForTra gGmbH for Research Transfer (ForTra), a subsidiary of the Else Kröner-Fresenius Foundation, is supporting the project with EUR 1.1 million. The aim is to further develop the patch for production according to pharmaceutical quality standards and subsequently assess its safety and tolerability in an initial clinical trial. Prototypes of transdermal drug patches in the laboratory (left) and study data on the transdermal passage of aminophylline, a compound of theophylline and ethylenediamine, usingsix different transdermal patches that deliver the drug through the skin (right) . Image Credit: Sophia Engel; ill./ Engel et al., AAPS PharmSciTech, 2026 With this funding, we are taking a decisive step toward our goal of translating our research findings into a potential treatment for people with MS." Claire Jacob, Professor of Cellular Neurobiology, Johannes Gutenberg University Mainz (JGU) Multiple sclerosis is a chronic inflammatory autoimmune disease in which the fatty myelin sheath that protects nerve fibers in the brain and spinal cord is damaged. Current therapies primarily aim to reduce inflammation and prevent new relapses. However, there is currently no approved therapy that specifically promotes the rebuilding of already damaged myelin, a process known as remyelination. For more than 20 years, Claire Jacob has been investigating the molecular mechanisms that enable the body to regenerate damaged myelin. Her team identified the epigenetically active enzyme histone deacetylase 2, or HDAC2, as an important factor in this repair process. In their search for a compound capable of specifically activating this enzyme, the researchers came across theophylline, a drug that has been used for decades to treat asthma and other respiratory diseases, although at higher doses. In studies in mice, the Mainz team showed that low-dose theophylline increases HDAC2 activity and can thereby promote the rebuilding of myelin. The researchers are thus using a long-established and well-studied drug for a new therapeutic application at a substantially lower dose. Continuous delivery via a drug patch To achieve the low drug concentration required for remyelination and to maintain it over an extended period, the researchers worked with the group led by Professor Peter Langguth from JGU’s Department of Biopharmaceutics and Pharmaceutical Technology to develop a special transdermal patch. The patch continuously delivers a small amount of theophylline through the skin over several days. Preclinical testing of this therapeutic approach and earlier steps were previously supported by the Mainz Science Foundation with EUR 140,000. ForTra is now funding the next stage of development. The nonprofit subsidiary of the Else Kröner-Fresenius Foundation supports promising medical research projects in their transition toward clinical application. As a first step, the drug patch is to be transferred to a German patch-manufacturing company, where it will be further optimized for larger-scale production under Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards. Compliance with these standards is required for a medicinal product to be used in clinical trials involving humans. The patch will subsequently be tested at the Mainz University Medical Center in a Phase I clinical trial involving healthy volunteers. At this stage, the primary focus will be on patch adhesion, tolerability, and how effectively the drug is delivered through the skin. The trial will not yet show whether the treatment can actually promote the regeneration of damaged myelin sheaths in people with MS. The research team led by Professor Claire Jacob therefore plans a multicenter Phase II trial involving patients with MS in Germany as the next step. This study could investigate for the first time whether the approach can promote remyelination in people with MS. The team is currently seeking additional funding for this trial. "Our long-term goal is to develop a therapy that not only slows the progression of the disease," said Claire Jacob, "but also helps the nervous system repair damage that has already occurred."
