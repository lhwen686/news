英文原文

Original
Fatty liver disease is commonly associated with obesity, yet a growing number of people with normal body weight develop a severe form of the condition. Scientists at Duke-NUS Medical School have now identified a distinct mechanism in a mouse model that may help explain why: excess dietary salt can alter liver metabolism and trigger inflammatory pathways associated with lean metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH). The findings, published in Molecular Metabolism, could help explain why treatments designed for obesity-associated MASH may not work as effectively in lean patients and point towards more tailored approaches to diagnosis and treatment. MASH is a progressive and severe form of liver disease marked by persistent inflammation and tissue scarring. Left untreated, the condition can advance to liver failure, cirrhosis, and liver cell cancer. MASH is increasingly common, particularly in Singapore and other westernised Asian populations, with an estimated prevalence of up to 40 per cent. A growing proportion of people around the world, particularly across Asian populations, have developed MASH despite maintaining a normal body mass index-hence the term 'lean MASH'. Currently, the environmental triggers and molecular mechanisms underlying lean MASH are unclear, and there are no suitable laboratory models to study the disease. To address these challenges, the scientists developed the world's first diet-induced mouse model of lean MASH. The model mirrors key features of lean MASH, including relatively low levels of liver fat but severe inflammation and scarring. This reflects a pattern seen in some patients, who may have less liver fat but face a higher risk of serious liver complications and death. The team, which included researchers from Duke-NUS, National Heart Centre Singapore, Duke University School of Medicine and University College London, also discovered that excess dietary salt changes how the liver handles fat-increasing fat breakdown but at the same time activating immune cells that drive inflammation in the liver. "Our findings suggest that lean MASH is not simply the same disease occurring in a thinner person. The biology appears to be different. While obesity-associated MASH is closely linked to excess fat accumulation, our model shows that high dietary salt can alter liver metabolism and activate inflammatory pathways even without obesity. That distinction matters because treatments developed for obesity-associated MASH may not address what is driving disease in lean patients," explained Dr Zhou Jin, lead author of the study. She is also Principal Research Scientist at Duke-NUS' Cardiovascular & Metabolic Disorders Signature Research Programme and Gilead Research Scholar. Because cardiovascular disease is a primary cause of mortality among MASH patients, establishing a relevant experimental model will have impacts beyond liver health. Professor Derek John Hausenloy, Cardiovascular & Metabolic Disorders Signature Research Programme at Duke-NUS Medical School and one of the authors of the study, said: "This model gives us a way to investigate and understand lean MASH as a whole-body disease, not just a liver condition. That is particularly important because cardiovascular disease is a leading cause of death in patients with MASH. We can now begin to ask whether the mechanisms driving liver injury in lean MASH also affect the heart." This study challenges the assumption that fatty liver disease follows the same biological pathway in every patient. By revealing a distinct mechanism in lean MASH, and providing a preclinical model with which to study it, the team has created an important foundation for developing diagnostics and treatments that reflect the biology of the individual patients." Professor Lok Shee-Mei, Vice-Dean of the Office of Research, Duke-NUS The team will next use this model to identify early diagnostic biomarkers of lean MASH and test compounds that can selectively block salt-induced inflammatory pathways identified in the study. Ultimately, this could help clinicians distinguish lean MASH earlier and determine which treatments are most likely to benefit individual patients. This research is supported by the Singapore Ministry of Health through the National Medical Research Council (NMRC) Office, MOH Holdings Pte Ltd under the NMRC Clinician Scientist Award – Senior Investigator Category (MOH-000306, IGMS-MOH-001964) and the National Research Foundation, Singapore (NRF) under the NMRC Open Fund – Individual Research Grant (IGMS-MOH-001617) administered by the Singapore Ministry of Health through the NMRC Office, MOH Holdings Pte Ltd.

脂肪肝通常与肥胖相关，然而，越来越多的体重正常人群也罹患这种疾病的严重形式。杜克-新加坡国立大学医学院的科学家们现已在小鼠模型中发现了一种独特的机制，这可能有助于解释这一现象：过量的膳食盐分可以改变肝脏代谢，并触发与瘦型代谢功能障碍相关脂肪性肝炎（MASH）相关的炎症通路。 这些发表在《分子代谢》杂志上的研究结果，可能有助于解释为何针对肥胖相关MASH的治疗方法在瘦型患者中效果不佳，并预示着更具个体化的诊断和治疗方法。 MASH是一种进行性且严重的肝脏疾病，其特征是持续性炎症和组织瘢痕化。若不加以治疗，该疾病可进展为肝衰竭、肝硬化和肝细胞癌。MASH的发病率日益增高，尤其在新加坡和其他西化亚洲人群中，估计患病率高达40%。 全球范围内，尤其是亚洲人群中，有越来越多的个体尽管保持正常体重指数，却发展出MASH——因此被称为“瘦型MASH”。目前，瘦型MASH的环境触发因素和分子机制尚不明确，且缺乏合适的研究该疾病的实验室模型。 为应对这些挑战，科学家们开发了世界上首个饮食诱导的瘦型MASH小鼠模型。该模型反映了瘦型MASH的关键特征，包括相对较低的肝脏脂肪水平，但伴有严重的炎症和瘢痕化。这与部分患者的模式相符，他们可能肝脏脂肪较少，但面临更高的严重肝脏并发症和死亡风险。 该团队成员包括来自杜克-新加坡国立大学、新加坡国家心脏中心、杜克大学医学院和伦敦大学学院的研究人员，他们还发现过量的膳食盐分会改变肝脏处理脂肪的方式——增加脂肪分解，但同时激活驱动肝脏炎症的免疫细胞。 该研究的第一作者周瑾博士解释说：“我们的发现表明，瘦型MASH并非仅仅是发生在瘦体质人群中的同一种疾病。其生物学机制似乎有所不同。虽然肥胖相关MASH与过多的脂肪堆积密切相关，但我们的模型显示，高膳食盐分即使在没有肥胖的情况下也能改变肝脏代谢并激活炎症通路。这种区别至关重要，因为为肥胖相关MASH开发的治疗方法可能无法解决瘦型患者的致病驱动因素。”她同时也是杜克-新加坡国立大学心血管与代谢疾病重点研究项目首席研究科学家以及吉利德研究学者。 鉴于心血管疾病是MASH患者的主要死亡原因，建立一个相关的实验模型将对肝脏健康之外的领域产生影响。 杜克-新加坡国立大学医学院心血管与代谢疾病重点研究项目的德里克·约翰·豪森洛伊教授（Professor Derek John Hausenloy）是该研究的作者之一，他表示： “这个模型为我们提供了一种方法，将瘦型MASH作为一种全身性疾病而非仅仅是肝脏疾病来研究和理解。这一点尤为重要，因为心血管疾病是MASH患者的主要死因。我们现在可以开始探讨，驱动瘦型MASH肝损伤的机制是否也影响心脏。” 杜克-新加坡国立大学研究办公室副院长骆思梅教授（Professor Lok Shee-Mei）表示：“这项研究挑战了脂肪肝疾病在所有患者中遵循相同生物学通路的假设。通过揭示瘦型MASH中独特的机制，并提供一个用于研究的临床前模型，该团队为开发反映个体患者生物学特征的诊断和治疗方法奠定了重要基础。” 该团队接下来将利用此模型来识别瘦型MASH的早期诊断生物标志物，并测试能够选择性阻断研究中发现的盐诱导炎症通路的化合物。最终，这可能有助于临床医生更早地鉴别瘦型MASH，并确定哪些治疗方案最有可能使个体患者受益。 本研究由新加坡卫生部通过国家医学研究理事会（NMRC）办公室、MOH Holdings Pte Ltd在NMRC临床科学家奖 – 高级研究员类别（MOH-000306, IGMS-MOH-001964）下以及新加坡国家研究基金会（NRF）通过由新加坡卫生部国家医学研究理事会（NMRC）办公室、MOH Holdings Pte Ltd管理的NMRC开放基金 – 个人研究补助金（IGMS-MOH-001617）提供支持。
