A gut microbial health index as a composite biomarker for evaluating overall health

深度解析

这篇新闻报道详细阐述了微生物健康指数（MHI）这一核心专业概念，它是一种创新的复合生物标志物，用于量化肠道微生物群落对整体身心健康的影响。MHI通过计算97种健康相关细菌与31种疾病相关细菌扩增子序列变异体（ASVs）的对数比来得出，将复杂的微生物组数据简化为一个可操作的单一数值。文章强调，MHI不仅受到饮食和生活方式（如高纤维摄入、地中海饮食、体育锻炼）的显著影响，也能反映超加工食品摄入和亚临床炎症等负面因素。这种动态响应机制揭示了微生物组并非仅仅是疾病的旁观者，而是宿主健康状况的敏感指示器，其结构和功能随着宿主行为而迅速调整，从而影响全身健康，例如改善血脂状况和运动能力。 MHI及其背后的研究具有深远的行业影响。首先，在个性化医疗领域，MHI提供了一个强大的工具，可根据个体微生物组特征和生活方式因素制定量身定制的干预方案。其次，它推动了预防医学的发展，通过识别可干预的修改因素，MHI能帮助临床医生和个体早期发现健康风险并主动采取措施。再者，对于公共卫生策略制定者，MHI将复杂的微生物学转化为可量化的指标，有助于评估和指导基于饮食和运动的健康促进项目。最后，它为药物研发和生物标志物发现提供了新的视角，MHI作为一个动态、敏感的生物标志物，有望用于评估新疗法的效果或识别新的治疗靶点。

Credit: Yael Haberman Each person has a unique gut microbial population, with variation shaped by diet, lifestyle and disease. We are seeing a rapid rise in metabolic disorders, obesity and inflammatory diseases that cannot be explained by our relatively stable genetic code. One of the most encouraging findings in common modern diseases is that modifiable factors can outweigh genetic predispositions. Unlike genetics, the microbiome is a dynamic system that responds to the daily choices we make. We previously identified shared microbial responses across cohorts with different diseases (PMID: 35197084) and used this signal to build a per-sample microbial health index (MHI). This tool was later applied successfully in analyses of independent disease cohorts. However, most of those studies focused on disease because detailed information about lifestyle and dietary exposures was limited, and they mainly compared specific diseases with controls. Technically, the MHI is the log ratio of 97 health-associated bacterial variants to 31 disease-associated variants (amplicon sequence variants, or ASVs), providing a single quantifiable metric that condenses complex microbial data into a per-subject score. Testing the index in adults In the journal Gut Microbes we now identify the gut MHI as a powerful composite biomarker for assessing overall physical and mental well-being in adults. In a cohort of 893 individuals, we show that the index is strongly influenced by lifestyle and dietary choices, both in those without documented disease and among people with existing conditions such as hypertension or metabolic syndrome. Participants underwent routine annual Executive Medical Checkups at a tertiary medical center. Data were collected from hospital electronic medical records (EMR), laboratory tests, ICD-10 disease codes, prescribed Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) medication classes, cardiac stress test ergometry (Bruce protocol), and detailed lifestyle and food frequency questionnaires. Fecal microbial composition was determined by 16S sequencing (V4), and we calculated per-participant microbial indices. Diet and fitness leave a mark We found that high fiber intake, adherence to a Mediterranean diet and better physical fitness correlated with a healthier microbiome and improved blood lipid profiles. In contrast, ultra-processed food consumption and higher levels of subclinical inflammation were associated with lower MHI scores. Using the Bruce protocol cardiac stress test, we measured METs (metabolic equivalents), an objective measure of exercise capacity, and found a positive correlation between METs and the MHI. This suggests that exercise does more than modulate cardiovascular tone; it is also linked to microbial architecture that supports systemic health. By quantifying the contribution of modifiable dietary and lifestyle factors to the MHI, we identified actionable ways to improve or worsen an individual's score using regression models. Estimating the dose of change Drawing on previously published intervention studies, we show that the MHI responds quickly to dietary change and shifts within the predicted range. Based on our regression models, we can estimate the behavioral "dosage" needed to improve a personal MHI score by 0.5 points—a shift that may reflect a meaningful gain in microbial resilience and has previously been linked to multiple diseases. Overall, this suggests that the gut microbiome may serve as a sensitive reporter of health status, much like blood pressure or lipid levels. A single metric for complex health The World Health Organization (WHO) defines health as a state of complete "physical, mental and social well-being." In that context, the Microbial Health Index can be viewed as a holistic health reporter that captures the intersection of lifestyle, environment and biology. For health strategists, the complexity of the gut microbiome has often limited clinical utility. The MHI addresses this by providing a single quantifiable metric that turns complex microbial architecture into actionable data. It also suggests that the same bacteria may contribute to both disease- and lifestyle-related effects, implying that these bacteria respond to the host's exposures and conditions rather than directly causing the health issues themselves. The lab (www.habermanlab.sites.tau.ac.il/) uses large clinical datasets, omics technologies including microbiome research, and basic studies with patient-derived models to better understand disease development, build predictive models, and identify new treatment targets. I am a professor of pediatric gastroenterology, lead the Microbiome Center at the Sheba Medical Center, am affiliated with the Faculty of Medicine at Tel Aviv University, and serve as an adjunct associate professor at Cincinnati Children's Hospital.
