研究人员探讨血管损伤与记忆力减退的关系

Researcher examines how blood vessel damage relates to memory loss

深度解析

这篇研究通过对死后大脑的细致检查，揭示了脑部血管病变在认知功能障碍中扮演的复杂角色，并区分了两种主要血管病理与其对记忆和思维影响的差异。核心专业概念在于区分“小动脉硬化症”（arteriolosclerosis）和“脑淀粉样血管病”（cerebral amyloid angiopathy, CAA）的临床意义及其与阿尔茨海默病（AD）病理学的不同关联。小动脉硬化症表现为脑部小血管壁的增厚和硬化，即便在统计学上排除了AD相关病变的影响后，仍与记忆、语言及执行功能下降独立相关，提示其可能通过慢性缺血或血脑屏障功能障碍等机制直接损害神经元功能。相比之下，脑淀粉样血管病是淀粉样蛋白在血管壁的异常沉积，其与认知能力下降的关联在很大程度上可由AD相关的淀粉样斑块和神经原纤维缠结等病理变化解释。 这一区分对痴呆症的预防和治疗策略具有深远影响。它强调了认知障碍的病因是多因素的，单一靶向阿尔茨海默病淀粉样蛋白的治疗方案可能无法完全解决血管性认知功能障碍。未来，结合血管健康干预（如控制高血压、糖尿病等血管风险因素）与AD特异性治疗的多模式干预策略，有望更全面地延缓或逆转认知衰退。此外，该研究也提示了在活体诊断中，需要更精确的生物标志物来区分不同类型的血管病理，从而实现更精准的个体化治疗。

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: Credit: Pixabay/CC0 Public Domain Alzheimer's disease is often the focus when someone develops memory problems. But changes in the brain's blood vessels may also be part of the picture—and new research by the University of Kentucky's Cellas Hayes, Ph.D., helps explain why those changes deserve closer attention. Hayes, an assistant professor who recently joined UK's Sanders-Brown Center on Aging, and his collaborators examined how different types of blood vessel damage relate to memory and thinking, and how much of those relationships could be explained by Alzheimer's-related brain changes. Their findings, published in the journal Brain, point to an important distinction: While amyloid protein buildup in blood vessel walls was closely connected with Alzheimer's-related changes, thickening and hardening of the brain's small blood vessels remained linked to poorer thinking abilities even after researchers accounted for those changes. "Different types of blood vessel damage may tell us different things about memory loss," Hayes said. "Understanding those differences could help researchers develop more complete approaches to dementia prevention and treatment." Linking cognitive tests with brain examinations The study used data from 1,269 people who participated in research at 34 Alzheimer's Disease Research Centers (ADRCs), including those at Sanders-Brown's ADRC. Participants completed tests of memory and thinking during their lives and donated their brains for examination after death. Their average age at death was 84. Researchers compared participants' most recent test results with findings from their brain examinations. The tests assessed memory, the ability to use and understand words, and skills such as planning and problem-solving. The team examined five types of blood vessel damage, including thickened small vessels, fatty deposits in larger arteries, protein buildup in vessel walls and areas of brain tissue damaged by interrupted blood flow. Their analyses accounted for differences in age, sex, education and a genetic factor associated with Alzheimer's risk. Two vessel conditions show different patterns Two forms of vessel disease stood out. The first, called arteriolosclerosis, involves thickening and hardening of the brain's small blood vessels. About 41% of participants had moderate or severe changes of this type. More severe small-vessel thickening was associated with poorer performance in memory, language, and planning and problem-solving. Although Alzheimer's-related changes explained part of those relationships in the researchers' statistical models, a substantial connection remained after those changes were taken into account. "Blood vessel health is an important part of understanding memory loss," Hayes said. "In our study, damage to the brain's small blood vessels was linked to poorer memory and thinking even after accounting for Alzheimer's-related changes." The second condition, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, occurs when amyloid protein builds up in blood vessel walls. About 56% of participants had these deposits. Greater severity of this condition was also associated with poorer performance across all three thinking abilities. However, Alzheimer's-related changes accounted for a much larger share of those relationships—roughly 60% to 90%, depending on the thinking ability measured. These estimates describe relationships within the study's statistical models, rather than an individual person's risk of dementia. Treatments may need to address multiple contributors Together, the findings help distinguish conditions that often occur in the same brain but may have different relationships with memory and thinking. That distinction could help guide future research into treatments that address several contributors to cognitive problems. Therapies focused on Alzheimer's-related changes alone may leave other sources of difficulty unaddressed, including changes to the vessels and blood flow in the brain. "To develop more complete approaches to dementia, we need to understand the multiple types of damage that can occur in the same brain," Hayes said. "An important next question is whether addressing blood vessel disease alongside Alzheimer's-related changes can better preserve memory and thinking." Causation and broader applicability remain uncertain The study does not establish that blood vessel damage causes Alzheimer's disease or that treating it will prevent memory loss. Because the brain conditions were examined together after death, researchers could not determine which developed first. The participants also represented a selected research population: Approximately 95% were white, their average education was 16 years and about 60% had dementia at their last assessment. Further studies are needed to determine whether the same patterns appear in more diverse populations. Following people over time, while measuring blood vessel changes, Alzheimer's markers and thinking abilities, will be an important next step. The findings connect with broader work underway in Hayes' laboratory at Sanders-Brown and UK's Department of Neuroscience. His team studies how blood markers of Alzheimer's disease and other brain changes evolve over time, alongside genetic research and investigations into how vascular health and social factors contribute to differences in brain aging. The goal is to identify opportunities to reduce risk, slow disease progression and develop more personalized approaches to care. Hayes also emphasized the contributions of research participants and their families, whose decisions to donate made the study possible. "Brain donation provides an extraordinary opportunity to understand what underlies memory and thinking problems," Hayes said. "This work reflects the generosity of participants and families who wanted to help future generations." Publication details Cellas A Hayes et al., Alzheimer's pathology mediates arteriolosclerosis and cerebral amyloid angiopathy associations with cognition, Brain (2026). DOI: 10.1093/brain/awag380 Journal information: Brain
