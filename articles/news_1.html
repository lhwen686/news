The sleepy sickness in Vilhelmina—100-year-old mystery investigated

深度解析

这项研究通过回溯性流行病学分析，揭示了20世纪20年代瑞典嗜睡性脑炎（Encephalitis lethargica）的地理传播模式及其与前期其他流行病（如西班牙流感和腮腺炎）的潜在关联。其核心专业概念在于提出了“多重感染假说”，即多种病原体感染序列或共同作用，可能导致宿主免疫系统异常激活或对神经系统造成累积性损害，最终诱发嗜睡性脑炎。维尔赫尔米纳地区流感与腮腺炎联合高发，暗示了协同感染在疾病病原学中的重要角色。 该研究对当今医学和公共卫生领域具有深远影响。它强调了感染后状态（post-infectious conditions）的长期性和复杂性，与当前“新冠后遗症”（post-COVID）等问题高度契合，促使我们深入探讨病毒感染如何诱发慢性病变。研究方法论突出了历史健康数据在重构流行病学图谱中的不可替代性，为现代流行病学研究提供了借鉴。理解多重感染与疾病发展关系，有助于更全面评估新发传染病的潜在风险，并指导制定更具前瞻性的疾病预防和治疗策略，从而提升全球卫生应对能力。

Credit: Umea University Researchers at CEDAR have studied and perhaps found an important piece in understanding one of the biggest medicinal mysteries of our time: the Sleepy Sickness that swept over Europe in the early 1900´s. During the 1920's, Sweden was one of the hardest-hit countries in the world, with the highest number of reported cases in Västerbotten, and especially the inland community of Vilhelmina. Lena Karlsson and Erling Häggström Gunfridsson are both researchers at the Centre of Demographic and Ageing Research (CEDAR). On Sept. 8, they published a research article titled "Encephalitis lethargica in Sweden, 1920–1930: national incidence and the 1921 outbreak in northern Sweden," a study that investigates the geographic spread of the mysterious sleepy sickness. The work is published in the journal Epidemiology and Infection. Wanted to examine the spread in Sweden The disease encephalitis lethargica, or sleepy sickness, emerged around 1926 and disappeared around 1930, and what caused this disease is a mystery to this day. Sweden was one of the hardest-hit countries, which made the researchers Lena and Erling interested in examining how the disease spread in the country. To map the spread, they analyzed national statistics from historical health care reports from SCB, and provincial medical reports from Regionarkivet Västerbotten. "We wanted to see the geographic spread of the disease, and which other epidemics emerged before and after the outbreak. We also wanted to see if the results could contribute to understanding possible causes and mechanisms of the disease," says Karlsson. The results show Vilhelmina as the hardest-hit community The results show a clear geographical pattern in the spread of the sleepy sickness. It affected the Stockholm area first, and then quickly spread north. In 1921, Sweden had the highest number of reported cases in the world, with a concentration in Västerbotten. Out of all reported cases in Västerbotten, almost 80% were located in Vilhelmina. The result surprised the researchers, since epidemics usually hit the hardest in densely populated cities, rather than sparsely populated areas. "We suspect that the sleepy sickness most likely spread in the cities first, but that it happened before it was mandatory to report the cases," explains Häggström Gunfridsson. Can perhaps explain the development of the disease Another interesting discovery was that Vilhelmina had severe cases of both the Spanish flu and mumps prior to the sleepy sickness outbreak, which may help explain the emergence of the sleepy sickness. "We were surprised to find such a major mumps epidemic only a few months prior to the outbreak of the sleepy sickness. There are theories of a connection between the Spanish flu and sleepy sickness, but we could not see such connections in other areas. However, the combination of the Spanish flu and mumps may be relevant in the understanding of why Vilhelmina was so severely affected by the sleepy sickness. This finding is also consistent with hypotheses that multiple infections may have interacted in the development and spread of the disease," the researchers elaborate. The results are relevant for the medical research of today The study shows how important historical population and health data can be to reconstruct epidemics and thus highlight questions that are still relevant within medical research today. Additionally, the results can contribute new perspectives on post-infectious conditions and long-term effects after infections. These questions are still highly relevant to this day, for example when it comes to post-COVID. More information Lena Karlsson et al., Encephalitis lethargica in Sweden, 1920–1930: national incidence and the 1921 outbreak in northern Sweden, Epidemiology and Infection (2026).
