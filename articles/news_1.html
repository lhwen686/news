深度解析

该新闻深度探讨了活髓切断术（Pulpotomy）作为一种牙齿保存治疗方案在全科牙科实践中推广的潜力，并阐述了其与传统根管治疗（Root Canal Therapy）的区别及行业影响。 核心专业概念与医学机制： 根管治疗是一种清除牙齿内部感染或坏死牙髓组织（包括冠髓和根髓）并进行填充的复杂手术，通常需转诊至牙髓病专科医生。它适用于不可逆性牙髓炎或牙髓坏死。而活髓切断术（亦称活髓治疗）的机制则是在保留牙根部健康牙髓组织的前提下，仅移除发炎的冠部牙髓组织，以达到缓解疼痛、控制感染并维持牙齿活力的目的。其侵入性相对较小，更易于由训练有素的全科牙医完成，适用于部分选择性患者。 行业影响与商业逻辑： 这一转变具有显著的行业影响。首先，活髓切断术的普及将极大提高牙齿保存治疗的可及性，尤其是在牙髓病专科医生资源匮乏的地区。患者能更早、更便捷地获得治疗，减少等待时间、出行成本和医疗费用。其次，它提升了全科牙医在牙齿保存治疗中的角色和能力，减少不必要的转诊，优化诊所工作流程，符合“在家门口看专家”的社区医疗理念。从商业逻辑看，国家牙科实践培训中心（NDP-TC）通过标准化培训（如基于病例的学习、3D模型模拟、Project ECHO虚拟指导）来弥合研究证据与临床实践之间的鸿沟，降低了新技术的采纳壁垒。这种可扩展的转化平台模式，不仅能推广活髓切断术，未来也可应用于其他循证牙科实践，加速科学发现转化为日常患者护理的速度，推动整个口腔医疗行业的持续发展和创新。

Original
Which would you rather have: a root canal or a pulpotomy? Even if you're not familiar with a pulpotomy, you might be inclined to learn more. That is what a new national center has started to explore, funded with nearly $2 million from the National Institutes of Health, and co-led by principal investigator Rahma Mungia, BDS, MSc, DDPHRCS, associate professor in the School of Dentistry at The University of Texas at San Antonio. While a root canal often is performed for a tooth that is deeply decayed and painful, drilling into its top to remove inflamed pulp tissue inside and sealing with a crown, the procedure usually must be referred by your general dentist to an endodontic specialist. With that can come a second appointment, a new office, time away from work and another bill. In places where endodontists are scarce, the wait can be long, and some patients never complete the referral. But research suggests that a pulpotomy – sometimes called vital pulp therapy, in which inflamed tissue is removed from inside the crown of the tooth while preserving healthy pulp tissue in the roots, relieving pain while saving the tooth – could be done more often in a general dental practice for select patients. It might not be appropriate for every patient, as sometimes a root canal is unavoidable. But for appropriately selected patients, it might provide a tooth-preserving treatment without the need for immediate root-canal therapy. As an endodontist, I know how much it means to a patient to stop the pain and keep their natural tooth. This alternative approach may help general dentists and endodontists offer it safely to the right patients." Kenneth M. Hargreaves, DDS, PhD, Dean of the UT San Antonio School of Dentistry Closing the gap between what research supports and what happens in everyday clinical practice is the focus of the new NIH award establishing the National Dental Practice Training Center (NDP-TC), a collaboration of the UT San Antonio Health Science Center with the University of Alabama at Birmingham and the University of Rochester, together with the National Dental Practice-Based Research Network and the Project ECHO Superhub based at UT San Antonio's health science center. The National Dental Practice-Based Research Network is a U.S. consortium in which dental clinics and researchers work together on real-world patient care studies. Project ECHO (Extension for Community Healthcare Outcomes) is a virtual training and mentoring framework that helps local health providers treat complex medical conditions in their own communities. Joining Mungia as co-leader and principal investigator of the new center is Joana Cunha-Cruz, DDS, PhD, professor at the UAB School of Dentistry. Together, they will lead the development, implementation and rigorous evaluation of the national training platform, beginning with pulpotomy. Mungia also leads the Southwest Regional Node of the National Dental Practice-Based Research Network and directs the South Texas Oral Health Network. Translating evidence to clinical practice The project began Sept. 1 and runs through Aug. 31, 2028. It marks the opening phase of an NIH UG3/UH3 award funded through the National Center for Advancing Translational Sciences (NCATS) and the National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research (NIDCR). If the team meets its predefined milestones, the project will move into a funded three-year multicenter trial of the training program. The center's first course focuses on pulpotomy treatment, using this procedure as an initial model for accelerating the translation of evidence into clinical practice. In its first year, the research team will survey dentists about what stands in the way of using it: clinical knowledge, confidence, technical training, office workflow or access to expert support. The answers will shape the course. Dentists then will work through patient cases and X-rays, discuss difficult decisions with colleagues and mentors and practice on 3D-printed tooth models. The training aims to do more than teach a sequence of clinical steps. It will help dentists decide which patients may benefit, which patients need another approach and how to carry out the procedure safely. "A treatment decision takes place in the context of a patient's symptoms, clinical and radiographic findings, and the practice environment," Mungia said. "Case-based learning gives dentists a way to practice that judgment. Hands-on simulation lets them develop the procedural skills before they use them with a patient. Our broader goal is to understand how we can move evidence into everyday practice more efficiently and then apply that model to other areas of care." Many dentists were trained at a time when the pulpotomy procedure was not routinely taught as a definitive option for permanent teeth, before strong evidence for its effectiveness emerged. Cunha-Cruz says the goal is to understand what makes it challenging for dentists to adopt the procedure and build training that supports real change in practice. "Patients in pain may face a delay, travel and extra expense simply because the treatment they need is not available in the office they already trust," Cunha-Cruz said. "For appropriately selected patients, pulpotomy may provide another option. "When we understand the real barriers to putting research into practice, we can build training that actually works," she said. "I'm grateful to NIH for supporting this vision, and I'm confident it will help patients get evidence-based care sooner." The center is designed as a scalable, reusable national translational platform that can be adapted to other evidence-based dental practices beyond pulpotomy, helping shorten the time between scientific discovery and routine patient care. Mungia is a nationally recognized leader in translational oral health research, implementation science and practice-based research networks. Her professional journey began in India, where she earned her Dental Degree from the KLE Institute and practiced as a general dentist. Witnessing firsthand the profound impact of social and structural determinants on oral health inspired her to pursue advanced training in public health and population-based research. She earned a Master of Science in dental public health from University College London and a Diploma in Dental Public Health from the Royal College of Surgeons (DDPHRCS) of England, followed by completion of a dental public health residency at the then-University of Texas Health Science Center at San Antonio in 2005. Today, the South Texas Oral Health Network she directs is a community-engaged practice-based research network supported by UT San Antonio Health Science Center's Clinical and Translational Science Award (CTSA) program, creating a direct bridge between community dental practices, the National Dental PBRN and the university's translational science infrastructure.
