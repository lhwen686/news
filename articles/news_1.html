儿童时期抗生素使用与青年期肠易激综合征发病风险增加相关

Antibiotic use from childhood linked to higher odds of IBS in young adulthood

深度解析

本文深入探讨了儿童期至成年早期抗生素使用与24岁时肠易激综合征（IBS）风险之间的流行病学关联。IBS是一种常见的功能性胃肠道疾病，以慢性腹痛和排便习惯改变为特征。研究利用瑞典BAMSE出生队列的客观处方数据，克服了以往研究中依赖患者回忆的局限性，发现抗生素使用次数越多，患IBS的几率越高，特别是四环素类和头孢菌素类抗生素。这一发现为抗生素暴露与IBS的潜在联系提供了更坚实的证据。 其核心医学机制推测与肠道微生物组的改变密切相关。抗生素作为广谱或窄谱的抗菌药物，在清除病原菌的同时，也会不可避免地破坏肠道内共生微生物群的平衡，导致肠道菌群失调（dysbiosis）。这种失调可能影响肠道屏障功能、免疫调节及神经内分泌信号传导，进而诱发或加剧IBS的症状。尽管本研究未建立明确的因果关系，但其结果强调了抗生素使用，尤其是不必要的或重复的疗程，可能带来的长期健康影响。从行业影响来看，这项研究进一步强化了全球范围内推广“抗生素管理”（Antibiotic Stewardship）的必要性。医疗专业人员应在权衡抗生素治疗的益处与潜在长期风险后，更加谨慎地开具处方，避免不必要的广谱抗生素使用。这不仅有助于减少IBS等慢性疾病的发生风险，也是应对日益严峻的抗生素耐药性问题的重要策略。未来的研究需要通过更深入的微生物组分析和随机对照试验来阐明确切的因果链和潜在干预靶点。

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: Credit: Unsplash/CC0 Public Domain Antibiotic use from childhood through early adulthood was associated with higher odds of irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) at age 24, with the odds increasing as the number of antibiotic prescriptions rose, according to a new study presented at UEG Week 2026. IBS is a common digestive disorder that affects more than 60 million people across Europe, causing symptoms such as abdominal pain and changes in bowel habits. While previous research has suggested a link between antibiotic use and IBS, evidence on whether childhood exposure is associated with IBS in adulthood remains inconsistent. Much of the existing evidence relies on people recalling past antibiotic use, with a lack of population-based studies using objectively recorded antibiotic data across childhood. To address this gap, researchers analyzed data from 2,853 participants in the population-based Swedish BAMSE birth cohort. Antibiotic prescriptions were obtained from the Swedish Prescribed Drug Register from 2005, when participants were aged 9–11 years, until one year before IBS was assessed at age 24. The researchers examined whether participants had received antibiotics, the total number of prescriptions received and the classes of antibiotics prescribed. Higher prescription counts, higher IBS odds At age 24, 10.0% of participants had IBS, of whom 79.4% were female. Antibiotic use was more common among those with IBS, with 87.8% having received antibiotics during the study period compared with 80.2% of those without IBS. After accounting for sex and family socioeconomic status, those who had received antibiotics had 1.5 times the odds of IBS (95% CI, 1.0–2.2) compared with those who had not. The odds of IBS increased progressively with the number of antibiotic prescriptions (P for trend <0.001). Compared with those who received no antibiotics, participants receiving one or two prescriptions had 1.3 times the odds of IBS (95% CI, 0.9–2.0), rising to 1.6 times (95% CI, 1.0–2.4) with three to five prescriptions and 1.7 times (95% CI, 1.1–2.6) with six or more. When individual antibiotic classes were examined, tetracyclines and cephalosporins remained significantly associated with higher odds of IBS after adjusting for sex and family socioeconomic status. Any use of tetracyclines was associated with 1.4 times (95% CI, 1.1–1.8) the odds of IBS, while three or more prescriptions of cephalosporins were associated with 6.0 times the odds (95% CI, 1.9–18.8). Lead author Dr. Jessica Sjölund said the study provides an important step forward in understanding the relationship between antibiotic exposure and IBS. "By examining a population-based group of young adults with antibiotic exposure objectively recorded from childhood and adolescence, we were able to address key limitations of earlier research, including reliance on recalled antibiotic use and specialist health care records to identify IBS, which may not capture the full spectrum of IBS in the general population." "These findings strengthen the epidemiological evidence for a potential link between antibiotic exposure and IBS and support further research into this relationship, although it does not establish causality." The gut microbiome as a possible pathway While the study did not investigate why antibiotic exposure was associated with IBS, one possible explanation for the association involves the gut microbiome. Sjölund explained, "Previous studies have shown that antibiotics can alter the gut microbiome, with observational research suggesting that these changes may persist for several years and may be more pronounced following repeated courses. As alterations to the gut microbiome are thought to play a role in IBS, this could be one possible pathway underlying the association we observed." Discussing the next steps for research, Sjölund said, "Larger, long-term studies with repeated assessment of IBS over time could help determine whether antibiotic exposure precedes IBS and whether the timing of exposure or type of antibiotic matters. "Looking more closely at infections, other health conditions and psychological and family factors could also help clarify what is contributing to the association. Alongside this, repeated assessments of the gut microbiome could offer further insight into whether microbiome changes are involved." Essential treatment, careful prescribing Reflecting on the practical implications of the findings, Sjölund stressed the need to balance the essential role of antibiotics with careful prescribing. "Antibiotics remain an important and sometimes essential treatment for bacterial infections, and our findings should not discourage their use when they are needed. Rather, they highlight the possibility of longer-term effects from repeated exposure and add to the reasons for avoiding unnecessary antibiotic prescribing." More information Sjölund, J. et al.
