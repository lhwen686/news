ESCRT-III proteins play an unexpected role in protecting DNA bridges during cell division

深度解析

这项开创性研究阐明了一种新的细胞保护机制，用于应对DNA桥的危害。DNA桥是细胞分裂不完全时连接新生子细胞的危险DNA链。通常，精准的DNA复制和染色体分离对于基因组完整性至关重要。然而，当染色体未能完全分离时，DNA桥便会形成，对基因组稳定性构成重大威胁，而基因组不稳定已知是包括癌症在内多种疾病的前兆。 研究发现，ESCRT-III蛋白（此前已知其在细胞膜重塑和修复中发挥作用）是这些DNA桥的关键防御者。该机制的一个核心组成部分是NoCut检查点，它在DNA桥存在时延迟细胞分裂的最后阶段。这种延迟为ESCRT-III蛋白赢得了时间，使其能够迁移至DNA桥处，形成保护性涂层，从而有效防止DNA损伤。尽管DNA桥在健康细胞中罕见，但其与基因组不稳定性和早期癌症事件的关联不容忽视。该发现虽未直接证实与癌症的因果关系，但为理解细胞如何抵御导致基因组不稳定和疾病的DNA损伤事件提供了新的视角，为未来针对癌症等疾病的早期干预和治疗策略开发奠定了重要的理论基础，具有重要的临床前研究意义。

英文原文

Original
Every time a cell divides, it must accurately copy and distribute its DNA between two new cells. However, in some cases, chromosomes do not fully separate, leaving behind thin strands of DNA known as DNA bridges that connect the two newly formed cells. These fragile structures can be vulnerable to damage and, if not properly managed, can have severe consequences for genome stability. Researchers from King's, in collaboration with research teams at the University of Utah and biotechnology company Altos Labs, have now revealed how cells protect themselves from this threat. Published in Nature Structural & Molecular Biology, the study shows that ESCRT-III proteins, previously known for helping cells reshape and repair membranes, have an unexpected role in protecting DNA bridges during cell division. During cell division, a safety mechanism called the NoCut checkpoint helps delay the final stages of division when DNA bridges are present. The researchers found that this delay allows ESCRT-III proteins to move to the DNA bridges, where they form a protective coating around the exposed DNA and help prevent damage. Although DNA bridges are rare in healthy cells, research has linked them to genome instability and some early events associated with cancer development. While the study does not directly demonstrate a link between this mechanism and cancer development, it provides new insight into how cells protect themselves from DNA damage events that have previously been associated with genome instability and disease. Juan Martin-Serrano, Professor of Viral Cell Biology at King's and co-senior author of the study, said: "The discovery came from an unexpected observation. We saw ESCRT proteins binding to DNA but didn't understand why. After more than 20 years studying these proteins, I never imagined we would uncover this completely new role for them. There is still much more to understand, and our next steps will be to investigate how these DNA bridges form and what enables them to protect the genome. To study these events at the molecular level, the team used cryo-electron microscopy to determine the structure of the ESCRT/DNA complex. Advanced live-cell microscopy allowed them to watch cells divide in real time. Researchers created specialised cell lines containing fluorescently labelled proteins, enabling them to track DNA and ESCRT proteins as they moved inside living cells. Because DNA bridges occur infrequently, the researchers used two approaches to study them. They first analysed large numbers of cells to identify naturally occurring DNA bridges. They also temporarily blocked the activity of a protein involved in separating DNA during cell division, increasing the number of DNA bridges available for detailed analysis. Dr James Glover, co-first author of the study, completed his undergraduate degree, master's and PhD at King's, with his doctoral research carried out in Professor Martin-Serrano's laboratory through the Medical Research Council Doctoral Training Partnership (MRC DTP). He is now continuing his research career internationally as a postdoctoral researcher at the University of California, Berkeley. Reflecting on the significance of the discovery, Dr Glover said: "When we think about cell division, we often imagine the textbook picture of one cell dividing into two cells with DNA being perfectly distributed. But in reality, many complications can happen as those cells separate. "What we've discovered is that ESCRT proteins can come in and protect DNA bridges, preventing them from causing damage. The fact that a family of proteins known for remodelling membranes can also protect DNA is such an unexpected and exciting discovery. The study was supported by the Wellcome Trust, the Biotechnology and Biological Sciences Research Council (BBSRC), and the Medical Research Council (MRC) Doctoral Training Partnership (DTP).

AI Translation
细胞每次分裂时，都必须准确复制其DNA并将其分配到两个新细胞中。然而，在某些情况下，染色体未能完全分离，从而留下连接两个新形成细胞的细长DNA链，即所谓的DNA桥。 这些脆弱的结构易受损伤，如果处理不当，可能对基因组稳定性造成严重后果。现在，伦敦国王学院的研究人员与犹他大学及生物技术公司 Altos Labs 的研究团队合作，揭示了细胞如何保护自身免受这种威胁。 这项发表在《自然结构与分子生物学》杂志上的研究表明，ESCRT-III蛋白（此前已知其功能是帮助细胞重塑和修复细胞膜）在细胞分裂过程中，对保护DNA桥具有意想不到的作用。 在细胞分裂过程中，当DNA桥存在时，一种称为NoCut检查点的安全机制有助于延迟分裂的最后阶段。研究人员发现，这种延迟使得ESCRT-III蛋白能够移动到DNA桥处，并在暴露的DNA周围形成一层保护性涂层，从而帮助防止损伤。 尽管DNA桥在健康细胞中很少见，但研究已将其与基因组不稳定以及与癌症发展相关的一些早期事件联系起来。虽然这项研究并未直接证明该机制与癌症发展之间存在联系，但它为细胞如何保护自身免受此前已与基因组不稳定和疾病相关的DNA损伤事件提供了新的见解。 伦敦国王学院病毒细胞生物学教授兼该研究的共同通讯作者胡安·马丁-塞拉诺（Juan Martin-Serrano）表示：“这项发现源于一次意想不到的观察。我们看到ESCRT蛋白与DNA结合，但不明白其原因。在研究这些蛋白20多年后，我从未想到我们会揭示它们这个全新的作用。仍有许多方面需要理解，我们的下一步将是研究这些DNA桥如何形成以及是什么使它们能够保护基因组。” 为了在分子水平上研究这些事件，该团队使用了低温电子显微镜来确定ESCRT/DNA复合物的结构。先进的活细胞显微镜使他们能够实时观察细胞分裂。研究人员创建了含有荧光标记蛋白的特异性细胞系，从而能够追踪DNA和ESCRT蛋白在活细胞内的移动。 由于DNA桥的发生频率不高，研究人员采用了两种方法来研究它们。他们首先分析了大量细胞以识别自然发生的DNA桥。他们还暂时性地阻断了参与细胞分裂过程中DNA分离的一种蛋白质的活性，从而增加了可用于详细分析的DNA桥数量。 该研究的共同第一作者詹姆斯·格洛弗博士（Dr James Glover）在伦敦国王学院完成了本科学位、硕士学位和博士学位，其博士研究在马丁-塞拉诺教授的实验室通过医学研究理事会博士培训合作项目（MRC DTP）进行。他现在正作为加州大学伯克利分校的博士后研究员，在国际上继续其研究生涯。 谈及这项发现的重要性，格洛弗博士表示：“当我们思考细胞分裂时，我们通常会想象教科书上一个细胞分裂成两个细胞、DNA完美分配的画面。但实际上，当细胞分离时，可能会发生许多并发症。” “我们发现，ESCRT蛋白可以进入并保护DNA桥，防止它们造成损伤。一个以重塑细胞膜而闻名的蛋白质家族，竟然也能保护DNA，这真是意想不到且令人兴奋的发现。” 这项研究得到了威康信托基金会、生物技术与生物科学研究理事会（BBSRC）以及医学研究理事会（MRC）博士培训合作项目（DTP）的支持。
