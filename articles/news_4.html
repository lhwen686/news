青少年时期更敏锐的推理能力与晚年较低的痴呆症风险相关

Sharper reasoning skills in teen years tied to lower dementia risk later in life

深度解析

本研究揭示了儿童早期认知能力与成年后期痴呆风险之间一项重要的长期关联。核心发现是，13岁时在归纳推理测试中表现更优异的个体，即便在考虑了教育背景和心血管疾病、糖尿病等共病因素后，其在几十年后罹患痴呆的风险显著降低。这表明归纳推理能力可能作为一种早期神经生理学指标，反映了大脑更深层次的健康状况和认知储备水平。 从医学机制来看，归纳推理涉及复杂的高级认知功能，其发展可能与大脑皮层的功能完整性和神经网络的效率密切相关。早期优秀的归纳推理能力或许意味着个体具备更强的神经可塑性和适应性，这有助于抵御或延缓痴呆病理生理过程的临床表现。 其行业影响深远。在临床实践上，尽管不能直接诊断，但儿童期认知评估（特别是归纳推理）有望成为早期风险分层的工具，有助于识别高风险人群，并可能为未来制定个性化的预防干预策略提供依据。在公共卫生领域，这项研究强调了儿童早期认知发展的重要性，提示社会和教育系统应更加关注和投资于儿童的认知技能培养，将其视为一项预防未来痴呆的长期公共卫生策略。这促使人们将对痴呆的关注点从晚期治疗前移至早期预防和促进脑健康。

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: Credit: RDNE Stock project from Pexels Could the way we think in our 20s offer clues about our risk of dementia decades later? Large population studies suggest that people who perform better on thinking ability tests in adolescence or young adulthood are less likely to develop dementia later in life. However, cognitive performance in early adulthood is shaped by more than thinking ability alone; it can also reflect education, life experiences and family environment. This makes it difficult to determine whether scores on cognitive tests are a reliable indicator of future dementia risk. In a recent study published in Alzheimer's Research & Therapy, researchers tested children's reasoning at age 13, before major differences in schooling and social pathways emerged. Lower cognitive scores in youth are also linked to higher risks of cardiovascular disease and diabetes in adulthood, so researchers examined whether early reasoning ability was directly linked to dementia risk, independent of these diseases. After following more than 10,000 Swedish individuals from sixth grade to 60 years later, researchers found that children who scored higher on inductive reasoning tests at age 13 went on to have a lower risk of dementia decades later. This link held up even after accounting for parents' education, the participants' own education in midlife, and major physical illnesses, including cardiovascular disease and diabetes. Childhood cognitive scores, education, sex and dementia risk. Credit: Alzheimer's Research & Therapy (2026). DOI: 10.1186/s13195-026-02179-1 Rewinding to childhood Dementia doesn't just erode memory. It can also reshape how a person thinks, communicates, feels and behaves, making everyday tasks harder to manage. The global burden of dementia is larger than ever, and it's expected to rise because of an aging population. In 2021, 57 million people were living with dementia, making it the seventh-leading cause of death worldwide and a major cause of disability and dependency among older people. Dementia doesn't develop in isolation. Heart health, diabetes, education and even how the brain develops in early childhood all play a role over a lifetime. This led scientists to wonder whether cognitive test scores from someone's youth could offer early clues about their dementia risk decades later. They did find a possible association: Higher cognitive ability in late adolescence and early adulthood lined up with lower dementia risk, but there was a catch. By that age, one's thinking ability was already shaped by years of education and experience, making cause and effect difficult to separate. Also, most existing evidence linked long-term dementia risk to a single, general intelligence score rather than specific cognitive tasks. In search of a solid link, the team tested 10,539 Swedish 13-year-olds born in 1948, who took cognitive tests in sixth grade in 1961 measuring three distinct mental abilities: inductive reasoning, verbal ability and spatial ability. The researchers then tracked these participants through adulthood, spanning six decades of follow-up into November 2025. Rather than relying on self-reported health, they linked childhood test results directly to Swedish national hospital and death registers, extracting diagnoses of dementia, cardiovascular disease, diabetes and other major conditions. Inductive reasoning and dementia risk in the expanded cohort. Credit: Alzheimer's Research & Therapy (2026). DOI: 10.1186/s13195-026-02179-1 Protective effect of a skill Children who scored higher on the inductive reasoning number-series test at age 13 grew up to have a lower risk of dementia. Each additional point on that test was linked to roughly a 2% drop in dementia risk decades later. The other two tests, verbal ability and spatial ability, didn't show the same pattern and failed to predict dementia risk over the long run on their own. This link between childhood reasoning and dementia held up even after accounting for parental education and the participants' midlife education level. What stood out was that once childhood reasoning ability was factored in, midlife education no longer showed an independent effect on dementia risk. The researchers noted that while these findings can't prove causality, they build on earlier evidence linking early-life cognitive function to later disease risk. Future long-term studies can take this further and explore whether supporting healthy cognitive development in childhood is one of the best long-term investments against dementia.
