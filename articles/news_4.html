减少超加工食品、积极运动可降低2型糖尿病风险，与基因构成无关

Reducing ultra-processed foods, being physically active can cut risk of type 2 diabetes, regardless of genetic makeup

深度解析

这项研究深入揭示了2型糖尿病（T2D）病理生理学中遗传易感性与可干预生活方式因素（如饮食和体育活动）的复杂相互作用。T2D的核心机制在于胰岛素抵抗和/或胰岛素分泌功能障碍，导致体内血糖稳态失衡。超加工食品（UPFs）因其高糖、高脂、低纤维的特点，可迅速升高血糖负荷，长期刺激胰岛素分泌，从而加剧胰岛素抵抗，并通过改变肠道菌群和诱导慢性炎症进一步损害代谢健康。相反，中度至剧烈体育活动（MVPA）能通过增加肌肉葡萄糖转运蛋白（GLUT4）的数量和活性，提升细胞对胰岛素的敏感性，促进葡萄糖摄取和利用，同时有助于体重管理和减少内脏脂肪，从根本上改善糖代谢。 该研究对医疗健康领域具有深远影响。它不仅提供了强有力的证据支持“基因并非宿命”的理念，也为临床医生和公共卫生专家提供了更坚实的科学依据，以推广生活方式干预作为T2D预防和管理的核心策略。这意味着未来可以通过结合基因风险评估与个性化营养和运动处方，为高危人群提供更精准、更有效的干预措施。同时，研究结果也呼吁食品行业向更健康的食品配方转型，减少UPFs的生产和消费，并鼓励全社会采纳更积极的生活模式，共同应对T2D的全球挑战。

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: Credit: Pixabay/CC0 Public Domain Eating a diet low in ultra-processed foods (UPFs) and exercising can cut the risk of type 2 diabetes, even in people with a strong genetic predisposition to the condition, new research suggests. The findings are being presented at the annual meeting of the European Association for the Study of Diabetes (EASD) in Milan, Italy, Sept. 28–Oct. 2. The analysis of data on tens of thousands of people in the U.K. found that those who ate fewer ultra-processed foods and did regular moderate to vigorous physical activity were up to 70% less likely to develop the condition. In those with a strong genetic predisposition, the diet-exercise combination halved the risk of type 2 diabetes. "Type 2 diabetes is caused by a combination of a person's genetic susceptibility and their lifestyle," says Wenxin Xu of City University of Hong Kong in Hong Kong, China, who led the research. "In terms of lifestyle, eating more ultra-processed food is associated with a higher risk, whereas physical activity is protective. "However, whether a lower UPF intake reduces genetic susceptibility hasn't been clear. Nor have we known whether exercising helps offset the impact of UPFs. "It is important to find this out because, unlike genetic susceptibility, diet and physical activity can be modified—they are things people can change." To find out more, Xu and colleagues at the City University of Hong Kong (Hong Kong and Dongguan) examined data on 56,964 adults in the U.K. Biobank study (average age 69.5 years, 42.3% male). Detailed questionnaires provided information on UPF intake at baseline, as defined by the Nova system. The amount of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) was calculated from activity trackers worn on the wrist for a week. MVPA is exercise that increases heart rate and breathing rate. Examples include brisk walking, cycling, hiking and walking the dog. Sports, housework and gardening jobs that require sustained physical effort are also included. Genetic susceptibility to type 2 diabetes was estimated from genetic data. During an average follow-up period of 7.8 years, 926 of the participants developed type 2 diabetes. As expected, eating more UPFs was associated with a higher risk of the condition, while MVPA was associated with a lower risk. Type 2 diabetes risk increased in line with UPF intake. For every 10% (by weight) of an individual's diet made up of UPFs, their risk of type 2 diabetes increased by 7%. Meanwhile, participants who met the World Health Organization (WHO) recommendation of at least 150 minutes of MVPA a week were 39% less likely to develop type 2 diabetes than those who exercised less. Further analysis indicated that lower UPF intake and MVPA both partially offset genetic susceptibility to type 2 diabetes. In those whose genes put them at high risk of type 2 diabetes, low UPF intake (approximately 11% or less of total food intake by weight came from ultra-processed foods) was associated with a 35% lower likelihood of the condition. Meeting the MVPA recommendation was associated with a 33% lower risk of type 2 diabetes. The researchers also found that exercise seems to reduce the impact of UPFs, even in those with a strong genetic predisposition to type 2 diabetes. Among participants with high UPF intake (approximately 33% or more of total food intake by weight came from ultra-processed foods), meeting WHO recommendations for MVPA was associated with a 31% lower risk of type 2 diabetes in those at high genetic risk, a 57% lower risk in those at intermediate genetic risk and a 58% lower risk in those at low genetic risk. The combination of lower UPF intake and MVPA was associated with even greater protection against the condition. It was linked to a 49% reduction in the risk of type 2 diabetes in those whose genes made them highly susceptible to the condition, a 70% reduction in those with intermediate genetic susceptibility and a 59% reduction in those with low genetic susceptibility. This highlights the importance of considering lifestyle changes together, rather than in isolation, the authors say. They acknowledge that the findings are observational and so cannot prove cause and effect. However, they are in line with existing advice to eat a healthier diet and stay physically active to prevent or delay the onset of type 2 diabetes. Xu adds, "Our study indicates that our genes are not our destiny. "Even among people with a high genetic susceptibility to type 2 diabetes, lower intake of ultra-processed foods and regular moderate-to-vigorous physical activity were associated with a substantially lower risk of developing the condition. "While we cannot say that type 2 diabetes can always be avoided, our results suggest that healthy lifestyle choices may help reduce the risk whatever your genetic background."
