间歇性禁食帮助小鼠受损肌肉再生

Intermittent fasting helps injured muscles regenerate in mice

深度解析

这项科奇大学的研究揭示了间歇性禁食在小鼠肌肉损伤再生中的分子机制及其潜在的双重作用。核心发现是，禁食通过激活一个特定的分子通路——EDA2R-NIK-Myomixer 轴，促进了肌肉卫星细胞的增殖与融合，从而加速受损肌纤维的修复与重建。卫星细胞作为成年肌肉干细胞，在损伤后被激活并分化为肌母细胞，进而融合形成新的肌管或修复受损肌纤维，是肌肉再生的关键环节。 该研究的深度在于指出，EDA2R-NIK-Myomixer 信号通路可能具有“双面性”。虽然在禁食诱导的肌肉修复中发挥积极作用，但该团队早前研究也发现，在癌症恶病质（cachexia）中，同一通路持续激活反而会导致肌肉分解。这提示了信号激活的持续时间与细胞环境对通路功能调控的重要性。 从行业和临床角度看，这项研究为肌肉损伤、衰老性肌少症或某些肌病（如杜氏肌营养不良症，尽管在该模型中结果复杂）的治疗提供了新的思路。如果能找到在不进行热量限制的情况下，安全、精准地短暂激活该再生通路的方法，将有望开发出不依赖饮食干预的药物或基因疗法，以促进肌肉修复和功能恢复，从而改善患者的生活质量。然而，将小鼠模型的结果转化到人类临床应用仍需大量研究，包括验证其有效性、安全性以及确定最佳的激活策略。

DOI: 10.1016/j.celrep.2026.118239 In a new study from Koç University, alternating days of fasting and feeding helped injured muscles regenerate in mice by encouraging repair cells to multiply and fuse into muscle fibers. The effect depended on a molecular pathway previously linked to muscle wasting in cancer. The findings suggest that the same signaling machinery can contribute to rebuilding or breaking down muscle, depending on the circumstances. Dilsad H. Arabaci, Melis Sucuoglu, Serkan Kir and colleagues at Koç University in Istanbul have published the work in Cell Reports. Muscle repair relies on adult stem cells called satellite cells. After an injury, they produce descendants that multiply and merge to form or repair muscle fibers. The researchers investigated how cyclical changes in food availability influence this process. More cells join the repair The researchers changed the feeding patterns of young adult male mice by alternating 24 hours without food and 24 hours of unrestricted feeding. Control mice could eat whenever they wanted. To compare recovery from similar injuries, the team injected all mice with a muscle-damaging toxin into a lower-leg muscle. The feeding schedule began before injury and continued during recovery. Ten days after injury, the fasting group's damaged muscles had regained more weight and contained larger regenerating fibers than those of the controls. Labeling newly dividing cells showed that more of their nuclei had become incorporated into muscle fibers. On feeding days, these mice ate nearly twice the control group's usual daily amount, largely compensating for their fasts. Their weight dropped during fasting and recovered during feeding. The benefit depended on an active repair process. Uninjured muscles showed no comparable increase in satellite cells or fiber size. Separate experiments also found benefits when fasting began only after injury. A signal with two sides Fasting increased the RNA messages used to make EDA2R, a protein that receives signals at the cell surface. Activating this receptor in cultured mouse muscle cells stimulated a signaling protein called NIK, which increased production of Myomixer—a protein that helps muscle-building cells fuse. Reducing Myomixer blocked the extra fusion. The mouse experiments reflected this connection. Mice lacking EDA2R, or lacking NIK specifically in satellite cells and their descendants, still regenerated muscle but lost the additional benefit associated with intermittent fasting. Yet the team's earlier research showed that the same pathway helped drive muscle wasting in tumor-bearing mice. The authors propose that brief activation in cells rebuilding muscle may have a different effect than sustained signaling in mature muscle. "We reasoned that short fasting cycles could improve myogenic fusion, while intermittent refeeding supports this activity by providing the necessary energy and nutrients," the researchers wrote. A separate experiment in a mouse model of Duchenne muscular dystrophy produced mixed results. Fasting increased signs of regeneration and improved grip strength relative to body weight at two of four weekly assessments. However, those mice ate less overall and grew more slowly, complicating interpretation of the benefits. The injury experiments did not establish whether the larger regenerating fibers produced more force, and the study does not show that fasting improves muscle repair in people. The molecular findings do suggest another treatment possibility, however: Activating the repair signals without restricting food. That would require finding a way to encourage regeneration while avoiding the muscle-wasting effects associated with prolonged activation of the same pathway.
