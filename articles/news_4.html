Autism empathy measures may be flawed, researcher argues

深度解析

这篇文章深入探讨了长期以来关于自闭症（Autism Spectrum Disorder, ASD）患者缺乏同理心的普遍误解，并对其根源进行了批判性分析。核心论点在于，这种误解并非源于ASD个体真正缺乏同理心，而是现有同理心测量工具和评估方法存在固有的局限性和神经典型偏见。文章区分了认知同理心（理解他人思想感受）和情感同理心（分享他人情绪），并指出当前问卷和行为观察往往未能捕捉到ASD个体深刻的内在同情体验，反而可能因不符合神经典型社会互动规范（如眼神交流、即时肢体回应）而错误地将其归类为缺乏同理心。这种对同理心概念及其测量方式的重新审视，对医疗健康和相关行业具有深远影响。首先，它呼吁改进ASD的临床评估工具，开发更具包容性和个体化的同理心量表，以准确反映ASD个体的真实情感能力，而非仅依赖外显行为。其次，这将促进干预策略的革新，从单纯的行为修正转向支持ASD个体以其独特方式表达和体验同理心。再者，它有助于提升社会对神经多样性的理解和接纳，减少对ASD群体的污名化。最终，这项研究将推动心理学和神经科学领域在同理心研究方法上的范式转变，确保未来的研究能够超越神经典型偏见，更全面、准确地揭示人类情感体验的丰富性。

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: Credit: Pexels There is a widespread belief that people with autism are less empathetic. In other words, that they are less able to put themselves in someone else's shoes. But is that actually true? Autism researcher Dr. Lisa Krijnen is investigating this question. The research is published in the journal Autism. Where does this idea come from? Both research and popular opinion have long portrayed people with autism as being less empathetic. But why is this the case? Krijnen explains, "Partly, it may stem from the way empathy has been measured in previous studies. It is often assessed using questionnaires in which people are asked to evaluate for themselves how well they understand or relate to what someone else is thinking or feeling. Autistic people regularly report finding these kinds of questions difficult to answer." How these questionnaires work What makes these questionnaires so challenging? "They typically contain a variety of questions about empathy," says Krijnen. "For example, you might be asked whether you can predict how someone else is feeling. But is that really possible? Can anyone look into a crystal ball and know exactly how another person feels? And what context should you imagine when answering? Is it someone you know well or a complete stranger? Do you know what is happening in their life, or not?" According to Krijnen, these are the kinds of thought processes autistic people often describe when completing such questionnaires. "It raises an important question about how this affects their responses. There is a good chance someone will answer that they cannot really predict how another person feels. As a result, the questionnaire may categorize them as less empathetic. But does that genuinely tell us anything about whether a person is empathetic?" The experiences of people with autism In her research, Krijnen speaks directly with autistic individuals and asks them how they interpret the statements used in these questionnaires. These conversations reveal how people arrive at their answers, rather than simply showing the answers themselves. According to Krijnen, the findings do not suggest that autistic people are necessarily less empathetic. "Some participants describe feeling deep compassion for others, so intensely that it can become overwhelming. People also explain that they sometimes need time to process another person's experiences and consider how best to respond. To an outsider, it may appear as though they do not care because they do not immediately put an arm around someone. A questionnaire might ask whether other people consider you insensitive, and in that case the answer can easily lean toward 'yes."' What if empathy isn't visible? Putting an arm around someone is often seen as a typical indicator of empathy. But is behavior really what determines whether a person is empathetic? "In principle, empathy should not be defined by behavior alone," says the autism researcher. "However, behavior is what is visible to other people. That helps explain how the perception can arise that autistic people are less empathetic. This is precisely why it is important for researchers to critically examine the ways empathy is measured and to challenge existing assumptions." Empathy explained This article is about empathy. But what do we actually mean when we talk about it? Krijnen: "Empathy is not always clearly defined. Does it mean understanding what other people think, do and feel? Or is it mainly about sharing in other people's emotions? Or does it refer to how you respond, for example when someone sitting next to you starts to cry?" "The first two aspects are generally regarded as forms of empathy: understanding another person's thoughts and feelings is known as cognitive empathy, while sharing their emotions is referred to as affective empathy. How someone responds, in other words their social skills and behavior, is sometimes included as part of empathy and sometimes considered a separate concept." How should we measure empathy? Krijnen also asked participants how they believed empathy should be assessed in order to measure it in a reliable and inclusive way. "Many of them felt that the focus should not be on behavior but on what a person experiences internally. That is the essence of empathy: sharing in and understanding someone else's feelings and thoughts. When we judge empathy based solely on behavior, we risk making a great deal of assumptions. Often, we assess empathy according to behaviors that align with what society considers 'normal' or 'appropriate."' A neurotypical view of empathy But what exactly counts as "normal" or "appropriate"? These standards are often shaped by the expectations of the average neurotypical person. "For example, when someone tells you a personal story, you are generally expected to maintain eye contact to show that you are listening. If you frequently look away, there is a common assumption that you are not paying attention, which may lead others to believe you are less engaged or less empathetic." "But what if you can focus better on what is being said when you are not looking directly at the speaker? This is something autistic people regularly describe. In that situation, looking away can actually be a sign of engagement. It all depends on the perspective from which someone's behavior is viewed." Ongoing research Krijnen and her colleagues are continuing their research. Their ultimate goal? "Together with autistic people, we are working toward the development of a questionnaire that measures empathy without relying on a neurotypical perspective. This will help us gain a better understanding of empathy and prevent misunderstandings about empathy and autism." Publication details Riley Buijsman et al, 'Autistic person' or 'person with autism'?
