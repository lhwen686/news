混合束测量方法有助于使离子肿瘤治疗更精准

Mixed beam measurement method could help make ion cancer therapy more precise

深度解析

这篇新闻报道详细阐述了离子束治疗领域的一项突破性进展——混合离子束技术，及其核心的非侵入性监测方法。传统的离子束治疗，例如质子或碳离子治疗，通过高能粒子精确靶向并摧毁肿瘤细胞，相较于X射线放疗能更好地保护周围健康组织。然而，其临床应用面临一大挑战：器官运动等因素导致的射程不确定性，需要设置额外的安全边际来确保肿瘤区域的完整覆盖，这可能牺牲部分健康组织。 混合离子束技术（由碳离子和氦离子组成）旨在解决这一痛点。其核心医学机制是利用碳离子进行肿瘤消融，同时以氦离子作为体内剂量学验证的探针。技术原理在于两种离子在加速器中因荷质比接近而可同步加速并遵循相似轨迹，但在生物组织中，它们与物质的相互作用和穿透深度显著不同。氦离子射程更长，可穿透肿瘤后被检测，其剩余能量能反演出碳离子的实际射程。更关键的技术创新在于，研究团队利用碳核与氦核之间微小的质量差异（约0.065%）导致加速器射频稳定所需的精细调整，从而非侵入性地实时监测混合束中两种离子的精确比例。 这项技术具有深远的行业影响。它有望大幅提升离子束治疗的精度，通过实时射程验证来缩小安全边际，最大程度地保护肿瘤附近敏感器官的健康组织，降低治疗毒性。这不仅能改善患者预后和生活质量，也可能扩大离子束治疗的适用范围，使其能应用于目前因器官邻近度高风险而受限的肿瘤类型，推动精准放疗进入新的维度。

This article has been reviewed according to Science X's editorial process and policies . Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: A mixed beam of carbon and helium ions. Credit: TU Wien In ion beam therapy, atomic nuclei are accelerated to high energies in a particle accelerator, for example, a synchrotron, and precisely directed at a tumor. There, they damage cancer cells and destroy tumor tissue. This technique is now used at several specialized medical facilities around the world, including MedAustron in Wiener Neustadt, Austria. A new approach currently being investigated involves so-called "mixed beams," which consist of two different ion species: Carbon nuclei destroy the tumor cells, while helium nuclei simultaneously help monitor the patient's anatomy during irradiation. To make this possible, however, the precise ratio of carbon to helium in the beam must be known. But how can an ion beam be measured while it is being used for treatment? TU Wien and MedAustron have now found a solution—an important step on the path toward cancer treatment using mixed ion beams. The paper is published in the journal Physical Review Accelerators and Beams. Irradiating as little as possible Despite the high precision of ion beam therapy, uncertainties remain. Organ motion, for example, can shift the location at which the beam deposits most of its energy—also called the "range" of the beam—by a few millimeters. To ensure that the entire tumor is treated despite these uncertainties, a small safety margin around the tumor is irradiated as well. The smaller this margin can be made, the more precise the therapy becomes and the better sensitive organs nearby can be spared. Matthias Kausel (MedAustron/TU Wien) and Elisabeth Renner (TU Wien). Credit: TU Wien To minimize side effects, it would therefore be ideal to monitor the range of the beam as precisely as possible during treatment. This is where the concept of mixed beams comes in: Carbon and helium ions are combined in a single beam. All electrons are removed, leaving carbon nuclei with exactly three times the electric charge of helium nuclei and almost exactly three times their mass. "Because their charge-to-mass ratios are almost identical, the two ion species also follow almost exactly the same trajectory in the particle accelerator, allowing us to accelerate them simultaneously to high energies and direct them toward the patient," explains Elisabeth Renner. In tissue, however, a crucial difference emerges: Helium ions can travel much farther through the body—approximately three times as far. A mixed beam can therefore be sent through the body in such a way that the carbon ions are used to destroy the tumor tissue, while the helium ions pass through the body. The emerging helium ions can then be measured. From the residual energy of the helium ions, one can reconstruct how far the carbon ions have actually penetrated into the tissue and whether the irradiation is proceeding as planned. This could allow researchers to reduce the safety margins and hence spare even more healthy tissue, particularly in the case of tumors located very close to sensitive organs. "For this method to work, however, we need to know and control very precisely how large the carbon and helium fractions in the beam are," says Renner. And that is a major challenge: Either the beam is measured, or it is used for irradiation. Doing both at the same time is difficult. How to measure a beam without stopping it The research team at TU Wien and MedAustron has now found a solution. Strictly speaking, a carbon nucleus does not have exactly three times the mass of a helium nucleus, but about 0.065% less. This tiny difference means that carbon and helium are forced onto slightly different trajectories inside the particle accelerator. "In the accelerator, we continuously monitor whether the center of the particle beam remains on its intended trajectory," explains Renner. "If it deviates, the accelerator's radio frequency is adjusted ever so slightly. Because helium and carbon nuclei behave slightly differently due to their tiny mass difference, the required frequency correction depends on the mixing ratio." By precisely monitoring the frequency needed to stabilize the beam, the carbon-to-helium ratio can therefore be determined. This was calculated at TU Wien and tested in collaboration with MedAustron—and the results show excellent agreement. "We were able to demonstrate that this method allows us to determine the helium and carbon fractions in the beam without affecting or interrupting the beam itself. This means that during experiments, and possibly also during treatments in the future, we can check whether the helium fraction in the beam corresponds to the desired value. Only if that is the case should the beam be cleared for irradiation." The results have now been published and are already attracting international attention: On Oct. 8 and 9, 2026, researchers from a range of international particle accelerator facilities came to TU Wien for the "1st Workshop on Mixed Ion Beam Delivery" to discuss current developments and challenges in the delivery and use of mixed ion beams. More information Elisabeth Renner et al., Noninvasive ion fraction quantification of dual-species beams in synchrotrons, Physical Review Accelerators and Beams (2026).
