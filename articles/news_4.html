First leishmaniasis vaccine enters phase one trial as disease spreads to US

A phase 1 clinical trial testing the safety of a leishmaniasis vaccine is set to begin in the coming months—the first vaccine created to protect against the disfiguring skin disease common in tropical regions of the world and gaining ground in the United States.

The researcher who led development of the vaccine is the senior author of a New England Journal of Medicine review article published last week describing the difficulties of treating most forms of the disease and reporting that the live vaccine, found to be safe in animals, is expected to be tested in humans later this year.

Up to 1 million new cases of cutaneous leishmaniasis, or CL, caused by many species of Leishmania and transmitted through the bite of infected sand flies, are diagnosed worldwide each year. At any given time, approximately 6 million people are infected—yet up to 1 billion people in 99 territories and countries where leishmaniasis is endemic, including the U.S., are at risk of contracting the disease, the authors wrote.

Lead researcher Abhay Satoskar, a professor of pathology at The Ohio State University College of Medicine, has been working on development of a leishmaniasis vaccine for more than 20 years. In 2020, he and colleagues reported strong results in mice with a vaccine developed using CRISPR gene-editing technology to mutate the parasite that causes the skin disease. Late last year, the vaccine cleared a key U.S. Food and Drug Administration hurdle: approval of an investigational new drug, or IND.

"This means the FDA has seen the product and our preclinical data and has deemed that this product can be tested in people now," Satoskar said. "It's a big milestone in terms of product development, especially when the product was conceived in academia."

"This helps everywhere in the world because once the U.S. FDA gives its approval, it means other regulators know that it has gone through a very thorough review."

To develop the vaccine, the researchers used CRISPR technology to edit the genome of Leishmania major by deleting centrin, the gene for a protein that supports the parasite's physical structure.

The team applied the new technology to the century-old Middle Eastern practice of leishmanization—deliberately introducing the live parasite to the skin to create a small infection that, once healed, leads to lifelong immunity against further disease.

These parasites cause infection by hijacking immune cells and using those host cells to replicate indefinitely. Studies of the vaccine in mice showed that the mutant parasite lacking centrin could still find its way into cells and make copies of itself, but for only a limited amount of time and not at a pace that leads to clinical disease. Immunization with the mutated parasites induced immunity against vector-transmitted infections.

"Live vaccines like that are the best vaccines, but there's a potential risk of causing serious disease in some people," said Satoskar, also a professor of microbiology at Ohio State.

"We refined the concept using modern technology, making a parasite that does not cause clinical disease but allows for induction of immunity."

Satoskar and his collaborators will be responsible for conducting the initial trials in Kenya and Brazil, two countries in which leishmaniasis is endemic and among the countries targeted for eventual vaccine deployment. A site in Cincinnati may also be added to the trial. Between about 75 and 100 people will participate.

This initial trial is focused on testing the safety of the vaccine in healthy people, but the team will also collect blood samples to investigate whether the vaccine provokes an immune response. Testing in endemic regions is an important step in gauging whether people who may have already been exposed to the parasite, and therefore have antibodies in their blood, experience an allergic reaction or other adverse effect.

In a parallel project, the team is developing a leishmaniasis skin test similar to a tuberculosis skin test.

"Testing positive with the skin test means that a person has been exposed to the disease and is now immune to the disease," Satoskar said. "It is a test for immunosurveillance but also for immunogenicity of our vaccine. Without this kind of test, you cannot do surveillance. You cannot know the level of immunity in the community."

After interim analysis of the phase 1 results, the team is aiming to conduct a phase 2 trial in at least 3,000 people with the purpose of understanding the effectiveness of the vaccine and any side effects.

"An effective prophylactic vaccine is indispensable for elimination or eradication of leishmaniasis," Satoskar said. "This is true for almost all infectious diseases."

A vaccine would also provide an affordable way to prevent a disease that can lead to disfigurement, disability, social stigma and poverty, he said. Researchers estimate that a vaccine would likely cost less than $5, compared with the $100 to $200 cost for treatment in the hardest-hit countries—treatment that requires weeks of daily drug injections with unpleasant side effects, leading to poor patient compliance that allows parasites to develop resistance to the drugs.

A much more severe form of the disease, visceral leishmaniasis, affects organs and is fatal if left untreated. While the L. major vaccine may also protect against the visceral form, Satoskar and colleagues have used the CRISPR technique to work on a vaccine with the Leishmania donovani parasite that causes visceral leishmaniasis.

The phase 1 trial and leishmaniasis skin test production are supported by $10 million in grants from the Japan-based Global Health Innovative Technology Fund, which also supported the vaccine's development along with the Wellcome Trust UK.

Additional partners on the trial include Nagasaki University in Japan; Gennova Biopharmaceuticals Ltd., which manufactures the vaccine; Universidade Federal de Minas Gerais in Brazil; HJF Medical Research International in Kenya; the FDA; the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases; and Johns Hopkins University.

Co-authors of the review article were Naomi Aronson of the Uniformed Services University of the Health Sciences in Maryland and Ahmed Musa of the University of Khartoum in Sudan.

一项旨在测试利什曼病疫苗安全性的I期临床试验预计将在未来几个月内启动——这是首款旨在预防这种在全球热带地区常见且在美国日益增多的毁容性皮肤病的疫苗。

领导该疫苗开发的科学家是上周发表在《新英格兰医学杂志》上的一篇综述文章的资深作者。该文章描述了治疗大多数形式利什曼病的困难，并报告称，这种在动物身上被证实安全的减毒活疫苗预计将于今年晚些年在人体进行测试。

全球每年诊断出的皮肤利什曼病（cutaneous leishmaniasis，简称CL）新病例多达100万例，该病由多种利什曼原虫引起，并通过受感染沙蝇的叮咬传播。任何时候约有600万人感染此病——然而，作者写道，在包括美国在内的99个利什曼病地方性流行地区和国家中，多达10亿人面临感染风险。

首席研究员阿布海·萨托斯卡（Abhay Satoskar）是俄亥俄州立大学医学院病理学教授，他从事利什曼病疫苗的开发工作已超过20年。2020年，他及其同事报告称，利用CRISPR基因编辑技术对导致皮肤病的寄生虫进行突变，所开发的疫苗在小鼠身上取得了良好效果。去年底，该疫苗通过了美国食品药品监督管理局（FDA）的一个关键障碍：获得了试验性新药（Investigational New Drug，简称IND）批准。

“这意味着FDA已经审查了该产品和我们的临床前数据，并认为该产品现在可以在人体上进行测试了，”萨托斯卡说。“就产品开发而言，这是一个重要的里程碑，尤其是在产品最初构思于学术界的情况下。”

“这有助于世界各地，因为一旦美国FDA批准，就意味着其他监管机构知道它已经经过了非常彻底的审查。”

为了开发这种疫苗，研究人员利用CRISPR技术编辑了大利什曼原虫的基因组，删除了中心粒蛋白（centrin）基因，该基因编码一种支持寄生虫物理结构的蛋白质。

该团队将这项新技术应用于已有百年历史的中东地区“利什曼化”实践——即故意将活体寄生虫引入皮肤以制造轻微感染，一旦愈合，就能对未来的疾病产生终身免疫力。

这些寄生虫通过劫持免疫细胞并利用这些宿主细胞无限复制来引起感染。对该疫苗在小鼠中的研究表明，缺乏中心粒蛋白的突变寄生虫仍然可以进入细胞并进行自我复制，但其复制时间有限，且复制速度不足以导致临床疾病。用这种突变寄生虫进行免疫接种可诱导对媒介传播感染的免疫力。

“像这样的活疫苗是最好的疫苗，但有些人可能会有引起严重疾病的潜在风险，”萨托斯卡说，他同时也是俄亥俄州立大学微生物学教授。

“我们利用现代技术改进了这一概念，制造出一种不会引起临床疾病但能诱导免疫力的寄生虫。”

萨托斯卡及其合作者将负责在肯尼亚和巴西开展初步试验，这两个国家是利什曼病的地方性流行区，也是最终疫苗部署的目标国家之一。辛辛那提的一个地点也可能被加入到试验中。约有75至100人将参与试验。

这项初步试验主要关注测试疫苗在健康人群中的安全性，但该团队也将采集血液样本，以调查疫苗是否会引发免疫反应。在地方性流行区进行测试是衡量那些可能已经接触过寄生虫，因此血液中存在抗体的人是否会经历过敏反应或其他不良反应的重要一步。

在一个并行项目中，该团队正在开发一种类似于结核病皮肤测试的利什曼病皮肤测试。

“皮肤测试结果呈阳性意味着该人曾接触过这种疾病，现在对该疾病具有免疫力，”萨托斯卡说。“这既是一种免疫监测测试，也是我们疫苗免疫原性的测试。没有这种测试，就无法进行监测。就无法了解社区中的免疫水平。”

在对I期结果进行中期分析后，该团队的目标是在至少3000人中进行II期试验，旨在了解疫苗的有效性和任何副作用。

“一种有效的预防性疫苗对于消除或根除利什曼病是不可或缺的，”萨托斯卡说。“这几乎适用于所有传染病。”

他说，疫苗还将提供一种经济实惠的方式来预防这种可能导致毁容、残疾、社会污名和贫困的疾病。研究人员估计，疫苗的成本可能低于5美元，而受灾最严重的国家的治疗费用为100至200美元——这种治疗需要数周的每日药物注射，并伴有不愉快的副作用，导致患者依从性差，从而使寄生虫对药物产生耐药性。

一种更为严重的疾病形式是内脏利什曼病，它影响内脏器官，如不治疗则会致命。虽然大利什曼原虫疫苗也可能预防内脏型，但萨托斯卡及其同事已利用CRISPR技术研究一种针对杜氏利什曼原虫的疫苗，该原虫是引起内脏利什曼病的原因。

I期试验和利什曼病皮肤测试的生产得到了日本全球健康创新技术基金（Global Health Innovative Technology Fund）1000万美元的资助，该基金还与英国维康信托基金（Wellcome Trust UK）共同支持了疫苗的开发。

该试验的其他合作伙伴包括日本长崎大学、生产该疫苗的Gennova生物制药有限公司、巴西米纳斯吉拉斯联邦大学、肯尼亚HJF医学研究国际、FDA、美国国家过敏和传染病研究所；以及约翰霍普金斯大学。
