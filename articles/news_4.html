英文原文

Original
Cambridge scientists have solved the mystery of why both stimulating and blocking a particular 'switch' in the brain can help people lose weight – findings which could help boost the effectiveness of obesity drugs. Published today in Nature Metabolism, the study in mice shows that the answer lies in where the switch is located: stimulating the switch in the brainstem suppresses appetite, while the same effect can be achieved by blocking the switch in the hypothalamus. More than a billion people worldwide are living with obesity, which increases the risk of diseases such as 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease and cancer. Weight loss can help mitigate these complications, but losing weight through diet and exercise alone can prove challenging. In the past few years, a new generation of weight loss drugs has emerged that target particular receptors in the brain, reducing appetite and leading to weight loss, as well as helping control blood sugar levels. Several of these, such as Wegovy and Ozempic, work by stimulating a protein 'switch' known as the glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor (GLP-1R). Other weight loss drugs act on both this receptor and a second one, the glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide receptor (GIPR). However, some of these drugs, such as Mounjaro and Zepbound, stimulate GIPR, while others, such as MariTide, block it. Why these opposite actions have the same result has puzzled scientists. Now, researchers at the Institute of Metabolic Science, University of Cambridge, have used mice to solve the puzzle, showing that the two different types of GIPR drugs act on distinct regions of the brain – but also that they can boost weight loss when combined with certain GLP-1-based weight-loss drugs. The team used genetically engineered mice and selectively removed GIPR from different parts of the brain to see which regions were responsible for the effects of the obesity drugs. One group of mice lacked GIPR in the brainstem – the area at the base of the brain, just above the spinal cord, involved in appetite and nausea. A second group lacked GIPR in the hypothalamus, a major centre controlling hunger and body weight. The third, control group were normal, unmodified mice. The researchers then treated these mice with various combinations of a GIPR agonist (which activates the receptor), a GIPR antagonist (which blocks the receptor) and a GLP-1 drug, and measured food intake, body weight, fat mass, glucose control and brain activity. By comparing the responses of normal mice with mice lacking GIPR in different brain areas, they showed that GIPR agonists act on the brainstem to suppress appetite and reduce weight. They then showed that GIPR antagonists help weight loss by acting on this receptor, but in the hypothalamus, where they release a 'brake' that otherwise limits the brainstem's ability to respond to signals telling us we are full. Blocking GIPR also appeared to boost the effect of emerging new drugs targeting the amylin receptor, suggesting that GIPR antagonists could potentially be used to strengthen several types of anti-obesity medicines. The findings explain why drugs such as MariTide, currently in phase 3 clinical trials, which combines GIPR antagonism with GLP-1 receptor agonism, are effective, and suggests how to design even better combination therapies. Understanding which brain circuits respond to these medications – and how they do so – could help us design better drugs that produce more weight loss with fewer side effects, and which might work in combination with other obesity medicines to even greater effect. Our work also strengthens the idea that the brain is central to obesity treatment. Obesity drugs are not acting simply on the gut or pancreas. Instead, they have important effects on specific, identifiable brain circuits that regulate appetite and food intake." Dr. Jo Lewis, study's first author, Institute of Metabolic Science, University of Cambridge The research was funded by the Medical Research Council and Wellcome.

AI Translation
剑桥大学科学家已经解开了为什么同时刺激和阻断大脑中某个特定“开关”都能帮助人们减肥的谜团——这些发现有助于提升肥胖症药物的疗效。 这项今天发表在《自然·代谢》上的小鼠研究表明，答案在于该开关的定位：刺激脑干中的此开关可抑制食欲，而阻断下丘脑中的此开关也能达到相同的效果。 全球有超过十亿人罹患肥胖症，这会增加患2型糖尿病、心血管疾病和癌症等疾病的风险。减肥有助于减轻这些并发症，但单纯依靠饮食和运动来减肥可能具有挑战性。 在过去几年中，新一代减肥药物应运而生，它们靶向大脑中的特定受体，从而降低食欲、导致体重减轻，并有助于控制血糖水平。其中一些药物，如Wegovy和Ozempic，通过刺激一种名为胰高糖素样肽-1受体（GLP-1R）的蛋白质“开关”发挥作用。 其他减肥药物则同时作用于此受体和第二个受体——葡萄糖依赖性促胰岛素多肽受体（GIPR）。然而，这些药物中，有些（如Mounjaro和Zepbound）会刺激GIPR，而另一些（如MariTide）则会阻断它。为什么这些相反的作用却能产生相同的结果，一直困扰着科学家们。 现在，剑桥大学代谢科学研究所的研究人员利用小鼠解开了这个谜团，他们发现两种不同类型的GIPR药物作用于大脑的不同区域——而且当它们与某些基于GLP-1的减肥药物联合使用时，还能进一步促进体重减轻。 该团队使用了基因工程小鼠，并选择性地从大脑不同部位移除GIPR，以观察哪些区域对肥胖症药物的效果负责。一组小鼠的脑干中缺少GIPR——脑干是位于大脑底部、脊髓正上方，参与食欲和恶心控制的区域。第二组小鼠的下丘脑中缺少GIPR，下丘脑是一个控制饥饿和体重的关键中枢。第三组对照组是正常、未经基因改造的小鼠。 研究人员随后用GIPR激动剂（激活受体）、GIPR拮抗剂（阻断受体）和GLP-1药物的各种组合来治疗这些小鼠，并测量了它们的食物摄入量、体重、脂肪量、血糖控制和大脑活动。 通过比较正常小鼠与大脑不同区域缺少GIPR小鼠的反应，他们发现GIPR激动剂作用于脑干，从而抑制食欲并减轻体重。 随后他们发现，GIPR拮抗剂通过作用于GIPR受体，但在下丘脑中，它们能够释放一个“刹车”，否则这个刹车会限制脑干响应饱腹信号的能力，从而帮助减肥。阻断GIPR似乎还能增强靶向胰淀粉样肽受体的新兴药物的效果，这表明GIPR拮抗剂有可能用于增强多种抗肥胖药物的作用。 这些发现解释了为何目前处于3期临床试验阶段的MariTide等药物（结合了GIPR拮抗作用与GLP-1受体激动作用）能够有效，并为如何设计出更优异的联合疗法提供了思路。 “了解哪些大脑回路对这些药物有反应——以及它们如何反应——可以帮助我们设计出更有效的药物，实现更大的体重减轻，同时减少副作用，并且这些药物可能与其他抗肥胖药物联合使用以产生更显著的效果。我们的工作也进一步强化了大脑在肥胖治疗中处于核心地位的观点。肥胖症药物并非仅仅作用于肠道或胰腺。相反，它们对调节食欲和食物摄入量的特定、可识别的大脑回路产生重要影响。”——剑桥大学代谢科学研究所，该研究的第一作者Jo Lewis博士。 这项研究由医学研究委员会和威康信托基金会资助。
