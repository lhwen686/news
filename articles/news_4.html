英文原文

Original
Australians may trust official nutrition advice, but how well do younger adults understand and follow the country’s dietary fat recommendations? Study: Self-Reported Awareness and Adherence to Recommendations for Dietary Fat Amongst Australian Adults Aged 18–45 Years: Potential Relevance to Cardiovascular Health. Image Credit: Iryna Inshyna / Shutterstock.com In a recent study published in Nutrients , researchers evaluated self-reported awareness, beliefs, and dietary adherence to Australia's national dietary fat recommendations among 132 Australian adults aged 18–45 years. The findings suggest that current dietary guidance may not reach some adults effectively, pointing to a need for stronger digital communication that translates nutrient targets into practical, food-based guidance. Why Australia's dietary fat guidance matters Decades of public health records and clinical reports identify cardiovascular disease ( CVD ) as one of Australia's leading causes of morbidity and mortality. Since dietary fat quality and quantity are modifiable factors associated with cardiovascular health, the Australian government's National Health and Medical Research Council ( NHMRC ) has prescribed Nutrient Reference Values ( NRVs ) alongside the Australian Dietary Guidelines ( ADGs ) to help improve cardiovascular health at the population level and mitigate the country's burden of CVD. NRVs and ADGs currently recommend that Australian adults limit total fat intake to less than 30% of dietary energy and reduce saturated fatty acid ( SFA ) intake to less than 10%. These recommendations are based on cardiovascular research showing that replacing just 5% of energy from saturated fats with polyunsaturated fatty acids ( PUFAs ) is associated with a 25% lower risk of coronary heart disease ( CHD ). Recent national nutrition data indicate that Australian adults obtain about 12.5% of their total energy from saturated fat, exceeding the recommended 10% limit and suggesting a disconnect between NRV and ADG recommendations and population-scale dietary choices. Contemporary evidence describing awareness, beliefs, and adherence to these recommendations among Australian adults remains limited, particularly among younger adults. How researchers measured awareness and adherence The present study aimed to address this knowledge gap and inform future adherence-focused nutrition communication strategies by systematically evaluating awareness, comprehension, and adherence to fat recommendations among Australian adults aged 18–45 years. The sample comprised 132 eligible respondents recruited through the online survey platform Qualtrics between October and November 2023. The survey collected participant-provided data across several domains: sociodemographic, anthropometric, and general health characteristics; physical activity; awareness, beliefs, and practices surrounding dietary fats; supplement use; and participants' primary dietary information sources. Researchers used Fisher's exact tests to examine associations between selected participant characteristics and dietary fat awareness, beliefs, and practices. Because these analyses were exploratory and hypothesis-generating, they did not adjust for multiple testing. Awareness was low, even when trust remained high The respondent cohort was predominantly female (89.2%), with about 71% holding a bachelor's degree or higher, 90.1% employed, and 93.1% non-smoking. Among respondents, 49% somewhat or strongly agreed that they trusted the information provided in the NRVs and ADG. In comparison, 53% somewhat or strongly disagreed that they were aware of the exact amount of fat recommended in the NRVs. Another 21% of the study cohort reported being unsure whether they knew the exact recommendation. Only 12% of the sampled cohort reported being somewhat or strongly aware of the exact dietary fat recommendations in the NRVs. Adherence was also low. About 48% of the study cohort somewhat or strongly disagreed that they always followed the exact NRV recommendation, and only 7% reported somewhat or strongly agreeing that they did so. For the Eat for Health ADG, 12% reported following the specific fat recommendations, while 47% disagreed that they did so. Participants also reported problems accessing and understanding the guidance. Thirty-seven percent of respondents disagreed that the guidelines were easy to access, while 24% were unsure about their accessibility. Twenty-two percent disagreed that the guidelines were easy to understand, whereas 32% were unsure about their comprehensibility. Participants generally understood some broad distinctions between fat types. About 72% considered saturated fat detrimental to health, while 75% considered omega-3 PUFAs beneficial. Uncertainty was greater around monounsaturated fats, with 31% of respondents unsure how to classify them. Social media emerged as the most commonly reported information source (20%), exceeding both scientific publications and doctor or GP consultations at 16% and 14%, respectively. Information source was associated with self-reported NRV adherence and perceptions of saturated fat, though the researchers could not determine the direction of these associations. These observations may not represent the broader Australian population, as the sample was small, predominantly female, and highly educated. Furthermore, recruitment primarily through social media may have favored people already engaged with online platforms. The questionnaire was pilot-tested but not formally validated, and participants self-reported awareness and dietary behaviors. Clearer digital guidance could help close the gap The findings show low self-reported awareness and adherence to dietary fat recommendations in this sample of Australian adults aged 18–45 years. Trust in official dietary guidance was reasonably strong among respondents; however, awareness, accessibility, and practical application of the recommendations remained limited. Social media's prominence as a nutrition information source also points to an opportunity for health professionals, researchers, and public health agencies to provide evidence-based content through digital platforms. Translating complex nutrient targets into clear, food-based dietary advice may improve understanding and adherence and support dietary behaviors linked to cardiovascular and overall health.
