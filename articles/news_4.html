新研究显示，运动量更大的青少年日后报告的睡眠问题更少

More active teens report fewer sleep problems down the line, new study reveals

深度解析

这项研究采用的纵向设计是其核心亮点，它超越了横断面研究的局限性，通过多时间点追踪有效揭示了身体活动与青少年失眠症状之间明确的单向因果方向性：即运动能够改善睡眠，而非睡眠问题反过来导致运动减少。这种方法学严谨性对于建立循证医学证据至关重要。研究进一步阐明，运动对睡眠的益处部分通过缓解适应不良的认知情绪调节机制（如反刍思维和灾难化思维）实现。这些认知扭曲是焦虑和抑郁的核心特征，通过影响大脑皮层激活和自主神经系统平衡，直接干扰正常的睡眠启动和维持。例如，持续的负性思维会激活下丘脑-垂体-肾上腺（HPA）轴，导致皮质醇水平升高，从而抑制褪黑素分泌，破坏昼夜节律。此外，研究发现性别差异对这种中介作用有调节效应，提示临床干预时需考虑个体化策略。这些发现对公共卫生和临床实践具有深远影响，支持将规律性身体活动纳入青少年健康促进和失眠干预的指南。在学校环境中推广体育运动，并结合针对认知情绪调节的心理健康教育，将构成一套更为全面的预防和治疗青少年睡眠障碍的策略。

Credit: Pexels. Pulling an all-nighter before a test or assignment deadline is almost a teenage rite of passage, but scientists are concerned that these exceptions are becoming a regular habit. Insomnia symptoms during adolescence are linked to a range of adverse health and developmental outcomes. Studies have found that physical activity can be a good antidote to sleep issues, but can it help ease adolescent insomnia? A recent study published in Sleep Health investigated this question by tracking 1,988 Chinese middle school students, around 13 to 14 years old, across three time points six months apart. Researchers looked at how changes in physical activity and insomnia symptoms influenced each other over time. Teenagers who were more active reported fewer insomnia symptoms six months later. Poor sleep, on the other hand, did not predict a drop in activity once differences between individual students were factored in. In addition to helping with sleep, exercise also led to a drop in unhelpful mental habits like rumination, which means dwelling on negative feelings, and catastrophizing, which means expecting something worse to happen. The sleep debt When we sleep at night, our bodies get a chance to go into mechanic mode, repairing themselves, balancing various chemical systems, consolidating memories and supporting immune function. During the teenage years, proper sleep is crucial for brain development, physical strength and even emotional well-being. Unfortunately, sleep disturbances among teenagers are on the rise, with prevalence estimates ranging from 14% to 68% worldwide and sitting around 26% in China. The link between exercise and sleep seemed fairly clear at first: More physically active teens have fewer issues falling asleep and fewer disturbances. This, however, only holds true for studies looking at a single point in time. Longitudinal studies haven't settled the question yet. Some studies show that exercise reduces sleep problems, while others show that poor sleep reduces exercise, with no effect in the opposite direction. In this study, researchers wanted to clarify whether activity predicts later sleep problems, sleep problems predict later activity, or both. Most existing data also come from Western adults or college samples, so they wanted to understand these connections among middle school students in China, where academic demands and sleep deprivation are particularly high. The team enrolled participants from five middle schools in Hubei, China. The students had an average age of 13.79 years, and 54.5% were boys. The researchers followed a three-wave longitudinal design, tracking students at three separate time points spaced six months apart and assessing their physical activity, negative overthinking and insomnia symptoms. Does it go both ways? After three visits, the researchers found that students who were more physically active tended to report fewer insomnia symptoms six months later, although the link was modest. At first, the statistics pointed both ways, suggesting that more exercise could mean better sleep, while poor sleep could also lead to less exercise. That changed with a more advanced model that accounted for stable differences between individuals. Only one direction remained—exercise led to better sleep, not the reverse. Cross-lagged panel model for physical activity, maladaptive cognitive emotion regulation, insomnia symptoms (missing data handled via FIML approach. Credit: Sleep Health (2026). DOI: 10.1016/j.sleh.2026.07.007 Exercise was tied to less negative overthinking, which was itself associated with fewer insomnia symptoms later. More physical activity at the start of the study predicted significantly less negative overthinking six months later in girls, but not boys. Boys showed a different connection: Higher negative overthinking at six months predicted worse insomnia symptoms later, an association that did not appear in girls. Based on these findings, the researchers propose that exercise may help protect teenage sleep in part by easing unhelpful thought habits, such as dwelling on worries or expecting the worst. The effect, however, was modest, so they urge caution in interpreting the results.
