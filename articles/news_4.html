Researchers show how leukemia cells 'hide' from treatment

深度解析

这项研究深入揭示了慢性淋巴细胞白血病（CLL）细胞的耐药机制，对临床实践和药物研发具有深远影响。核心机制在于CLL细胞并非仅依赖单一的B细胞受体（BCR）信号通路生存。当前广泛使用的布鲁顿酪氨酸激酶抑制剂（BTKi）通过阻断BCR通路，迫使癌细胞从淋巴结等“避难所”进入血流，从而易于被清除。然而，研究发现部分CLL细胞具备“备用”生存机制：当BCR通路被阻断时，它们能激活Toll样受体9（TLR9）通路，继续逃避治疗，导致耐药和疾病复发。 这一发现的行业影响是多方面的。首先，它为20-30%原因不明的CLL患者耐药问题提供了合理解释，填补了认知空白。其次，它强烈支持开发更个性化的治疗策略，即在BTKi治疗无效时，通过检测TLR9通路活性来指导后续治疗，例如联合靶向TLR9的药物。这不仅能提高治疗有效率，还能减少不必要的副作用和住院时间。最后，计算机模型的应用展示了其在预测患者反应和加速新药研发方面的巨大潜力，预示着未来精准医疗将更多地依赖多学科交叉研究。

DOI: 10.1182/blood.2025032914 In a first-of-its-kind study collaboration, new research has uncovered that chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) cells may switch between different survival signals, helping explain why treatments work well for some patients but stop working for others. These findings could ultimately help pave the way for more personalized treatment. Simon Mitchell, a professor at the University of Sussex and a Leukemia UK John Goldman Fellow Follow-up Fund recipient, developed sophisticated computer models to predict how CLL cells from different patients would respond to signals that help them survive. The research, led by Mitchell's collaborators professors Andrea Pepper and Chris Pepper and Dr. Kennedy and published in the journal Blood, could help scientists better understand why some patients respond particularly well to certain treatments while others develop drug resistance. This is the first time a detailed computer model of B-cell receptor (BCR) signaling in CLL has been directly tested and supported using cells from patients. CLL is the most common adult leukemia and remains incurable and a lifelong condition. Advances in therapies known as Bruton's tyrosine kinase inhibitors (BTKi) have significantly changed outcomes for patients over the years, but responses can vary. These drugs disrupt an important communication system used by CLL cells called the B-cell receptor (BCR) pathway. This forces cancer cells out of protective areas such as the lymph nodes, where they "hide," and into the bloodstream, allowing them to be targeted more effectively. However, some patients eventually develop resistance to treatment. While mutations can explain resistance in some cases, for 20% to 30% of patients who relapse, the reason remains a mystery, with no detectable mutations that would explain why their cancer has become resistant. Using Mitchell's model, the researchers uncovered that CLL cells can use two different communication pathways to survive. The team found that when the usual B-cell receptor (BCR) pathway is blocked by inhibitors, some CLL cells can use a second pathway, called Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9), to continue to evade treatment. This offers a new explanation for why some patients develop resistance to treatment, while others respond exceptionally well. The findings could help scientists and clinicians better understand treatment resistance and, for patients, this could ultimately mean identifying an effective drug or drug combination earlier while avoiding treatments that are unlikely to work. It could also reduce unnecessary side effects, treatment toxicity and time spent in the hospital. Mitchell said, "Therapies targeting BCR signaling have transformed the treatment of CLL, but we still don't fully understand why they work so well for some patients and eventually stop working for others. "Our research suggests that CLL cells may be able to change the signals they rely on to survive. Understanding these differences could ultimately help us better predict how patients will respond to treatment." Andrea Pepper said, "Combining laboratory experiments with mathematical models revealed insights neither approach could have achieved alone. Our findings not only pinpoint which CLL patients need an additional drug alongside BTK inhibitors, but provide clear clues on where to focus next to develop new, targeted therapies for these individuals." Dr. Simon Ridley, director of research and advocacy at Leukemia UK, said, "We're proud to support innovative researchers such as professor Mitchell. Patients with the same diagnosis can respond very differently to the same treatment and, for people with blood cancer, finding the right treatment at the right time is vital. "This groundbreaking new modeling is an exciting example of how technology could help us understand those differences in response and move toward a more personalized treatment plan for patients."
