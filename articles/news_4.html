Patients with relapsed multiple myeloma treated with the immunotherapy teclistamab lived significantly longer and remained in remission far longer than those receiving standard therapies, according to results from a major international Phase III clinical trial published in The New England Journal of Medicine and presented at the 2026 American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) Annual Meeting.

The study, led by senior author C. Ola Landgren, M.D., Ph.D., found nearly 70% of patients receiving teclistamab had no disease progression after 18 months—compared with about 27% of patients receiving standard treatments—while nearly two-thirds achieved complete remission, including many with no detectable cancer on highly sensitive testing.

Landgren is chief of the Sylvester Myeloma Institute at Sylvester Comprehensive Cancer Center, part of the University of Miami Miller School of Medicine.

"Now we have chemotherapy-free immunotherapy options for patients whose myeloma has relapsed for the first time," said Landgren. "We are seeing very deep responses and long clinical benefit from these therapies. This is part of a much bigger transformation happening in myeloma care."

When Landgren began his medical training more than 30 years ago, treatment for multiple myeloma relied almost entirely on chemotherapy. Today, the field has shifted toward immune-based therapies designed to help the body's immune system identify and attack cancer cells more precisely and effectively.

Researchers are also moving these therapies earlier in the disease course instead of reserving them for later stages after multiple treatments have failed.

Teclistamab is part of a newer class of drugs known as bispecific antibodies. The therapy works by linking T cells, a type of immune cell, to BCMA, a protein found on myeloma cells, allowing the immune system to recognize and attack the cancer directly.

The Phase III trial, called MajesTEC-9, enrolled 593 patients across 24 countries. All participants had multiple myeloma that had returned after one to three prior treatments. About three-quarters had already taken and stopped responding to standard therapies, including immune-modulating drugs such as daratumumab and lenalidomide, leaving them with limited treatment options in routine practice.

Even among patients who had failed other therapies, teclistamab improved outcomes. Patients lived longer, stayed in remission longer and, in some cases, saw their cancer reduced to extremely low or undetectable levels known as minimal residual disease negativity.

For many patients, teclistamab did more than slow disease progression. Nearly two-thirds achieved complete remission, meaning physicians could no longer detect signs of myeloma using standard tests. In many cases, even highly sensitive minimal residual disease tests could not find remaining cancer cells.

"To see that this drug is so efficacious and so safe across patients from all these locations worldwide is a very strong signal," Landgren said. "To see that in patients who have been exposed and refractory to commonly used myeloma treatments is very important."

Teclistamab activates immune cells, which can also affect normal immune defenses. As a result, patients receiving the therapy face a higher risk of infection, particularly during the first six months of treatment. Physicians manage that risk through close monitoring and preventive care.

Patients receiving teclistamab often take antiviral and antibiotic medications to help prevent infections. Care teams regularly monitor antibody levels, and patients may receive immunoglobulin infusions if levels drop too low.

Researchers are now studying whether bispecific antibodies such as teclistamab could benefit patients even earlier in the disease course.

"For me, the goal is to develop curative strategies," Landgren said. "We are working toward treatments that can either eliminate the disease entirely or control it for very long periods while minimizing the burden on patients and preserving quality of life."

根据一项发表在《新英格兰医学杂志》并于2026年美国临床肿瘤学会（ASCO）年会上公布的重要国际III期临床试验结果，使用免疫疗法替利司他（teclistamab）治疗的复发性多发性骨髓瘤患者比接受标准疗法的患者显著延长了生存期，并维持了更长的缓解期。

由资深作者C. Ola Landgren医学博士、哲学博士领导的这项研究发现，接受替利司他治疗的患者中，近70%在18个月后没有疾病进展，而接受标准治疗的患者中这一比例约为27%；同时，近三分之二的患者达到了完全缓解，其中许多患者通过高敏感度检测也未检测到可识别的癌细胞。

兰德格伦博士是迈阿密大学米勒医学院下属西尔维斯特综合癌症中心西尔维斯特骨髓瘤研究所的主任。

兰德格伦博士表示：“现在，对于首次复发的多发性骨髓瘤患者，我们有了无需化疗的免疫治疗选择。我们正在看到非常深入的缓解和长期的临床获益。这是骨髓瘤治疗领域正在发生更大转变的一部分。”

兰德格伦博士在30多年前开始医学培训时，多发性骨髓瘤的治疗几乎完全依赖于化疗。如今，该领域已转向基于免疫的疗法，旨在帮助身体的免疫系统更精确有效地识别和攻击癌细胞。

研究人员还将这些疗法提前到疾病早期阶段使用，而不是等到多种治疗失败后的晚期。

替利司他属于一种新型药物，即双特异性抗体。该疗法通过将T细胞（一种免疫细胞）与骨髓瘤细胞上发现的BCMA（一种蛋白质）连接起来，从而使免疫系统能够直接识别并攻击癌细胞。

这项名为MajesTEC-9的III期临床试验招募了来自24个国家的593名患者。所有参与者均患有多发性骨髓瘤，且在接受一至三种既往治疗后疾病复发。约四分之三的患者已经使用过标准疗法（包括达雷妥尤单抗和来那度胺等免疫调节药物），并且对其不再产生应答，这使得他们在常规实践中治疗选择有限。

即使在那些对其他疗法失败的患者中，替利司他也能改善治疗结果。患者的生存期延长，缓解期更长，在某些情况下，癌细胞数量减少到极低或检测不到的水平，即微小残留病阴性。

对于许多患者而言，替利司他不仅仅是减缓了疾病进展。近三分之二的患者达到了完全缓解，这意味着医生无法再使用标准检测方法检测到骨髓瘤的迹象。在许多情况下，即使是高敏感度的微小残留病检测也未能发现残余癌细胞。

兰德格伦博士说：“看到这种药物在全球所有这些地区的患者中都表现出如此高的疗效和安全性，这是一个非常强烈的信号。在那些已经接受过并对常用骨髓瘤治疗方案产生耐药性的患者中看到这一点，尤为重要。”

替利司他激活免疫细胞，这也可能影响正常的免疫防御。因此，接受该疗法的患者面临更高的感染风险，尤其是在治疗的前六个月。医生通过密切监测和预防性护理来管理这种风险。

接受替利司他治疗的患者通常服用抗病毒和抗生素药物以帮助预防感染。护理团队定期监测抗体水平，如果水平过低，患者可能会接受免疫球蛋白输注。

研究人员目前正在研究替利司他等双特异性抗体是否能使患者在疾病进程的更早期获益。

兰德格伦博士表示：“对我而言，目标是开发治愈性策略。我们正致力于开发能够完全消除疾病或长期控制疾病，同时最大限度地减轻患者负担并保留生活质量的治疗方案。”
