癌症通过破坏免疫细胞的协同作用逃避免疫攻击

Cancer can evade immune attack by disrupting the teamwork of immune cells

深度解析

本文核心揭示了自然杀伤细胞（NK细胞）与树突状细胞（DCs）之间的协同作用在抗肿瘤免疫中的关键性。NK细胞作为先天免疫系统的首要防线，不仅能直接杀伤癌细胞，还能通过释放如干扰素-伽马（IFN-γ）等细胞因子，招募并活化DCs。活化的DCs则能产生白细胞介素-12（IL-12），进而刺激NK细胞释放更多IFN-γ，形成一个正反馈循环，有效增强DCs呈递肿瘤抗原的能力，并启动适应性T细胞反应，从而放大炎症并维持对肿瘤的攻击。然而，文章强调在实体瘤中，代谢应激和免疫抑制通路常导致NK细胞-DC通讯回路中断，造成肿瘤免疫逃逸。这一机制的深入理解对肿瘤免疫学和临床治疗具有重大影响。传统的免疫疗法多侧重于T细胞或免疫检查点，但通过恢复NK细胞与DC之间的有效交流，可以激活更广泛、更持久的免疫应答。例如，将IL-12基因疗法与免疫检查点抑制剂局部联合应用，或利用FLT3配体增强DC活性，以及诱导NK细胞自身产生招募DCs的信号，均代表了未来免疫治疗的新策略。这预示着抗肿瘤策略将从单一细胞靶向转向多细胞（NK细胞、DCs和T细胞）协同作用的整合方案，以克服现有免疫治疗的耐药性，将“冷”肿瘤转变为免疫可及的“热”肿瘤，为癌症患者带来更有效的治疗选择。

DOI: 10.1038/s43018-026-01276-2 The immune system's attack on cancer depends on more than cells capable of killing tumors. It also requires a coordinated effort in which some immune cells recognize danger, rally reinforcements and help train other components in a vast disease-fighting network. Natural killer cells, or NK cells, are among the body's first responders. Their main job is to kill—but they also have other roles in the onslaught against cancer. They recognize and destroy cancerous cells without first having to be taught to identify a specific tumor. NKs also release chemical signals that recruit and activate other immune cells, helping to set a broader assault in motion. Dr. Nicholas D. Huntington and colleagues at Monash University and the University of Melbourne in Victoria, Australia, reviewed recent studies on natural killer cells and concluded that NKs help initiate and amplify inflammatory responses needed for an effective attack on tumors. Recruit and activate "Beyond direct tumor killing, NK cells trigger early immune responses by recruiting and activating dendritic cells—DCs—thereby enabling downstream T cell activation," Huntington, lead author of the review, wrote in Nature Cancer. "However, in established tumors," he continued, "NK cell-DC communication is often disrupted by metabolic stress and immunosuppressive pathways, leading to immune evasion." That evasion, the tumor's ability to escape the immune system, is a potentially deadly phenomenon under study worldwide. It is also a crucial topic being studied by scientists striving to make immunotherapy more effective. Developing a stronger understanding of natural killer cells can ultimately improve therapies for multiple forms of cancer, Huntington and colleagues say. Natural killer cells, for example, are key recruiters of dendritic cells, which grab fragments of malignant cells—antigens—and present them to T cells, helping to activate a more targeted assault. The exchange between NKs and dendritic cells can strengthen the immune response and draw additional immune cells into the hostile tumor microenvironment. It is teamwork par excellence—when it actually works. NK cell–cDC1 cross-talk coordinates tumor immunity and primes adaptive T cell responses. Credit: Nature Cancer (2026). DOI: 10.1038/s43018-026-01276-2 Teamwork can falter In established tumors, that teamwork can break down. Huntington and his colleagues reviewed evidence showing how disruptions in communication between natural killer cells and dendritic cells can actually help tumors evade immune attack—and why restoring that communication could make some cancers more responsive to immunotherapy. "NK cell-mediated tumor clearance is most effective in the circulation," Huntington wrote, "whereas once malignant cells extravasate and establish tissue-resident lesions, NK cell function and downstream immune priming are progressively lost. "This creates a central paradox: tumor cells readily cleared in circulation can persist and expand within solid tumors," Huntington continued. "We propose that this reflects not only a loss of NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity but also failures of NK cell-DC circuits required to initiate and sustain solid tumor inflammation." One important signal in that response is interleukin-12, or IL-12, produced by activated dendritic cells. It stimulates NK cells to release interferon-gamma, a molecule that can strengthen dendritic-cell activity, increase their ability to present tumor antigens and encourage other immune cells to enter the tumor. The resulting feedback loop can amplify inflammation and help sustain an attack. But cancer can interfere with the circuit at multiple points. Tumors can suppress signals that attract NK cells, impair dendritic-cell activity and produce molecules that weaken immune cells that infiltrate the tumor. Hostile territory The tumor microenvironment is unfriendly territory on multiple fronts. Solid tumors often consume much of the available glucose and oxygen while accumulating metabolic byproducts. Such conditions can damage NK cells' energy-producing machinery and diminish their ability to kill cancer cells. Researchers are investigating ways to restore NKs' capabilities rather than simply stimulating NK cells to become more aggressive killers. One approach involves IL-12, the powerful immune-stimulating molecule whose use has been limited by serious toxicities when administered to patients. In a clinical trial involving patients with advanced melanoma, local delivery of an IL-12 gene therapy combined with an immune checkpoint inhibitor produced a response rate of 41%, with complete responses in 36% of patients. The combination was reported to be well tolerated. Other approaches aim to increase the activity of dendritic cells. FLT3 ligand, a molecule that supports the development and survival of these cells, has been studied in clinical settings, including in combination with a melanoma vaccine. Researchers are also investigating ways to encourage NK cells themselves to produce signals that recruit dendritic cells into tumors and sustain their activity. The goal is to turn tumors that largely evade immune cells into accessible sites that immune cells can enter to communicate with each other and mount a sustained attack. The Australian teams say future therapies may need to coordinate several parts of the immune response—NK cells, dendritic cells and T cells—rather than target individual cells in isolation. Restoring that teamwork, they suggest, could offer a way to strengthen antitumor immunity and potentially help overcome resistance to existing immunotherapies.
