A major international Phase III clinical trial, led by Queen Mary University of London, has found that ocrelizumab—a medication already prescribed to some patients with MS—significantly slows disability progression in people with primary progressive multiple sclerosis (PPMS), including older patients and those with more advanced disease who are wheelchair users.

The ORATORIO-HAND study involved over 1,000 patients across 22 countries, making it the largest placebo-controlled treatment trial to date in a broad population of people with PPMS. The findings were published in The Lancet.

Overall, the study found that ocrelizumab significantly reduced disease progression across several important disability measures. The results show that patients treated with ocrelizumab had a 30% lower risk of disability progression compared with patients who received a placebo.

The treatment also reduced the worsening of hand and upper-limb function by 41% at 12 weeks and reduced the risk of requiring a wheelchair by 52% among those who were still unable to walk or move about freely at the start of the study.

Researchers found that the treatment effect of ocrelizumab was even greater in patients who showed signs of inflammatory disease activity on baseline MRI scans, with a 55% reduction in the risk of disability progression in this group.

PPMS, a form of MS in which disability worsens over time, affects between 10 and 15% of people with the condition. Unlike relapsing forms of MS, treatment options for PPMS remain limited, and there is ongoing debate about whether people with more advanced disease are still likely to benefit from treatment. This study goes a long way to settle that debate.

ORATORIO-HAND was specifically designed to address this question. Previous major studies often exclude patients aged 55 and above or those with advanced disability. In contrast, this study included patients up to the age of 65 and many with substantial mobility impairment and advanced disease progression.

In the study, researchers focused on walking ability and assessed upper-limb function using the 9-Hole Peg test, which measures hand and arm dexterity. They note that this method is particularly important because preserving hand function can have a major impact on independence, communication, and quality of life for people living with advanced PPMS.

In a separate survey conducted by Professor Giovannoni and colleagues at Queen Mary, people with worsening MS prioritized upper-limb and hand function over lower-limb function, citing independence, grooming, and toileting as their reasons.

Researchers note that their findings suggest ocrelizumab could benefit a wider patient population than currently has access to the medication, which will lead to changes in the way MS is managed on the NHS, and it will change future trial design for progressive MS.

Gavin Giovannoni, lead author and Professor of Neurology at Queen Mary, said, "These findings are important because they show that treatment can make a meaningful difference to people with more advanced forms of MS and can help preserve hand and arm function, which is important for maintaining independence, daily activities and quality of life.

"Our study suggests we should think differently about what successful treatment looks like in advanced MS and that we shouldn't assume a lack of benefit in certain groups. The O'HAND study changes the way we manage and think about MS."

Dr. Catherine Godbold, Senior Research Communications Manager at the MS Society, said, "The results of the ORATORIO-HAND trial are incredibly positive to see. MS can be debilitating, exhausting and unpredictable, and hand and arm function is essential for helping people to remain independent. Many previous trials have focused solely on walking ability as a measure of whether a drug is effective. But trials like this are vital in helping us find treatments for everyone.

"Ocrelizumab is already used as a treatment for active relapsing MS and early primary progressive MS. These results could mean it is made available for more people with primary progressive MS, who don't currently have access to any treatment options. The key now is how we work together to see these findings translated into clinical practice."

由伦敦玛丽女王大学领导的一项重要的国际III期临床试验发现，奥克瑞珠单抗（ocrelizumab）——一种已用于某些多发性硬化症（MS）患者的药物——能显著延缓原发性进展型多发性硬化症（PPMS）患者的残疾进展，包括老年患者和那些病情更严重、已使用轮椅的患者。

ORATORIO-HAND研究涉及22个国家的一千多名患者，使其成为迄今为止针对广泛PPMS人群进行的最大的安慰剂对照治疗试验。研究结果已发表在《柳叶刀》（The Lancet）杂志上。

总体而言，研究发现奥克瑞珠单抗在多项重要的残疾评估指标上显著降低了疾病进展。结果显示，接受奥克瑞珠单抗治疗的患者残疾进展风险比接受安慰剂的患者低30%。

该治疗还在12周时将手和上肢功能恶化风险降低了41%，并使研究开始时仍无法自由行走或活动自如的患者需要轮椅的风险降低了52%。

研究人员发现，在基线MRI扫描显示有炎症性疾病活动迹象的患者中，奥克瑞珠单抗的治疗效果甚至更大，该组患者的残疾进展风险降低了55%。

PPMS是一种多发性硬化症，其特点是残疾随时间逐渐恶化，影响10%到15%的多发性硬化症患者。与复发缓解型多发性硬化症不同，PPMS的治疗选择仍然有限，而且关于病情更严重的患者是否仍能从治疗中获益，一直存在争议。这项研究在很大程度上解决了这一争议。

ORATORIO-HAND研究专门旨在解决这个问题。以往的重大研究通常会将55岁及以上的患者或有严重残疾的患者排除在外。相比之下，这项研究纳入了高达65岁的患者，其中许多人有显著的活动障碍和晚期疾病进展。

在研究中，研究人员侧重于步行能力，并使用9孔钉测试（9-Hole Peg test）评估上肢功能，该测试测量手和手臂的灵巧度。他们指出，这种方法尤为重要，因为对手功能的保留对于晚期PPMS患者的独立性、沟通和生活质量具有重大影响。

在由乔瓦诺尼教授及其玛丽女王大学的同事进行的另一项调查中，病情恶化的多发性硬化症患者将上肢和手的功能置于下肢功能之上，理由是独立性、个人卫生和如厕能力。

研究人员指出，他们的发现表明奥克瑞珠单抗可能惠及比目前有权获得该药物的患者更广泛的人群，这将导致NHS（英国国家医疗服务体系）管理多发性硬化症的方式发生变化，并改变未来进行进展型多发性硬化症试验的设计。

研究主要作者、玛丽女王大学神经病学教授加文·乔瓦诺尼（Gavin Giovannoni）表示：“这些发现很重要，因为它们表明治疗可以对患有更晚期多发性硬化症的患者产生有意义的影响，并有助于保留手和手臂功能，这对于维持独立性、日常活动和生活质量至关重要。”

“我们的研究表明，我们应该以不同的方式看待晚期多发性硬化症的成功治疗，不应假定某些群体没有益处。ORATORIO-HAND研究改变了我们管理和思考多发性硬化症的方式。”

MS协会高级研究传播经理凯瑟琳·戈德博尔德博士（Dr. Catherine Godbold）表示：“ORATORIO-HAND试验的结果令人鼓舞。多发性硬化症可能使人衰弱、疲惫和不可预测，手和手臂功能对于帮助人们保持独立至关重要。许多以前的试验只关注步行能力作为衡量药物是否有效的指标。但像这样的试验对于帮助我们为所有人找到治疗方法至关重要。”

“奥克瑞珠单抗已用于治疗活动性复发型多发性硬化症和早期原发性进展型多发性硬化症。这些结果可能意味着它将可供更多目前无法获得任何治疗选择的原发性进展型多发性硬化症患者使用。现在的关键是如何共同努力，将这些发现转化为临床实践。”
