Wearing headphones during surgery may reduce nausea and vomiting afterward

深度解析

这篇研究揭示了术中噪音对术后恶心呕吐（PONV）的潜在影响，提供了一种简单而有效的非药物干预手段。PONV是全身麻醉和手术后常见的并发症，其发生涉及多种因素，包括麻醉药物、阿片类止痛药、手术类型以及患者个体易感性。传统的预防措施主要依赖于药物治疗。本研究的核心专业概念在于，即使在全身麻醉下患者处于无意识状态，其听觉通路可能仍在工作，对手术室噪音做出应激反应，进而影响自主神经系统或中枢神经系统相关区域（如化学感受器触发区），导致PONV。这种机制虽尚未完全阐明，但研究结果支持了“无意识声音处理”对生理结果的影响。从行业影响来看，这一发现具有重大意义：它为麻醉科医生提供了一种经济、非侵入性的辅助预防工具，可能减少止吐药物的使用，降低并发症风险，缩短患者在恢复室的停留时间，从而优化临床路径，提升患者体验，并有望降低医疗成本。未来研究将进一步探究其神经生理学机制，并可能推动手术室环境优化的新标准。

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: Credit: Unsplash/CC0 Public Domain Patients who wore noise-canceling headphones during minimally invasive gynecological surgery were significantly less likely to experience postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), suggest findings from a randomized clinical trial presented at the Anesthesiology 2026 annual meeting. PONV is one of the most common unpleasant side effects after surgery and anesthesia that can lead to dehydration, delayed recovery, added medication use and longer stays in the recovery room. Anesthetic and pain medications are well-known contributors to PONV, but researchers say noise in the operating room may be another, less-recognized factor. Patients who have minimally invasive gynecological surgery under general anesthesia are at particularly high risk for PONV, even when they receive recommended medications to prevent it. "Patients under general anesthesia aren't consciously aware of what's happening around them, but that doesn't mean the brain completely stops processing sound," said Huayue Liu, M.D., lead author of the study and an anesthesiologist at the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, Suzhou, China. "Our findings suggest that simply reducing intraoperative noise could give anesthesiologists another tool to help prevent PONV after surgery, without adding another medication." In the study, 192 adults ages 18 to 65 under general anesthesia were randomly assigned to one of two groups. Half wore commercially available over-the-head noise-canceling headphones from the start of anesthesia until the surgery ended; the other half did not. All patients received the same anesthesia and standard PONV-prevention medication. Within 48 hours of surgery, 35.4% of patients who wore headphones experienced PONV, compared to 52.1% of those who did not—a 32% lower risk. In other words, about 17 fewer patients experienced PONV for every 100 who wore headphones. Those who wore headphones also had less severe PONV within the first 24 hours. There was no increase in adverse events in the headphone-wearing group. Operating-room sound levels in the study averaged about 66 decibels, somewhere between normal conversation and a nearby vacuum cleaner. Although that level is far below the noise of a jackhammer, the authors note that patients under general anesthesia are not consciously aware of sound, but their bodies may still process it in ways that are not fully understood. "Future studies should measure the amount of noise patients are exposed to during surgery and how their brains and bodies respond to better understand whether and how sound may affect PONV," Liu said.
