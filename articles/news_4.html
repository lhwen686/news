Skull microchannels reveal hidden route for brain immune defense

Credit: Spanish National Research Center for Human Evolution

A study led by Rafael Gallareto-Sande, a predoctoral researcher at the Centro Nacional de Investigación sobre la Evolución Humana (CENIEH), provides new insights into a network of tiny blood vessels within the cranial bones that play a key role in brain protection. The research, published in The Anatomical Record, reports for the first time the number and size of these microscopic channels, known as vascular microforamina, which connect the cranial bone with the intracranial space.

To conduct the study, the team used computed tomography on a sample of adult human crania. The results show marked interindividual variability: each cranium may contain from around 100 to 400 of these small channels, distributed across the inner surface of the cranial vault.

Most of these conduits are extremely small, with diameters below 0.5 mm. However, the larger channels, although fewer in number, contribute a comparable share of blood flow. These are mainly concentrated in specific regions of the skull, particularly in the posterior and central areas of the parietal bone, in association with major venous structures.

The observed distribution suggests a close relationship with the meningeal vascular system. It also indicates that the number of these channels—especially those of intermediate size—increases with the expansion of the diploë, the spongy bone tissue, particularly in the posterior regions of the cranium.

Brain defense

These microforamina were long thought to be primarily involved in brain thermoregulation. However, recent studies indicate that they also function as pathways for immune cell trafficking. In particular, bone marrow within the diploë can release molecules associated with neuroinflammatory processes through these channels, contributing to the brain's defense against infection and injury.

"The microvessels are part of the so-called glymphatic system, a network responsible for the transport of solutes and the clearance of metabolic waste from the brain. Its proper functioning is essential and has been linked to conditions such as stroke and Alzheimer's disease. This study therefore opens new avenues for research with implications for both anthropology and medicine," says Gallareto-Sande.

来源：西班牙国家人类进化研究中心

由西班牙国家人类进化研究中心（CENIEH）的博士研究员Rafael Gallareto-Sande领导的一项研究，为颅骨内微小血管网络提供了新见解，该网络在脑保护中发挥着关键作用。这项发表在《解剖学记录》上的研究，首次报道了这些连接颅骨与颅内空间的微观通道（被称为血管微孔）的数量和大小。

为了进行这项研究，研究团队对一组成年人颅骨样本进行了计算机断层扫描。结果显示出显著的个体差异：每个颅骨可能包含约100到400个这种小通道，分布在颅顶内表面。

这些通道大多非常微小，直径小于0.5毫米。然而，较大的通道虽然数量较少，但其血流量贡献却相当。它们主要集中在颅骨的特定区域，特别是顶骨的后部和中央区域，并与主要的静脉结构相关联。

观察到的分布表明其与脑膜血管系统存在密切关系。它还指出，这些通道的数量——特别是中等大小的通道——随着板障（即松质骨组织）的扩张而增加，尤其是在颅骨的后部区域。

脑部防御

长期以来，人们认为这些微孔主要参与脑部体温调节。然而，最新研究表明，它们也作为免疫细胞运输的通路。特别是，板障内的骨髓可以通过这些通道释放与神经炎症过程相关的分子，从而有助于大脑防御感染和损伤。

Gallareto-Sande表示：“这些微血管是所谓胶淋巴系统的一部分，该系统负责转运溶质并清除大脑中的代谢废物。其正常功能至关重要，并已与中风和阿尔茨海默病等疾病相关联。因此，这项研究为人类学和医学领域的研究开辟了新途径。”
