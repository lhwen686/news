新型联合疗法增强免疫系统抗癌

A new combined therapy to boost the immune system against cancer

其核心医学机制并非iNKT细胞直接杀伤肿瘤，而是它们与APCs协同作用，激活并“编程”宿主免疫系统，尤其诱导了具有特异性识别和长期记忆功能的记忆表型T细胞的生成。这种机制实现了从单纯的细胞杀伤到更持久、更具适应性的免疫系统重构。该研究的行业影响深远，为难治性癌症的治疗提供了新的视角，预示着通过患者来源的APCs结合iPSC衍生的iNKT细胞，实现高度个性化且持久的免疫疗法。目前相关临床试验已在进行，有望为现有治疗手段束手无策的患者带来革命性的希望。
Original Text
Cancer immunotherapy is built on a simple but powerful idea: the immune system can recognize and destroy cancer cells if it is properly activated. In many patients, however, this response is too weak or too slow to be effective.

Recently, a type of immune cell called invariant natural killer T (iNKT) cells has attracted attention because of their ability to act as coordinators of immune responses, rapidly activating and rallying other immune cells to fight cancer.

One of the major obstacles holding back iNKT cell-based therapies is that patients with cancer often cannot produce enough iNKT cells on their own for therapeutic use. Scientists have worked around this issue by generating iNKT cells using donor-sourced induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs); these are reprogrammed cells obtained from healthy donors that can be grown in the laboratory. But an important question remains unanswered: when these lab-derived iNKT cells are introduced into a patient, do they actually set off the intended immune response?

In a recent study, a research team led by Assistant Professor Takahiro Aoki from the Department of Medical Immunology, Chiba University, Japan, set out to answer this question. They tested whether iPSC-derived iNKT cells could trigger effective antitumor immunity when combined with antigen-presenting cells (APCs) loaded with α-galactosylceramide (αGalCer), a lipid compound known to activate iNKT cells.

Their paper, published in the journal Stem Cell Research & Therapy on March 29, 2026, was co-authored by Dr. Haruhiko Koseki from RIKEN Center for Integrative Medical Sciences and Professor Shinichiro Motohashi from Chiba University.

Dr. Aoki shares the motivation behind his study.

I began my research after feeling powerless while treating pediatric patients with cancer who could not be cured even with multidisciplinary treatment of chemotherapy, including stem cell transplantation, radiation therapy, and surgery." Takahiro Aoki, Assistant Professor, Department of Medical Immunology, Chiba University

The team used a mouse model designed to reflect a human immune environment. These mice were transplanted with both patient-derived lung cancer cells and human immune cells, allowing the researchers to observe how iNKT cell therapy would interact with a human-like immune system. The animals were divided into four groups: each group received either iPSC-derived iNKT cells alone, APCs loaded with αGalCer, both cell types combined, or no treatment at all.

The combined therapy clearly outperformed all other groups in suppressing tumor growth. Notably, when human immune cells were excluded from the model, the antitumor effect largely disappeared. This implies that the benefit came not from direct tumor killing by the iNKT cells themselves but from the broader immune response they triggered.

Using single-cell RNA sequencing, the researchers found that the combined therapy generated a population of memory-phenotype T cells, a type of long-lasting immune cell that can recognize, remember, and respond to a specific threat repeatedly.

These memory-phenotype T cells carried receptors that were confirmed to be specifically reactive to the tumor cells used in the study. When the researchers removed these memory-phenotype T cells, the antitumor effect was significantly reduced, thus confirming their central role.

Looking ahead, the findings point toward more personalized forms of immunotherapy. By using APCs derived from a patient and combining them with iPSC-derived iNKT cells, it may be possible to tailor immune responses to the specific characteristics of an individual's tumor via genetic modification. "This approach could potentially save the lives of patients with intractable cancers that are difficult to cure with existing treatments, and a clinical trial is currently being conducted on patients with advanced head and neck cancer," concludes Dr. Aoki.

癌症免疫疗法基于一个简单而强大的理念：如果免疫系统被适当激活，它就能识别并摧毁癌细胞。然而，在许多患者中，这种免疫反应过于微弱或迟缓，无法有效发挥作用。

近来，一种名为不变自然杀伤T（iNKT）细胞的免疫细胞引起了广泛关注，因为它们能够作为免疫反应的协调者，迅速激活并集结其他免疫细胞来对抗癌症。

阻碍iNKT细胞疗法发展的主要障碍之一是，癌症患者自身通常无法产生足够用于治疗的iNKT细胞。科学家们通过使用供体来源的诱导多能干细胞（iPSCs）来生成iNKT细胞，从而解决了这个问题；iPSCs是从健康供体获得的、可以在实验室中培养的重编程细胞。但一个重要问题仍未得到解答：当这些实验室来源的iNKT细胞被引入患者体内时，它们是否真的能够触发预期的免疫反应？

在最近的一项研究中，由日本千叶大学医学免疫学系助理教授青木崇浩（Takahiro Aoki）领导的一个研究团队着手回答了这个问题。他们测试了当iPSC衍生的iNKT细胞与负载α-半乳糖基神经酰胺（αGalCer）的抗原呈递细胞（APCs）结合时，是否能触发有效的抗肿瘤免疫。αGalCer是一种已知能激活iNKT细胞的脂质化合物。

他们的论文于2026年3月29日发表在《干细胞研究与治疗》（Stem Cell Research & Therapy）期刊上，合著者包括日本理化学研究所综合医学科学中心的古濑木春彦（Haruhiko Koseki）博士和千叶大学的本桥新一郎（Shinichiro Motohashi）教授。

青木博士分享了他这项研究背后的动机。

“在治疗那些即使经过包括干细胞移植、放射疗法和手术在内的多学科化疗，也无法治愈的儿童癌症患者时，我感到无能为力，在那之后我便开始了我的研究。”千叶大学医学免疫学系助理教授青木崇浩说道。

该团队使用了一种旨在反映人类免疫环境的小鼠模型。这些小鼠被植入了患者来源的肺癌细胞和人类免疫细胞，使研究人员能够观察iNKT细胞疗法如何与类似人类的免疫系统相互作用。动物被分为四组：每组分别接受单独的iPSC衍生的iNKT细胞、负载αGalCer的APCs、两种细胞类型结合治疗，或者不进行任何治疗。

联合疗法在抑制肿瘤生长方面明显优于所有其他组。值得注意的是，当从模型中排除人类免疫细胞时，抗肿瘤效果大体上消失了。这表明益处并非来自iNKT细胞自身的直接肿瘤杀伤作用，而是来自它们所触发的更广泛的免疫反应。

研究人员利用单细胞RNA测序发现，联合疗法产生了一群记忆表型T细胞，这是一种能够反复识别、记忆并响应特定威胁的持久性免疫细胞。

这些记忆表型T细胞携带着被证实对研究中使用的肿瘤细胞具有特异性反应的受体。当研究人员移除这些记忆表型T细胞时，抗肿瘤效果显著降低，从而证实了它们的核心作用。

展望未来，这些发现指向了更个性化的免疫疗法形式。通过使用来源于患者的APCs并将其与iPSC衍生的iNKT细胞结合，有可能通过基因修饰，根据个体肿瘤的特定特征定制免疫反应。青木博士总结道：“这种方法可能挽救那些现有治疗难以治愈的难治性癌症患者的生命，目前正在对晚期头颈癌患者进行临床试验。”
