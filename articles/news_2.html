新的动物模型为研究燃烧卡路里的棕色脂肪提供了更精确的方法

New animal model offers more precise way to study calorie-burning brown fat

深度解析

本文介绍了一种新型UCP1-IRES-Cre基因敲入动物模型，标志着代谢研究领域的重大进展。该模型的核心在于利用解偶联蛋白1（UCP1），这是一种主要在棕色和米色脂肪细胞中表达的线粒体内膜蛋白。UCP1的关键生理机制是通过将氧化磷酸化与ATP合成解耦联，将质子梯度以热能形式散失，即非颤抖性产热。这一生物学功能对于能量消耗、维持体温和影响整体代谢健康至关重要。 新模型的技术创新在于其增强的特异性。与以往在大脑等非靶组织中表现出非预期Cre重组酶活性的UCP1-Cre模型不同，UCP1-IRES-Cre模型的Cre表达受到天然UCP1基因调控元件的精确控制。这种“敲入”策略确保Cre活性准确反映产热脂肪组织中的UCP1表达，从而最大程度地减少混杂实验变量。此外，该模型能够永久标记“曾经”表达UCP1的细胞，为脂肪细胞的谱系追踪和发育可塑性（特别是白色脂肪的“米色化”过程）提供了独特的历史视角。通过杰克逊实验室提供这一经过验证的资源，无疑将加速全球在肥胖症、糖尿病及其他代谢紊乱方面的研究，通过确保可靠的组织特异性基因操作，促进精确治疗靶点的发现。

DOI: 10.1038/s41598-026-74773-0 Researchers at LSU's Pennington Biomedical Research Center have developed and validated a new genetic animal model that gives scientists a more precise way to study brown and beige fat, specialized tissues that use energy to produce heat and play an important role in metabolism. The paper is published in the journal Scientific Reports. The UCP1-IRES-Cre animal model closely follows the expression of UCP1, a protein that plays a central role in the heat-producing function of brown and beige fat, while reducing unintended genetic activity. The new tool could help researchers better understand how thermogenic fat contributes to energy expenditure, body weight regulation and metabolic health. A more precise tool for studying brown fat Brown adipose tissue, commonly known as brown fat, helps the body produce heat by using stored energy. Beige fat cells can also develop thermogenic properties and contribute to this process. Because of their role in energy metabolism, brown and beige fat have become important areas of research in obesity and metabolic health. Scientists frequently use genetic tools called Cre animal models to study the function of specific cell types. Widely used UCP1-Cre models have allowed researchers to target brown and beige fat, but previous research has shown that some of these models can also produce unintended genetic activity in the brain and other tissues. That can make it difficult to determine whether an experimental effect is caused by changes in thermogenic fat or by genetic activity elsewhere in the body. To address this challenge, the Pennington Biomedical team characterized a knock-in model in which Cre expression is regulated by the same genetic mechanisms that control the body's natural UCP1 gene. The researchers found that the new model closely corresponded with UCP1 expression in brown fat in both males and females and showed substantially less unintended Cre activity in the brain than previously reported UCP1-Cre models. "This model gives researchers a more precise way to study the contribution of thermogenic adipose tissue while reducing the potential for unintended effects in the brain and other tissues," said Dr. Heike Muenzberg-Gruening, director of the Central Leptin Signaling Laboratory. "When we are studying how a particular tissue contributes to metabolism, we need to be confident that the genetic tools we use are actually targeting that tissue." The researchers found that reporter activity sometimes extended beyond areas where UCP1 protein was present. This was particularly evident in some white fat depots. The finding is consistent with the way the model permanently labels cells that have previously expressed UCP1. In other words, the reporter can provide a record of past UCP1 production even after current UCP1 protein levels have declined. This characteristic could give researchers a way to investigate the history and development of thermogenic fat cells, including changes that occur as white fat takes on characteristics of beige fat. The UCP1-IRES-Cre animal line was originally generated in the laboratory of the late Dr. Randall Mynatt at Pennington Biomedical. Although the animal line was generated previously, its tissue specificity and research utility had not been systematically characterized or described in the scientific literature. The UCP1-IRES-Cre animal line will be available to researchers through The Jackson Laboratory (JAX), a major international repository and distributor of research animal models. JAX has accepted the line for distribution to the research community and is making the resource available to researchers studying metabolism, adipose tissue biology and energy balance. "Having this animal model accepted by The Jackson Laboratory and made available to researchers around the world is an important next step," said Muenzberg-Gruening. "JAX accepts animal models that are expected to be useful resources for the community and provides an independent indication of the potential value of this resource for researchers studying metabolism, adipose tissue biology and energy balance." The researchers say the UCP1-IRES-Cre model provides an improved genetic tool for studying thermogenic adipocytes and may help reduce potential confounding effects in research examining energy expenditure, thermoregulation and metabolic health. Its availability through JAX will allow researchers beyond Pennington Biomedical to incorporate the model into studies of metabolism, adipose tissue biology and energy balance. The study was conducted by researchers in Pennington Biomedical's Neurobiology of Nutrition and Metabolism, Central Leptin Signaling, Matrix Biology and Gene Regulation and Metabolism Laboratories. Researchers included Joshua Meyer, Jaclyn Williams, Jennifer Bailey, Ji Suk Change, Carrie Elks and Muenzberg-Gruening.
