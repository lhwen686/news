Study links inherited genetics to cancer susceptibility and progression

深度解析

这项研究通过精心控制的小鼠模型，揭示了个体遗传背景在癌症发生和演变中的核心作用，为癌症生物学带来了范式转变。核心专业概念在于，肿瘤的起源和进展并非仅由环境致癌物诱导的获得性突变随机驱动，而是遗传基因与这些突变之间复杂的相互作用。研究发现，尽管多种肿瘤最终都趋向于激活MAPK通路（一个调控细胞生长、分化和存活的关键分子信号级联），但其上游的特定驱动突变类型及其下游对其他致癌信号通路活性的影响，乃至全基因组复制（一种严重的染色体不稳定性事件，整套染色体倍增）的发生倾向，均受到个体遗传背景的显著调控。这意味着，即便最终表型相似，不同遗传背景的个体其肿瘤发生路径和分子机制可能大相径庭。这一发现对肿瘤学领域具有深远影响。它强调了将遗传基因组学纳入未来的癌症风险评估、早期筛查和精准医疗策略的重要性，预示着基于个人遗传特征量身定制的预防、诊断和治疗方案，从而提升肿瘤防治的效率和准确性。

英文原文

Original
Scientists have found the first direct evidence of the powerful role our genetic makeup plays in influencing our risk of cancer, with inherited genes interacting with acquired genetic mutations to shape how tumors evolve. The findings could explain why some people are more susceptible to cancer than others in the same environment and suggest that future cancer prevention and screening strategies may need to account more carefully for inherited genetics and population diversity. The research, published today in Nature and carried out in mice, suggests that responses to DNA-damaging cancer treatments could differ depending on a patient's background genetics, strengthening the case for a more personalised approach to treatment. Tumors arise when our DNA accumulates errors, or mutations, causing the cells to grow faster and ignore signals that would otherwise instruct damaged cells to die before they can cause harm. Environmental exposures – for example, cigarette smoke or sunlight – influence how much DNA damage occurs, and inherited genetic alterations can alter how many mutations accumulate. Not everyone exposed to the same environmental risk factors will develop cancer, however. Most smokers do not develop lung cancer – and some non-smokers develop lung cancer. The reason why is almost certainly related to our inherited genetic makeup, but finding direct evidence has proved challenging in patient cohort studies. Even though many studies have suggested that inherited genetic differences affect cancer risk, proving this link has been difficult because individuals within – and between – human populations vary in lifestyle, environment, and exposure history. The study is the result of years of international collaboration, between the University of Cambridge, University of Edinburgh, and others around Europe and the US, and was co-led by Professor Duncan Odom, Dr Sarah Aitken and Professor Martin Taylor. In experiments largely carried out at the Cancer Research UK (CRUK) Cambridge Institute at the University of Cambridge, scientists developed an experimental approach that allowed them to control environmental factors experimentally and show that genetic background itself can alter how tumors arise and evolve. The team bred four strains of mice with varying susceptibility to liver cancer, spanning a level of genetic diversity comparable to that seen in human populations. They then exposed the mice to a single dose of the liver carcinogen diethylnitrosamine (DEN). DEN is found in tobacco smoke and some processed foods and is known to cause DNA damage in liver cells, leading to mutations that can initiate tumor growth. Because every mouse received the same dose at the same age – 15 days of age – under controlled conditions, the researchers were able to eliminate the environmental variation that confounds studies in humans. They then sequenced the genomes of almost 600 tumors and analysed the gene activity that developed as well as examining the untreated mice to compare spontaneous tumor formation across strains. Using this data, they were able to reconstruct how each tumor evolved from its original cancer-causing mutation. Across all mouse strains, cancers nearly always acquired a cancer-driving mutation that activated the same cancer-promoting signalling system, called the MAPK pathway. This is a multi-step cascade of molecular signals that controls important life processes, including cell growth and cell differentiation, and plays a key role in numerous types of cancer. However, depending on the inherited genetics of the mouse, the particular driver mutation that was acquired altered the activity of other cancer-associated signalling pathways, as well as causing a striking tendency for whole-genome duplication, an event in which the entire set of chromosomes is doubled. Senior author Professor Duncan Odom, who led the research while at the CRUK Cambridge Institute and is now based at DKFZ (German Cancer Research Centre) in Heidelberg, Germany, said: "Cancer does not arise entirely by chance. Although tumors often reach the same biological endpoint, the path to that endpoint is determined by an individual's genetic background. "We've been able to show for the first time the extent to which genetic background influences both the mutation processes and the pathways leading to tumor development." The researchers say the findings have implications for cancer screening and precision medicine. First author Dr/ Sarah Aitken, Assistant Professor at Yale School of Medicine, who also worked on the research at the CRUK Cambridge Institute, said: "If genetic background influences both cancer risk and the evolutionary trajectory of tumors, future cancer prevention and screening strategies will need to take into account inherited genetics and population diversity. "Similarly, how people respond to cancer drugs is likely to differ depending on their inherited genetics, and so we may need to tailor our diagnostics and treatments accordingly." This study gives us a fascinating hint that our inherited genes might have a big influence on the way that cancers develop after DNA damage. We still need to see more research before we can understand what this means in humans, but this finding could change our understanding of how cancer starts, and lead to more powerful and precise ways of tackling cancer." Dr. Sam Godfrey, research information lead, Cancer Research UK The research was largely funded by Cancer Research UK, the Medical Research Council, European Research Council and Wellcome.

AI Translation
科学家们首次发现直接证据，表明我们的基因构成在影响癌症风险方面发挥着强大作用，其中遗传基因与获得性基因突变相互作用，共同塑造肿瘤的演变方式。 这些发现可能解释了为什么在相同环境下，有些人比其他人更容易患癌症，并提示未来的癌症预防和筛查策略可能需要更仔细地考虑遗传基因学和人群多样性。 这项研究今天发表在《自然》杂志上，并在小鼠身上进行，它表明对DNA损伤性癌症治疗的反应可能因患者的背景遗传而异，这进一步强化了采取更个性化治疗方法的理由。 当我们的DNA积累错误或突变时，肿瘤就会产生，导致细胞快速增殖并忽视那些原本会指令受损细胞在造成危害前凋亡的信号。环境暴露——例如，香烟烟雾或阳光——会影响DNA损伤的程度，而遗传性基因改变则可能改变突变积累的数量。 然而，并非所有暴露于相同环境风险因素的人都会罹患癌症。大多数吸烟者不会患肺癌——而有些不吸烟者却会患肺癌。其原因几乎肯定与我们的遗传基因构成有关，但在患者队列研究中找到直接证据已被证明具有挑战性。尽管许多研究表明遗传基因差异会影响癌症风险，但要证明这种关联一直很困难，因为人类群体内部和群体之间在生活方式、环境和暴露史方面存在差异。 这项研究是剑桥大学、爱丁堡大学以及欧洲和美国其他机构多年国际合作的成果，由邓肯·奥多姆教授、莎拉·艾特肯博士和马丁·泰勒教授共同领导。 在主要于剑桥大学英国癌症研究中心（CRUK）剑桥研究所进行的实验中，科学家们开发了一种实验方法，使他们能够实验性地控制环境因素，并表明遗传背景本身可以改变肿瘤的发生和演变方式。 研究团队培育了四种对肝癌易感性不同的小鼠品系，其基因多样性水平与人类群体中观察到的相似。然后，他们让小鼠暴露于单剂量的肝脏致癌物二乙基亚硝胺（DEN）。DEN存在于烟草烟雾和一些加工食品中，已知会引起肝细胞DNA损伤，导致可能启动肿瘤生长的突变。 由于每只小鼠在相同年龄（15日龄）和受控条件下接受了相同剂量的暴露，研究人员能够消除在人类研究中造成混淆的环境变异。 随后，他们对近600个肿瘤的基因组进行了测序，分析了其发展出的基因活性，并检查了未经治疗的小鼠，以比较不同品系间的自发性肿瘤形成。利用这些数据，他们能够重构每个肿瘤如何从其原始致癌突变演变而来。 在所有小鼠品系中，癌症几乎总是获得了一种激活相同促癌信号系统——即MAPK通路——的癌症驱动突变。MAPK通路是控制细胞生长和细胞分化等重要生命过程的多步骤分子信号级联，在多种癌症类型中发挥着关键作用。 然而，根据小鼠的遗传基因学背景，所获得的特定驱动突变改变了其他癌症相关信号通路的活性，并引起了显著的全基因组复制倾向，即染色体整套倍增的事件。 资深作者邓肯·奥多姆教授在英国癌症研究中心剑桥研究所期间领导了这项研究，现任职于德国海德堡的DKFZ（德国癌症研究中心）。他表示：“癌症并非完全偶然发生。尽管肿瘤常常达到相同的生物学终点，但通往该终点的路径是由个体的遗传背景决定的。” “我们首次能够展示遗传背景对突变过程和导致肿瘤发展的通路影响的程度。” 研究人员表示，这些发现对癌症筛查和精准医疗具有重要意义。 第一作者、耶鲁大学医学院助理教授莎拉·艾特肯博士（她也曾在英国癌症研究中心剑桥研究所参与这项研究）表示：“如果遗传背景影响癌症风险和肿瘤的演变轨迹，那么未来的癌症预防和筛查策略将需要考虑遗传基因学和人群多样性。” “同样，人们对癌症药物的反应也可能因其遗传基因学背景而异，因此我们可能需要相应地调整我们的诊断和治疗。” 英国癌症研究中心研究信息负责人萨姆·戈弗雷博士说：“这项研究为我们提供了一个引人入胜的线索，表明我们的遗传基因可能对DNA损伤后癌症的发展方式产生巨大影响。我们还需要更多的研究才能理解这在人类中意味着什么，但这一发现可能会改变我们对癌症发生机制的理解，并带来更强大、更精确的癌症应对方法。” 这项研究主要由英国癌症研究中心、医学研究理事会、欧洲研究理事会和威康基金会资助。
