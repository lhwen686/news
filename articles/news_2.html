New research to be presented today at the annual conference of the European Society of Human Genetics shows that a cancer patient's genetic ancestry can have a significant effect both on how their disease progresses and their survival. In the largest study of its kind, researchers examined nearly 1,900 specific genetic changes in tumors to measure whether certain mutations were more common in patients with different historic geographic origins.

Presenting their results, Dr. Yixuan He, assistant professor of epidemiology at the University of Texas Health Science Center, Houston, explained how she worked with her Ph.D. student, Jiawei Tu, to analyze the genetic sequencing data from more than 30,000 patients from two large cancer centers—Dana Farber (Boston, Massachusetts) and MD Anderson (Houston). The data covered five different cancers: breast, colorectal, glioma (the most common type of brain cancer), pancreas and lung.

As well as examining mutations, the researchers also took socioeconomic status and air pollution environmental factors into account to make sure they did not distort the results. They then created a scoring system based on the genetic mutations to try to predict which patients were more likely to die from their cancer, and tested whether combining ancestry information improved the prediction.

Although prediction scoring has been done before, this is the first analysis of its kind to be carried out on such a scale. "Previous studies have been limited to small groups in a single population and in a single tumor," He said, "and they have often not taken environmental factors or long-term clinical outcomes into account. By broadening the scope of our study, we hoped to be able to show the real, measurable impact of genetic ancestry on cancer genomics and clinical outcomes."

Results showed dozens of mutations that were significantly more or less common depending on the patient's ancestry, about half of which can be targeted by existing treatments. The scoring system was able to predict patient survival, particularly in breast cancer and glioma. When ancestry information was added, the survival prediction became even more accurate, particularly in pancreatic cancer.

Tumor sequencing is relatively common in modern cancer care, and genetic ancestry can be estimated from those data, so integrating it would not involve additional costs or tests. Environmental factors can also be estimated simply based on where a patient lives. "The challenge of integration would not come from a lack of appropriate technology, but from a workflow that allows the derivation of these important factors from existing routine data collection," He said. "We are currently collaborating with oncologists in the hope of overcoming these barriers."

The researchers now intend to expand their analyses to include other cancers and to incorporate additional environmental factors such as smoking and other pollutants—ideally, working with other groups to replicate their findings in other patient cohorts. Although some of their conclusions were already known, they were also able to identify several new associations. For example, the overrepresentation (enrichment) of a gene involved in the control of cell proliferation (CDK6) in African American breast cancer patients, and the loss of SMAD2, another cell proliferation-controlling gene, in American colorectal cancer patients with an admixed ancestry.

"It was very encouraging to see consistent ancestry-related signals replicated between our two different biobanks despite the geographic and population differences between them. By identifying specific genetic markers linked to ancestry, we can pinpoint targetable mutations to help doctors use treatments with better survival outcomes. In validating these signals across different populations, we can ensure that a particular treatment is adapted to and effective across a diverse range of patients," He concluded.

Chair of the conference, Professor Alexandre Reymond, who was not involved in the research, said, "This study shows convincingly the need to assess the disease risks in diverse populations if we are to be able to fully personalize medicine and help the maximum number of patients."

今天将在欧洲人类遗传学学会年会上公布的一项新研究表明，癌症患者的遗传血统对其疾病进展和生存率均有显著影响。在这项同类规模最大的研究中，研究人员检测了近1,900种特定的肿瘤基因变异，以评估某些突变在具有不同历史地理起源的患者中是否更为常见。

德克萨斯大学休斯顿健康科学中心流行病学助理教授Yixuan He博士在介绍研究结果时解释说，她如何与她的博士生Jiawei Tu合作，分析了来自两家大型癌症中心——达纳法伯（波士顿，马萨诸塞州）和MD安德森（休斯顿）的30,000多名患者的基因测序数据。这些数据涵盖了五种不同的癌症：乳腺癌、结直肠癌、胶质瘤（最常见的脑癌类型）、胰腺癌和肺癌。

除了检测突变，研究人员还考虑了社会经济地位和空气污染等环境因素，以确保它们不会扭曲研究结果。随后，他们基于基因突变创建了一个评分系统，旨在预测哪些患者更可能死于癌症，并测试了结合血统信息是否能提高预测准确性。

尽管预测评分以前也曾进行过，但这是首次在如此大规模上进行的此类分析。“以往的研究仅限于单个群体和单个肿瘤中的小规模组，”He博士说，“并且通常没有考虑环境因素或长期临床结局。通过扩大我们研究的范围，我们希望能展示遗传血统对癌症基因组学和临床结局的真实、可衡量的影响。”

结果显示，数十种突变在患者血统不同时，其常见程度显著更高或更低，其中约一半可以通过现有治疗进行靶向。该评分系统能够预测患者的生存率，尤其是在乳腺癌和胶质瘤中。当加入血统信息后，生存预测变得更加准确，特别是在胰腺癌中。

肿瘤测序在现代癌症护理中相对普遍，并且可以从这些数据中估算出遗传血统，因此整合这些信息不会涉及额外的成本或检测。环境因素也可以根据患者的居住地简单估算。“整合的挑战并非来自缺乏适当的技术，而是来自需要一个工作流程，使我们能够从现有的常规数据收集中推导出这些重要因素，”He博士说。“我们目前正在与肿瘤学家合作，希望能克服这些障碍。”

研究人员现在打算扩展他们的分析，以纳入其他癌症并结合额外的环境因素，如吸烟和其他污染物——理想情况下，是与其他团队合作，在其他患者队列中重复他们的发现。尽管他们的一些结论已经为人所知，但他们也识别出了一些新的关联。例如，在非洲裔美国乳腺癌患者中，一个参与细胞增殖控制的基因（CDK6）过度表达（富集）；而在具有混合血统的美国结直肠癌患者中，另一个细胞增殖控制基因SMAD2的缺失。

“尽管我们的两个不同生物样本库之间存在地理和人群差异，但看到一致的血统相关信号在两者之间得到复制，这非常令人鼓舞。通过识别与血统相关的特定遗传标记，我们可以精确定位可靶向突变，以帮助医生使用具有更好生存结果的治疗方法。通过在不同人群中验证这些信号，我们可以确保特定治疗方案能适应并有效地作用于多样化的患者群体，”He博士总结道。

未参与这项研究的会议主席Alexandre Reymond教授表示：“这项研究令人信服地表明，如果要充分实现个体化医疗并帮助尽可能多的患者，就需要评估不同人群中的疾病风险。”
