Pro soccer players show signs of shrinking brains

深度解析

本研究揭示了职业足球运动中反复头部撞击与中期脑健康问题之间的潜在关联。核心医学机制在于，反复的亚临床或临床头部撞击可能导致神经元损伤和胶质细胞活化，进而引发神经炎症级联反应，加速灰质萎缩，特别是影响记忆、注意力和情绪调节等关键脑区。这与慢性创伤性脑病（CTE）的病理生理学基础相似，即tau蛋白病变等异常蛋白质累积，最终导致神经退行性改变。本研究观察到的脑容量减少、抑郁和焦虑症状以及思维决策困难，均是神经退行性疾病的早期或中期表现。 其行业影响深远。首先，它对职业体育界提出了严肃的健康警示，促使足球管理机构重新审视训练和比赛规则，考虑限制头球的频率和强度，或开发更安全的头盔保护技术。其次，对球员个人而言，这些发现强调了早期筛查和长期监测的重要性，以识别高风险个体并提供干预。此外，这项研究也将推动神经科学和运动医学领域对头部撞击损伤机制的深入探索，促进生物标志物的发现和预防策略的创新。最终目标是平衡竞技体育的魅力与运动员的长期健康福祉。

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: World Cup fever has America in its grip as the international soccer tournament grinds steadily toward the final. But a new study highlights a darker side to the sport: the toll soccer can take on the brains of professional players. Middle-aged former pro soccer players appear to have more shrinkage in key brain regions than people who have never played contact sports, researchers report at the Alzheimer's Association International Conference in London. "These findings suggest there may be measurable effects on brain health in former elite soccer players even in midlife, before clinically apparent neurodegenerative disease would typically emerge," lead author Caleigh Grace Lynch, a research technician at Imperial College London in the UK, said in a news release. The retired players also tended to have more symptoms of depression and anxiety and reported difficulties with thinking and decision-making, researchers said. "While we did not find significant differences in objective cognitive testing between the groups, we did observe important differences in symptoms and brain structure," Lynch said. Repetitive head impacts occur in soccer, mainly due to heading—hitting the ball with one's own head to direct its movement—researchers said. In other sports, these repetitive impacts have been linked to chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE), a degenerative brain disease linked to concussions and head trauma. For the new study, researchers compared 142 former pro soccer players with a group of 56 people who had served in the military but had no history of repetitive head impacts or contact sports. The players included 126 men who had held a full-time pro contract for at least three years and 16 women who had competed in pro soccer in the UK. All were 30–60 years old. Results showed that nearly a third, 31%, of the former soccer players had clinically significant depression symptoms, compared with 9% of the control group. Likewise, 42% of soccer players had serious anxiety symptoms, compared with 25% of controls. MRI brain images of the players revealed shrinking gray matter in several brain regions that play important roles in memory, attention, decision-making and emotional regulation, researchers said. Overall, the players had lower brain volume than the control group. Researchers plan to keep tracking soccer players over time to see whether brain volume loss results in symptoms of CTE, dementia or Alzheimer's disease. "By following participants over time, we hope to better understand how repetitive head impacts may influence long-term brain health and neurodegenerative disease, and help inform strategies to make sports safer for future generations," senior researcher Dr. Thomas Parker, a clinical lecturer and consultant neurologist at Imperial College London, said in a news release. "Research like this helps us better understand brain health factors across the lifespan and reinforces the importance of injury prevention and monitoring," Maria Carrillo, the Alzheimer's Association's chief science officer and medical affairs lead, said in a news release. "These findings can help players, physicians and sports organizations better understand the risks of contact sports and how to participate safely," added Carrillo, who was not involved in the study.
