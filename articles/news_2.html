再编程肿瘤微环境有望改善胰腺癌的治疗前景

Reprogramming tumor microenvironment could improve prospects for treatment of pancreatic cancer

深度解析

胰腺癌因其高度侵袭性和对传统疗法的抵抗而臭名昭著，这很大程度上归因于其独特的肿瘤微环境（TME）。本研究深入揭示了TME中的关键角色——肿瘤相关成纤维细胞（CAFs）——如何通过形成致密瘢痕样组织（细胞外基质）和释放免疫抑制性细胞因子来构建物理屏障并逃避免疫监视。文章的核心机制在于CD44分子对CAFs活性的调节作用。CD44的活化会增强CAFs的促肿瘤功能，使其分泌更多基质并抑制局部免疫反应。研究通过在小鼠模型和人类细胞中使CD44失活，展示了其关键的治疗潜力。CD44失活导致CAFs形态和功能发生显著改变，它们产生的细胞外基质和分泌的细胞因子减少，同时失去了免疫抑制能力。这一“重塑”或“再教育”CAFs的策略，使得肿瘤微环境从免疫抑制转向免疫激活，从而增强了免疫细胞对肿瘤的攻击，导致肿瘤尺寸明显缩小。这一发现对肿瘤治疗领域具有深远影响。传统的癌症治疗多聚焦于直接杀伤肿瘤细胞，但此研究提出了通过调节肿瘤微环境，尤其是CAFs，来间接抗癌的新范式。这种“再教育”而非“破坏”CAFs的方法，有望开发出更有效且副作用更小的治疗方案，尤其对胰腺癌这类免疫疗法效果不佳的癌症。结合此前已有的针对CD44v6的临床研究，该工作为靶向CD44的联合疗法提供了新的理论基础和应用前景，有望克服胰腺癌对治疗的抵抗，改善患者预后。

Credit: Treffert et al./KIT Pancreatic cancer is one of the most aggressive and difficult-to-treat cancers. One reason for its resistance to therapy is the dense tissue surrounding the tumor that acts as a protective barrier and suppresses the immune response. It is a complex tissue made of supporting cells, blood vessels and immune cells—the tumor microenvironment. In pancreatic cancer, this surrounding tissue accounts for up to 90% of the tumor mass. Of particular importance are cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), mutated connective tissue cells surrounding the tumor. They stimulate its growth and spread by growing a scar-like tissue structure and suppressing the body's own immune response. As a result, many immunotherapies that help with other forms of cancer are often ineffective. Researchers at the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology (KIT) have now identified a molecular mechanism that regulates this tumor-supporting environment. At the heart of this mechanism is CD44, a molecule that controls the behavior of cancer-associated fibroblasts, specialized connective tissue cells that play a crucial role in shaping the tumor microenvironment. Their research is published in Cell Death & Disease. Key role of the CD44 molecule In their study, the researchers focused on the role of the CD44 molecule in cancer-associated fibroblasts. They demonstrated that CD44 intensifies important signals that activate these cells. In the laboratory, they inactivated CD44 in the mouse model as well as in human cells. "This changed the fibroblasts dramatically. They produced less extracellular matrix, released fewer cytokines and lost their ability to suppress the immune system. As a result, immune cells could attack the tumor cells more successfully, which significantly decreased the tumor size in a mouse model," said professor Véronique Orian-Rousseau, head of the Cancer Cell Plasticity, Tumor Microenvironment and Metastasis research group at KIT's Institute of Biological and Chemical Systems—Functional Molecular Systems (IBCS-FMS). "The fact that we can change fibroblasts in a targeted manner by inactivating CD44 instead of destroying them opens up new prospects for more effective and less disruptive cancer treatments." Potential new treatment approaches The study ties in with a therapy approach against CD44 already pursued at KIT. Earlier work had shown that blocking certain CD44 variants on cancer cells can slow down the progression of pancreatic cancer. Based on this finding, the researchers developed a peptide directed at CD44v6, which has already been tested in a clinical study. The current results suggest that blocking CD44 in the supporting tissue of tumors could also be therapeutically promising. However, further research will be needed to develop a clinical application. In addition to the KIT scientists, researchers from TRON at the University Medical Center of Johannes Gutenberg University Mainz and researchers from the University of Freiburg also took part in the study. More information Sven Máté Treffert et al., Loss of CD44 re-educates pancreatic cancer-associated fibroblasts modulating their fibrotic and immunosuppressive functions, Cell Death & Disease (2026).
