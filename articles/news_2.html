New gene-editing platform gives doctors control over cell therapies

深度解析

本研究的核心专业概念在于解决了细胞和基因疗法在临床应用中的一个关键挑战：如何在需要全身性免疫抑制的患者体内精准控制经基因编辑的治疗性细胞。传统的细胞疗法在回输后，临床医生难以选择性地增强或抑制这些细胞；而患者所需的免疫抑制药物往往会无差别地抑制体内所有免疫细胞，包括有益的治疗性细胞，从而影响疗效。 该研究的创新机制在于利用引导编辑（prime editing）这一高精度基因编辑技术，赋予治疗性T细胞选择性的药物耐药性。具体而言，研究人员通过基因工程使T细胞对他克莫司（一种常用免疫抑制剂）产生耐药，同时保留对环孢素等其他免疫抑制剂的敏感性。这创造了一个“控制旋钮”：医生可以安全地使用他克莫司来抑制患者自身的免疫系统，而不损害经过改造的治疗性T细胞。一旦需要调节或“回拨”治疗性细胞的活性，环孢素仍可作为备选方案。 该平台的行业影响是深远的。通过将现有、临床医生熟悉的药物整合到细胞疗法方案中，它显著降低了临床转化的门槛，使得先进的细胞疗法比需要全新药物-细胞疗法组合的方法更具实用性和可扩展性。它拓宽了细胞疗法对更广泛患者群体的适用性，包括那些具有复杂免疫背景的患者，从而加速了这些挽救生命疗法在常规临床实践中的采纳和商业化，代表了可控细胞疗法在实现更安全、更有效的精准医疗方面迈出了重要一步。

英文原文

Original
An Australian clinician-led research team has developed a gene-editing platform that could give doctors more control over cell therapies, making them more practical for patients with complex immune conditions. The new approach using prime editing could allow clinicians to use existing immune-suppressing drugs as a 'control dial' for cell therapies and help corrected cells survive in the body. The WEHI-led research, published in Blood, aims to make cell and gene therapies more feasible for people who need ongoing immunosuppression, including those with rare immune disorders, organ or stem cell transplant recipients and patients receiving cell therapies for cancer. At a glance Researchers have turned existing medicines into a potential control system for future cell therapies. Published in Blood, the study shows the novel gene-editing platform could help therapeutic T cells survive in patients who still need immunosuppression. Because it uses common drugs, the platform may be more practical to translate into the clinic than strategies requiring entirely new medicines. Making cell therapies controllable Cell and gene therapies often involve taking a patient's immune cells, correcting or engineering them in the lab, and returning them to the body. But once those cells are back in the patient, clinicians have limited ways to selectively boost or suppress them. This is particularly challenging for people who need ongoing immunosuppression, including some patients with rare immune disorders, transplants, severe infections or cancers linked to immune suppression. The drugs needed to control disease or protect a transplanted organ can also suppress the therapeutic cells that the patient needs. Co-senior author Dr Miles Horton said the team used prime editing, an emerging gene-editing technology that can make precise DNA changes, to address a practical problem seen in clinical care. We've used genetic engineering to rewire these cells so clinicians could use existing drugs, already sitting in the hospital pharmacy, as a control dial. Some approaches being explored elsewhere would require doctors to adopt a new cell therapy and a new drug at the same time. Our aim was to build a platform around clinical reality, using medicines that clinicians already know and use." Dr. Miles Horton, a WEHI researcher and pediatrics resident, Royal Children's Hospital The team showed T cells could be made resistant to a commonly used immunosuppressive drug (tacrolimus), while remaining sensitive to a related alternative (cyclosporin). This precise 'control dial' could allow clinicians to use one drug to help corrected cells survive while other immune cells are suppressed, while preserving another drug option if the edited cells behaved unexpectedly and needed to be wound back. Built for clinical reality The study focused on a rare and serious immune-related blood cancer that can affect children and young adults, subcutaneous panniculitis-like T-cell lymphoma with HLH, or SPTCL-HLH. Using cells from people with the disease, researchers used prime editing to correct a disease-causing mutation and add a second edit that made the corrected cells resistant to the drug tacrolimus. The same platform was then tested in other types of cell therapy, including virus-specific T cells that could one day help transplant recipients fight infection without stopping anti-rejection drugs, and CAR T cells used in cancer treatment. Co-senior author Dr Tom Lew said the work was designed around clinical needs. "As clinicians, we are always thinking about whether a promising technology could realistically fit into patient care," said Dr Lew, a WEHI researcher who is also a clinical hematologist at Peter MacCallum Cancer Centre (Peter Mac) and the Royal Melbourne Hospital (RMH). "Our approach is built around medicines that are already part of routine clinical practice, which could make translation more practical." First author Dr Esther Bandala-Sanchez said the platform was designed to be precise and adaptable. "We showed this strategy could be applied across different types of therapeutic T cells, not just one disease setting," Dr Bandala-Sanchez said. "The edited cells stayed functional under immunosuppression, and importantly, remained sensitive to other drugs. "That precision is what makes the platform so promising as a foundation for future cell therapies." The team is now working toward a first-in-human trial. The research, involving collaborators from WEHI, Peter Mac, RMH, Westmead Hospital, the University of Sydney and Austin Health, was supported by the National Health and Medical Research Council, Victorian State Government, the Jack Brockhoff Foundation, Ramaciotti Foundation and Watt Mirisklavos Foundation.
