大麻二酚对治疗智力障碍儿童的严重行为问题无效

Cannabidiol not effective for treating severe behavioral problems in children with intellectual disabilities

深度解析

这项全球首创的随机对照试验明确指出，大麻二酚（CBD）在治疗智力残疾儿童的严重行为问题（如易怒、攻击性、自伤等）方面缺乏疗效。此研究的临床意义深远，特别是在当前CBD产品作为“天然”替代疗法受到广泛追捧且市场价值数十亿美元的背景下。尽管试验发现CBD的不良反应通常轻微且不常见，但这无法弥补其在核心疗效指标上的不足。 此研究强调了随机对照试验作为评估治疗有效性“黄金标准”的不可替代性。对于智力残疾儿童群体，现有药物如抗精神病药和精神兴奋剂往往效果有限且伴随显著不良反应，使得家长们积极寻求安全有效的替代方案。本研究的结果明确揭示，CBD并非此类行为问题的有效干预措施。这不仅有助于避免家庭因无效治疗而产生的经济负担和机会成本，更关键的是纠正了公众对CBD疗效的普遍误解，并警示临床医生和家庭在选择干预措施时必须坚持循证医学原则。未来的研究应继续探索针对智力残疾儿童行为问题的更有效、更安全的治疗策略，并将资源投入到具备确凿证据的干预措施中，以真正改善患者的生活质量。

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: Credit: Pixabay/CC0 Public Domain A world-first randomized controlled trial has found that cannabidiol—commonly referred to as CBD—is not effective in treating severe behavioral problems in children with intellectual disability. Published in JAMA Pediatrics, the Australian-led trial found that CBD was not effective in treating severe behavioral problems, such as anxiety, sleep disturbance, irritability, aggression, self-injury or elopement (wandering or running away), in children with intellectual disability aged between 6 and 18 years, as reported by parents using a standardized checklist. It found that, in this cohort, adverse effects associated with taking CBD are mild and infrequent. Excessive drowsiness, insomnia, diarrhea and decreased appetite were the most commonly reported. Cannabidiol is a non-intoxicating compound extracted from the cannabis or hemp plant that does not contain tetrahydrocannabinol, the compound which causes a 'high.' It has become a popular product worldwide represented by a multibillion-dollar industry. Until now, there have been few studies focused on CBD for severe behavioral problems in children with intellectual disability, and no randomized controlled trials—the gold standard for assessing whether a treatment is effective. The study was led by Murdoch Children's Research Institute in Melbourne and included researchers from the University of Sydney and Monash University. Elizabeth Elliott, professor of pediatrics and child health at the University of Sydney and consultant pediatrician at the Sydney Children's Hospitals Network, recruited, treated and assessed the Sydney cohort. She said, "Many children with intellectual disability experience severe behavioral problems, including irritability, aggression and self-injury. Some parents tell us they spend a significant amount on CBD in an effort to help their children, despite a lack of evidence for its effectiveness. "CBD has recently become a popular alternative therapy, given that many medications, including antipsychotics, psychostimulants and antidepressants, are common in this group with limited impact and a risk of adverse effects. "Many parents hoped CBD would be a solution, given its low risk of adverse effects and the perception that it is a more natural option. However, our study showed that CBD is ineffective in treating these behavioral problems in children with intellectual disability. "Rigorous testing of alternative therapies, such as CBD, is essential, both to avoid creating false hope and to ensure resources are directed toward developing and funding access to effective treatments." Monash University professor Katrina Williams said, "We wanted to investigate a therapy that might help the many families with a child or young person whose behavior creates risks and challenges in everyday life. We know existing interventions can be costly, time-consuming or have side effects that many people find difficult to tolerate. "Despite early promise, cannabidiol is not an effective intervention for these families, children and young people. It is important to establish this, so families can avoid the opportunity costs and financial burden of cannabidiol treatment, and efforts can instead focus on alternative ways to support them." While 84% of the 131 participants were diagnosed with autism, the study did not focus on any one particular genetic or developmental condition. Instead, it focused on children with intellectual disability associated with a range of diagnoses and behavioral difficulties. In the United States, several studies have documented families using CBD to manage behavioral and psychological difficulties in children with autism and other neurodevelopmental conditions, despite limited clinical evidence. In a 2023 survey, nearly one-third of parents of children with ADHD, autism and/or anxiety had given their child unlicensed CBD products. Another study of U.S. health records suggests that about one-third of children receiving pharmaceutical CBD may be receiving it without a recorded FDA-approved indication. How the trial worked The study was a parallel-design, double-blind, placebo-controlled randomized clinical trial conducted over four years, between 2021 and 2025. It involved 131 children and adolescents aged between 6 and 18 years, diagnosed with intellectual disability and severe behavioral problems. Participants were recruited from three children's hospitals: the Royal Children's Hospital in Melbourne, Monash Children's Hospital in Melbourne and The Children's Hospital at Westmead in Sydney. Of the participants, 25.2% were female and 74.8% were male. Participants were randomly assigned to receive either CBD isolate in oil (the purest form available, dosed at 10 mg per kilogram per day) or a placebo orally for eight weeks, followed by a four-week follow-up period. Parents reported scores on the Aberrant Behavior Checklist Irritability (ABC-I) subscale, which were assessed at the end of the eight-week maintenance phase. Earlier this year, a major University of Sydney-led study found no evidence to suggest medicinal cannabis is effective for depression, anxiety or PTSD in adults. Medicinal cannabis is an umbrella term that refers to both CBD and THC products. Publication details Adi Aran et al., Cannabidiol for Disruptive Behavior—The Verdict Is In, JAMA Pediatrics (2026).
