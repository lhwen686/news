极重度长新冠与肺部和口腔微生物组差异相关联

Very severe long COVID linked to differences in lung and mouth microbiomes

深度解析

这篇研究深入揭示了长期新冠患者口腔和肺部微生物组的独特变化，即其细菌组成与未患严重长期新冠的个体存在显著差异。核心专业概念在于“微生物组失衡”（dysbiosis），指特定部位微生物群落的结构和功能紊乱。研究发现，这种失衡不仅关联呼吸功能障碍，还与“脑雾”等神经认知症状及胃肠道问题紧密相关。值得注意的是，产乳酸菌的过度增殖与炎症迹象同步，提示微生物组失衡可能通过调控宿主免疫和炎症通路，影响长期新冠的病理生理。 这项发现对临床医学意义重大。它为长期新冠的致病机制提供了新的探索方向，将微生物组作为疾病发生发展的重要参与者。若未来研究能确证微生物组失衡的因果关系及具体机制，则有望为长期新冠的诊断和治疗策略带来革新。例如，通过靶向干预微生物组（如特异性抗生素、益生菌或粪菌移植），可能调控炎症反应或改善受损器官功能，从而开发出更精准有效的治疗方案，以应对这一全球性健康难题。

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: Credit: Pixabay/CC0 Public Domain People with long COVID have distinct differences in the mix of bacteria that live in their mouths and lungs, according to a study of more than 700 people published in the European Respiratory Journal. The research also shows that these differences in the lung microbiome are linked to respiratory symptoms, as well as the cognitive and digestive problems known to occur in long COVID. Researchers say their findings may shed light on why some people develop long COVID and could point to new ways to treat the condition. The lead author of the study was Dr. Daniela Alferes de Lima Headley from the University of Dundee, U.K. She said, "We know that millions of people worldwide are still affected by long COVID and its cause is still unknown. Long COVID affects multiple organs. "The microbiome is important to maintain health throughout the body, so it is a strong candidate for something that may contribute to long COVID. Prior studies have shown some changes in the gut microbiome with long COVID, but COVID is a lung disease and so it makes sense to look to see if the lung microbiome is changed in long COVID." Comparing microbes across symptom groups This research is part of PHOSP-COVID and included 771 people who had been treated for COVID-19 between February 2020 and March 2021 at one of 38 U.K. hospitals. Researchers divided the participants into four groups based on how severe their symptoms were five months after being discharged from the hospital. These were "very severe," with serious physical, mental and cognitive impairments; "severe," with serious mental and physical impairments but no cognitive problems; "moderate," with primarily cognitive difficulties; and "mild," with few symptoms and good recovery. Six months after patients were discharged, researchers collected saliva samples from their mouths and sputum samples from their lungs and used DNA sequencing to identify which microbes were present. Researchers analyzed the variety and quantities of the microbes and compared these with patients' symptoms. Microbiome differences linked to multiple symptoms This showed that people with very severe long COVID had clear differences in the microbiome in their mouths and lungs compared with people who did not have severe long COVID. It also showed that there were differences linked to specific symptoms, including reduced lung function (meaning your lungs cannot hold as much air as normal or air cannot flow in and out of your lungs as easily as normal), neurocognitive symptoms (including "brain fog," difficulties with memory, attention and concentration) and gut symptoms (including pain, nausea, diarrhea and loss of appetite). Among the changes associated with long COVID symptoms was an overabundance of bacteria that produce lactic acid. This was also linked to signs of inflammation found in samples of patients' blood. Cause and recovery remain uncertain The study was led by James Chalmers, chair of respiratory research at the University of Dundee, U.K. He said, "People who had the worst symptoms five to six months after COVID infection had big changes in the microbiome in their mouth and lungs. We also found that changes in the airway microbiome were linked not just to lung problems, but also to symptoms like brain fog and gut symptoms, suggesting the lung microbiome could have effects on health more broadly. "What we don't know is whether these changes came before a COVID infection, or whether the changes happened following a COVID infection. We also do not know what will happen to the microbiome in patients in subsequent years. We would love to know whether these changes get better with time. "We urgently need to know what causes long COVID because it remains a major health care problem. Understanding what causes it could lead to better treatments; the microbiome can be changed using antibiotics, probiotics or other measures. We hope our findings will stimulate more research into long COVID and in the future, we would love to see treatment trials to help people with this devastating condition." PHOSP-COVID is the U.K.'s flagship long COVID project, and its researchers are continuing to investigate the burden and management of long COVID. For example, researchers are studying the role of inflammation in long COVID and what kind of treatments might be able to reverse it. Further research needed before clinical use Raquel Duarte is head of the European Respiratory Society's assembly on infectious diseases, based at the University of Porto, Portugal, and was not involved in the research. She said, "Millions of people worldwide continue to live with the disabling consequences of long COVID. For some, persistent respiratory, cognitive and other symptoms affect their ability to work, study or manage everyday activities, with consequences not only for patients but also for their families, health services and society. "Six years after the start of the pandemic, we still do not fully understand why some people develop long COVID or how best to support their recovery. This large, multicenter study identifies distinct patterns in the oral and airway microbiota of people with the most severe symptoms following hospitalization for COVID-19. "Importantly, these patterns were associated not only with respiratory impairment, but also with cognitive and gastrointestinal symptoms. "These findings provide an important basis for further longitudinal and mechanistic research. If these associations are confirmed, they may ultimately help us identify new ways to predict, prevent or treat the most disabling forms of long COVID." Publication details Sputum and oral microbiota profiles are associated with long COVID severity and phenotype post-hospitalisation: a UK multicentre, prospective cohort study, European Respiratory Journal (2026).
