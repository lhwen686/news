英文原文

Original
Children with intellectual disabilities face greater mental health difficulties when they are from a disadvantaged background, finds a new study led by UCL researchers. The study, published in JCPP Advances, found that children and young people with intellectual developmental disabilities (IDD) from more socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds experience greater emotional and behavioral difficulties and are more likely to report mental health conditions. Lead author, Honorary Research Fellow Irene O Lee (UCL Great Ormond Street Institute of Child Health), said: "Children with intellectual developmental disabilities can face significant mental health challenges, and families experiencing socioeconomic disadvantage may have additional difficulties in accessing the support they need. "Our findings highlight the importance of looking beyond a child's disability and recognizing how family circumstances may be associated with their mental health. Improving access to appropriate mental health and social care support is an important step towards reducing these inequalities." The research team examined 2,778 children and young people between the ages of four and 25, across the UK from the UK-wide 'Intellectual Disability and Mental Health: Assessing the Genomic Impact on Neurodevelopment' (IMAGINE-ID) cohort, who had intellectual developmental disabilities caused by genetic conditions - such as 16p11.2 microdeletion syndrome, Prader-Willi or Angelman syndrome - some of whom had inherited variations. Parents or caregivers completed detailed questionnaires about their child's health, development and behavior. Meanwhile, socioeconomic disadvantage was measured using three main indicators: neighborhood deprivation (according to the Index of Multiple Deprivation), eligibility for free school meals and parental education level. The researchers then linked the questionnaire responses with the measures of deprivation to examine how social disadvantage relates to mental health outcomes. They found that children with IDD who were living in more deprived neighborhoods, those eligible for free school meals, and those whose parents had lower levels of education were more likely to experience emotional and behavioral difficulties (such as low mood, stress-related physical symptoms, conduct problems or peer relationship problems). They were also more likely to have mental health conditions including depression, anxiety, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and conduct disorder. Children eligible for free school meals were 2.5 times as likely to have a reported diagnosis of major depression and 2.3 times as likely to have conduct disorder as children who were not eligible. The researchers found no clear pattern showing that children with more severe learning disabilities experienced greater mental health difficulties. Instead, socioeconomic disadvantage was consistently associated with poorer mental health outcomes. Our evidence reinforces the necessity of recognizing the wider challenges faced by families caring for children with intellectual developmental disabilities. Financial pressures, caring responsibilities and difficulties accessing specialist support may add to the challenges these families experience, although this study could not establish the reasons behind the associations." Emeritus Professor David Skuse, Senior Author, UCL Great Ormond Street Institute of Child Health The IMAGINE-ID study also involved researchers from Cardiff University. It was funded by the Medical Research Council (MRC). Study limitations Most information came from parents rather than independent assessments, some families did not complete all questionnaires, and factors such as family circumstances, deprivation and the severity of disability could not be measured in detail. In addition, the study provides a snapshot at one point in time, so it cannot show whether disadvantage causes mental health difficulties or whether the challenges of supporting a child with intellectual and developmental disabilities can contribute to financial hardship for families.

AI Translation
智力障碍儿童若出身弱势背景，将面临更严重的精神健康问题，一项由伦敦大学学院研究人员领导的新研究发现。 这项发表在《JCPP Advances》上的研究发现，来自社会经济条件更差背景的智力发育障碍（IDD）儿童和青少年，经历更严重的情绪和行为困难，并且更有可能报告有精神健康状况。 主要作者、伦敦大学学院大奥蒙德街儿童健康研究所荣誉研究员Irene O Lee表示：“智力发育障碍儿童可能面临严重的精神健康挑战，而经历社会经济劣势的家庭在获取所需支持方面可能面临额外的困难。” “我们的研究结果强调了超越儿童残疾本身，认识到家庭环境如何可能与他们的精神健康相关的重要性。改善对适当精神健康和社会护理支持的获取，是减少这些不平等现象的重要一步。” 研究团队考察了来自英国全国性“智力障碍与精神健康：评估基因组对神经发育的影响”（IMAGINE-ID）队列的2,778名4至25岁的英国儿童和青少年。这些儿童和青少年患有由遗传疾病（如16p11.2微缺失综合征、普拉德-威利综合征或天使人综合征）引起的智力发育障碍，其中一些人存在遗传变异。 父母或照护者填写了关于孩子健康、发育和行为的详细问卷。 同时，社会经济劣势通过三个主要指标衡量：邻里剥夺程度（根据多重剥夺指数）、免费学校午餐资格以及父母教育水平。 研究人员随后将问卷回答与剥夺程度指标联系起来，以考察社会劣势如何与精神健康结果相关联。 他们发现，居住在更贫困社区、符合免费学校午餐资格以及父母教育水平较低的智力发育障碍儿童，更有可能经历情绪和行为困难（如情绪低落、压力相关的躯体症状、品行问题或同伴关系问题）。 他们也更有可能患有精神健康状况，包括抑郁症、焦虑症、注意力缺陷多动障碍（ADHD）和品行障碍。 符合免费学校午餐资格的儿童，被报告诊断出重度抑郁症的可能性是非符合资格儿童的2.5倍，被诊断出品行障碍的可能性是2.3倍。 研究人员没有发现明确的模式表明学习障碍更严重的儿童会经历更大的精神健康困难。相反，社会经济劣势始终与较差的精神健康结果相关联。 “我们的证据强化了认识到照护智力发育障碍儿童的家庭所面临的更广泛挑战的必要性。经济压力、照护责任以及难以获得专业支持可能会增加这些家庭所经历的挑战，尽管本研究未能确立这些关联背后的原因。”——伦敦大学学院大奥蒙德街儿童健康研究所资深作者、名誉教授David Skuse。 IMAGINE-ID研究还涉及了卡迪夫大学的研究人员。该研究由医学研究委员会（MRC）资助。 研究局限性 大部分信息来自父母而非独立评估，一些家庭未能完成所有问卷，并且家庭情况、贫困程度和残疾严重程度等因素无法详细衡量。 此外，该研究仅提供了一个时间点的快照，因此无法证明劣势是否导致精神健康困难，也无法证明支持智力发育障碍儿童的挑战是否可能导致家庭经济困难。
