深度解析

这项研究揭示了DNA甲基转移酶（DNMT）抑制剂，如地西他滨，在治疗实体瘤时面临耐药性的一个关键机制，并提出了克服该机制的潜在策略。核心专业概念在于细胞表观遗传学与药物代谢。DNMT抑制剂作为表观遗传药物，旨在通过逆转异常DNA甲基化来重新激活被癌细胞沉默的肿瘤抑制基因，从而恢复细胞对生长的正常调控。然而，实体瘤中普遍存在的耐药性极大地限制了其应用。 本研究确定了DCTPP1酶是导致DNMT抑制剂耐药性的一个重要酶屏障。DCTPP1的作用机制类似于“细胞粉碎机”，它在DNMT抑制剂的活性形式能够整合到DNA并发挥药效之前，将其降解。雷公藤内酯作为一种天然化合物，通过有效抑制DCTPP1，保护了DNMT抑制剂，使其能够成功整合进肿瘤DNA，抑制DNMTs，进而恢复肿瘤抑制基因的表达，最终导致癌细胞死亡。 这些发现对癌症治疗领域具有显著的行业影响。首先，它为克服先前对DNMT抑制剂无反应的癌症（尤其是实体瘤）的耐药性提供了一种合理的策略，有望拓宽其治疗范围。其次，DCTPP1被确认为一个新的、经过验证的药物靶点，为开发更高效、特异性的DCTPP1抑制剂铺平了道路，可能催生与现有表观遗传药物协同增效的新型联合疗法。尽管目前处于临床前阶段，这项工作强调了天然化合物作为药物先导物的潜力，以及理解耐药机制对于优化癌症治疗策略的重要性。

英文原文

Original
A natural plant compound may help overcome a built-in defense that allows many cancers to resist a class of drugs designed to restore normal gene activity, according to a study by researchers at the Johns Hopkins Sidney Kimmel Comprehensive Cancer Center. The researchers found that triptolide, a natural compound from Tripterygium wilfordii, commonly known as thunder god vine, blocks an enzyme called DCTPP1 that cancer cells can use to break down DNA methyltransferase inhibitors, or DNMT inhibitors, before the drugs can fully work. When researchers combined triptolide or related compounds with the DNMT inhibitor decitabine, it dramatically boosted the drug's ability to reactivate tumor suppressor genes and kill cancer cells in laboratory models. The findings, published Aug. 26 in Nature Communications, suggest a potential strategy for overcoming resistance to DNMT inhibitors and expanding their use to more types of cancer. "This gives us a potential way to remove one of the barriers that prevents these drugs from working effectively in many cancers," says senior study author Vasan Yegnasubramanian, M.D., Ph.D., professor of oncology, pathology, and radiation oncology and molecular radiation sciences at the Johns Hopkins Kimmel Cancer Center and director of inHealth Precision Medicine at Johns Hopkins Medicine. An epigenetic process called DNA methylation is one of the ways cells control which genes are turned on or off. DNMTs are enzymes that add chemical tags called methyl groups to DNA, helping regulate whether genes are on or off. Cancer cells can hijack this biological process, using abnormal DNA methylation to shut down genes that normally help prevent uncontrolled cell growth. Drugs such as decitabine and azacitidine, known as DNMT inhibitors, can help remove abnormal methylation and reactivate cancer-fighting genes. The drugs have been effective against certain blood cancers, including myelodysplastic syndromes and leukemia, but have had limited success against solid tumors, which can have built-in defenses that neutralize the therapies before they can begin to work, Yegnasubramanian explains. Researchers screened chemical libraries for compounds that could disable these defenses and resensitize cancer cells to the drugs. Among the compounds they identified was triptolide, an active ingredient found in thunder god vine. In laboratory-grown cancer cells and animal models, triptolide significantly increased both the gene-regulating and cancer-killing effects of decitabine. DNMT inhibitors such as decitabine must first be converted inside cells into an active form that can become incorporated into DNA. Once there, they interfere with enzymes that maintain DNA methylation, allowing previously silenced genes to become active again. However, DCTPP1 acts like a cellular shredder, destroying the active form of DNMT inhibitors before the drugs can be incorporated into DNA. Triptolide jams the shredder, inhibiting DCTPP1. By blocking this enzyme, the DNMT inhibitors survive, successfully integrating into the tumor DNA, allowing them to work as they are supposed to by inhibiting DNMTs, restoring the expression of crucial tumor suppressor genes and killing cancer cells." Vasan Yegnasubramanian, M.D., Ph.D., professor of oncology, pathology, and radiation oncology and molecular radiation sciences, Johns Hopkins Kimmel Cancer Center The findings build on related research from the Johns Hopkins team published earlier this year. In that study, investigators examined the atomic structure of DCTPP1 and identified several classes of small molecules specifically designed to inhibit it. When the experimental inhibitors were combined with decitabine in prostate cancer cells, the cancer cells became more sensitive to the drug. The new study reaches the same target from a different direction. Rather than starting with DCTPP1 and designing molecules to block it, researchers screened chemical libraries for compounds that enhanced decitabine's effects and discovered that triptolide did so by inhibiting DCTPP1. Researchers also found that high levels of DCTPP1 were associated with resistance to DNMT inhibitors, providing further evidence that the enzyme may be an important source of cancer drug resistance and a potential target for making these drugs more effective. "Combining these standard therapies with triptolide or its derivatives may give clinicians a rational strategy to overcome drug resistance, potentially expanding the reach of epigenetic therapy to a much broader range of cancers," says Yegnasubramanian. The researchers caution that the findings are preclinical. Their study tested triptolide and related compounds experimentally and does not establish that the herb itself is a cancer treatment. Additional research will be needed to develop DCTPP1 inhibitors with the potency, safety and drug properties needed for testing in people. In addition to co-senior authors Yegnasubramanian and William Nelson, and lead author Jianyong Liu, other researchers were Qing-Li He, Jianya Zhou, Ajay M. Vaghasia, Roshan Chikarmane, Glenn Hauk, Archana Rachakonda, Nicole Castagna, Ruchama Steinberg, Minh-Tam Pham, Jordan Gregg, Alok Mishra, Yuhan Yang, Talha Anwar, Roy Elias, Pengshan Li, Jasmine Yun-Tong Kung, Anthony O'Donnell, Nicole Anders, Teresia Wanjiku, Philipp Nuhn, Joong Sup Shim, Hugh Giovinazzo, David M. Esopi, Kunhwa Kim, Jonathan Coulter, Rulin Wang, Michelle Rudek, Kathleen Gabrielson, Angelo De Marzo, James Berger and Jun Liu. The research was supported by the National Institutes of Health/National Cancer Institute grants P50CA058236, R01CA183965, R01CA070196, P30CA006973, S10RR026824, UL1TR003098, Prostate Cancer Foundation Challenge Awards, the Patrick C. Walsh Koch Research Award, the Ellen B. Masenheimer Fellowship, the Flight Attendant Medical Research Institute and the Commonwealth Foundation. Jianyong Liu, Jun Liu, William Nelson and Srinivasan Yegnasubramanian are inventors on a provisional patent related to this work.
