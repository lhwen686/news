卒中数日后进行取栓可帮助更多患者康复，但该手术并非没有风险

Clot removal days after a stroke could help more patients recover, but the procedure is not without risks

深度解析

本研究通过LATE-MT试验，深入探讨了急性缺血性卒中患者机械性血栓切除术的治疗时间窗。传统上，该手术的最佳时间窗被限定在卒中发病后24小时内，秉持“时间就是大脑”的原则。然而，此研究的核心突破在于，它利用先进的神经影像学技术（如CT灌注或MR灌注成像），精确识别出仍存在“缺血半暗带”（即缺血但尚未坏死的脑组织），即使在发病24至72小时后，对这些经筛选的患者进行血栓切除术，仍能显著改善其90天时的功能结局，使其独立生活能力优于单独药物治疗组。 这项发现对临床实践和行业产生了深远影响。首先，它拓宽了机械性血栓切除术的治疗范围，意味着更多因各种原因延迟就医的患者，尤其是那些位于医疗资源有限地区的患者，有机会获得有效治疗。其次，该研究强调了精准影像学评估在卒中患者筛选中的关键作用，促使医院需加强其影像诊断能力和相关专业人员培训。尽管伴随颅内出血和90天死亡率增加的风险，提示临床医生在延长时间窗内实施手术时，必须进行严格的个体化风险效益评估，并权衡可挽救脑组织量与潜在并发症。总体而言，这推动了卒中治疗向更加精准化和个性化的方向发展，并可能重塑急诊卒中救治流程和资源配置。

Credit: Pexels. After a person suffers a stroke, the clock starts ticking for doctors. Every minute that blood flow to the brain remains blocked, more brain tissue risks permanent damage or death. One way to restore circulation is through endovascular thrombectomy, a minimally invasive procedure that can physically remove the clot, restore blood flow and significantly reduce the risk of long-term disability or death. Such mechanical thrombectomy procedures are a standard emergency treatment within the first 24 hours of an acute ischemic stroke. A recent study investigated a question that has long lingered in doctors' minds: Could thrombectomy still save brain tissue beyond this 24-hour window if scans showed that parts of the brain, although starved of oxygen and blood, had not yet suffered irreversible damage? The LATE-MT trial identified adult patients across 35 stroke centers in China who had suffered an ischemic stroke caused by a major blocked blood vessel. One group underwent mechanical thrombectomy between 24 and 72 hours after symptoms began alongside medication, while the other only received standard stroke medications. The researchers found that patients who received thrombectomy had significantly better overall functional recovery. About 49% of treated patients were able to live independently at 90 days, compared with 35% of those who received medication alone. The findings are published in the New England Journal of Medicine. To do or not to do thrombectomy In endovascular thrombectomy, doctors guide a thin device through the blood vessels to reach the blocked artery and physically remove the clot. They try to get on with the procedure as soon as possible, preferably within the first 24 hours after the stroke. But there might be situations when patients arrive at the hospital much later. Earlier clinical trials had firmly established mechanical thrombectomy as an effective treatment within the first 24 hours of a stroke. However, beyond this window, doctors lacked definitive evidence from randomized trials to determine whether removing the clot could still improve patients' recovery or expose them to greater risks. The LATE-MT study, a prospective, randomized controlled trial, brought together 336 eligible patients from stroke centers across China and divided them into two equal groups of 168. The control group received oral antiplatelet drugs to prevent clots from growing or reforming, treatment to manage brain swelling and routine hospital support as part of their standard care. The other group underwent mechanical thrombectomy alongside guideline-based medical care, with doctors aiming to begin the procedure within 60 minutes of randomization. The clot-removal procedure was performed between 24 and 72 hours after the first stroke symptoms appeared. Distribution of functional outcomes at 90 days in the intention-to-treat population. Credit: New England Journal of Medicine (2026). DOI: 10.1056/nejmoa2609362 24 hours not the ultimate deadline By the 90-day mark, patients who underwent mechanical thrombectomy were showing better recovery outcomes than those who received standard care alone. This was reflected in their modified Rankin Scale scores, which track the extent of disability after a stroke and how well patients regain their ability to function. Among patients who underwent thrombectomy, 26.9% had no significant disability despite some remaining symptoms, compared with 15.6% in the control group. They also reported better quality of life and greater ability to manage everyday activities. However, the gains came with some added risks. Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage, or bleeding in the brain severe enough to cause symptoms, occurred in 4.8% of patients who underwent thrombectomy, while no such cases occurred in the control group. The 90-day death rate was also higher among patients who received the procedure, at 6.0% compared with 1.8%. The findings suggest that patients arriving more than 24 hours after a stroke should not automatically be ruled out for thrombectomy. Instead, specialists could assess whether selected patients might still benefit from the procedure, while taking into account the amount of salvageable brain tissue post-stroke and potential risks of thrombectomy.
