County vaccination data may mask school-level measles risk

深度解析

本文深入揭示了麻疹疫情在美国复燃的根本原因：疫苗接种覆盖率的“空间异质性”。尽管全国或县级MMR疫苗接种率可能看似接近95%的群体免疫阈值，足以提供宏观保护，但研究发现，在学校等局部微观层面存在大量未接种或疫苗接种不足的儿童“口袋”。这些低接种率区域形成易感人群的局部聚集，即便少量麻疹病毒输入，也足以突破局部群体免疫屏障，导致疫情迅速蔓延并形成局限性暴发，从而将局部传播风险推高至流行病阈值之上。这种县级平均数据掩盖微观风险的现象，对公共卫生策略提出严峻挑战。 其行业影响深远：首先，它强调了传统宏观流行病学监测的局限性，促使公共卫生机构必须加强精细化、多尺度的数据收集和分析能力，例如建立全国性的学校层面疫苗接种数据库。其次，疫苗接种政策的制定需从单一的整体覆盖率转向关注局部薄弱环节，实施精准干预措施，以弥补空间异质性带来的免疫缺口。再者，它凸显了疫苗犹豫和错误信息传播对群体免疫的潜在威胁，提示需要更有效的风险沟通和社会动员策略。最终，这促使医疗卫生系统在疫苗接种规划、资源分配和应急响应方面，采取更具空间针对性的策略，以维护社区的整体健康安全。

DOI: 10.1038/s41591-026-04561-w Pockets of low vaccination rates in some schools help explain why measles outbreaks have been climbing in the U.S., even when county-level vaccination numbers—the data policymakers consult—appear high enough to provide community protection. A new study, published Aug. 18 in Nature Medicine, helps reveal why measles, which was declared eliminated in the U.S. in 2000, increased to more than 3,500 cases in 2025–26. The rise began before the COVID-19 pandemic but was made worse by it, largely because of vaccine hesitancy, a surge in vaccine misinformation on social media and vaccine exemption policies that vary across states. County averages mask school risk In the study, researchers collected publicly available county, school district and school-level data on measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccinations and ran them through a mathematical model that translated vaccination coverage into transmission risk. They found that a new case of measles could spread in a school with children who were unvaccinated or had missed one of the two recommended MMR doses. These risks, however, appeared largely invisible when looking at statewide and county-level MMR coverage because, in aggregate, vaccination rates can remain near the 95% herd immunity threshold known to protect a population from wider outbreaks. The data revealed that the average percentage of unprotected students across the country roughly doubled from 5% to nearly 10% following the pandemic. The study highlights the need for more detailed data to understand the true risks posed by measles. "Before this decrease in vaccination, even with an introduction of measles, because vaccination coverage was so high, you could still have one or two cases and the outbreak would fizzle out," said Ana Bento, assistant professor in the Department of Public and Ecosystem Health in the College of Veterinary Medicine and the paper's senior author. Siyu Chen, a postdoctoral research associate in Bento's lab, is the paper's first author. "Now we're in a situation where, given the very heterogeneous and low vaccine coverage in places, there is a possibility that an outbreak will become a big, localized outbreak after an introduction, which is what we see," Bento said. Building a map of hidden gaps Outbreaks in 2025 and 2026 in Texas, Arizona, New Mexico and South Carolina revealed the same epidemiological pattern, where MMR vaccination rates at the county level looked reassuring while school-level clusters of unvaccinated children had silently pushed local transmission risks above that epidemic threshold. In the study, Chen and Bento collected data and created a nationwide database of kindergarten vaccination records spanning 50,000 schools, 13,000 school districts, 3,000 counties and 45 states plus the District of Columbia, dating back to 2013. "There was no single national database of school-level vaccination coverage," Chen said. "We had to build one, state by state, and reconcile more than a decade of records that were never designed to be compared. That groundwork is what let us finally see risk at the scale measles operates on." "We can see the risks now," Bento said. "We just weren't watching at the right scale and the window to fix that is closing." Publication details Siyu Chen et al, County, district and community-level measles transmission in the United States in 2013−2025, Nature Medicine (2026).
