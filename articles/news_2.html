深度解析

本研究揭示了规律性饮酒对癌症免疫检查点抑制剂 (ICIs) 疗效的负面影响及其潜在机制，具有重要的临床和行业意义。核心机制在于酒精干扰了肿瘤微环境中关键抗肿瘤免疫细胞——T细胞的功能。具体而言，酒精可减少肿瘤浸润性CD4+和CD8+ T细胞的数量，损害CD8+ T细胞的激活及其产生干扰素-γ (IFN-γ) 等细胞因子的能力，同时上调TIGIT这一抑制性受体的表达，共同削弱了免疫系统对癌细胞的识别与杀伤。该研究通过PD-1与TIGIT的双重免疫检查点阻断策略，成功逆转了酒精导致的疗效下降，为临床干预提供了新思路。 从行业影响来看，首先，它强调了生活方式因素在免疫治疗中的关键作用，进一步强化了对癌症患者避免饮酒的临床建议。其次，发现TIGIT作为酒精介导免疫抑制的关键靶点，为开发针对性联合疗法（如抗PD-1与抗TIGIT抗体的联合应用）提供了理论基础，有望为那些伴有饮酒史或对单一ICI疗法应答不佳的患者带来新的治疗选择。最后，研究呼吁在癌症数据库中纳入更详细的患者饮酒数据，这对于推动精准医疗、优化患者管理和预后评估至关重要。

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: Scientists found that alcohol reduced tumor-infiltrating T cells in mice and impaired cancer-killing activity. Credit: Pexels. Cancer immunotherapy has transformed treatment for several advanced cancers, yet the difference between these treatments working and not could depend on simple routine habits. A recent study published in Science Signaling explores one such overlooked factor: regular alcohol consumption. The focus was on immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), a class of drugs that includes anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) therapies that train the body's immune system to attack tumor cells. The researchers used a mouse model and human clinical data to investigate how regular alcohol consumption impacts the success of ICIs. The effects were evident in both animal models and human patients. In mice with lung and bladder cancer, moderate-to-heavy alcohol consumption cut the success of immunotherapy by nearly half and increased the likelihood of tumors returning. A similar pattern emerged in human clinical data, where lung cancer patients who regularly consumed alcohol, more than one drink a week, experienced fewer survival benefits after starting anti-PD-1 treatment. Alcohol breaks the brakes The body's immune system relies on specialized white blood cells called T cells to seek out and destroy cancer cells. To prevent these immune cells from becoming overactive and attacking healthy tissues, T cells have natural off-switches, including a receptor protein called programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1). Model of alcohol inhibition of anti-PD1 efficacy. Credit: Science Signaling (2026). DOI: 10.1126/scisignal.aef1375 Cancer cells can hijack this mechanism by activating the PD-1 off-switch on T cells, effectively putting the brakes on the immune system and allowing tumors to escape attack. Anti-PD-1 drugs, such as pembrolizumab and nivolumab, use engineered antibodies that bind to the PD-1 receptor and block it. This releases the brakes on T cells, allowing them to recognize and attack cancer cells. While these medications have been found to be quite effective in clinical practice, some patients do not benefit from them as much as expected. Alcohol could be one reason why. Previous studies have found that it interferes with the immune system's response against the tumor. Doctors thus actively advise cancer patients to avoid alcohol. Yet, alcohol consumption remains common among people with cancer, with one study finding that 76.4% continued to drink. To understand how alcohol interferes with immunotherapy, the researchers turned to mice with lung and bladder cancer. For five weeks before introducing cancer cells, they gave the animals moderate or heavy amounts of alcohol to mimic human drinking habits. They then treated the mice with immunotherapy and tracked tumor growth, survival and recurrence. The researchers also examined electronic health records of cancer patients who had received anti-PD-1 treatment at the UNC Chapel Hill Cancer Hospital between 2014 and 2018. Individual tumor growth curves from mice receiving anti-PD1 and anti-TIGIT treatment. Credit: Science Signaling (2026). DOI: 10.1126/scisignal.aef1375 Weakening the effect of ICI therapies The study discovered that regular alcohol consumption significantly weakens cancer immunotherapy, but there is a way to restore its effectiveness. Moderate-to-heavy alcohol consumption reduced the success of ICI therapies by about 50% in mice, and in human patients, too, drinking more than one drink per week was associated with reduced survival benefits. They found that alcohol exposure reduced the overall number of tumor-killing T cells (CD4+ and CD8+ T cells) entering the tumor microenvironment. It also impaired CD8+ T cell activation, reducing their ability to produce key tumor-fighting signals like interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and kill cancer cells. Alcohol also causes T cells to express higher levels of a second inhibitory receptor called TIGIT, whose original job is to prevent immune cells from becoming overactive and attacking healthy tissues. Antidote to alcohol's effect To counter this effect, researchers combined standard anti-PD-1 therapy with an anti-TIGIT antibody. Blocking both PD-1 and TIGIT simultaneously prevented these two mechanisms from holding back the immune response. In alcohol-exposed mice, the combination restored complete tumor response rates to almost the same level as in mice that had not consumed alcohol. The findings together identified the mechanism by which regular alcohol use can impair the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors, like anti-PD-1 therapy against cancer. Future studies will need to use precise alcohol intake numbers to better understand how drinking affects ICIs. For that to happen, the researchers suggest that adding patients' alcohol use to cancer databases would not only help show how it affects different treatments across cancers but also help identify potential solutions.
