脑能量失衡能否解释精神分裂症中的异常体验？

Could brain energy mismatches explain altered experiences in schizophrenia?

深度解析

本研究深入探讨了精神分裂症患者感知异常（如时间、空间和自我感碎片化）的潜在生物学机制，提出了“能量适应瓶颈”这一核心假说。该假说指出，精神分裂症患者的脑细胞虽能维持正常基线能量产生，但其备用呼吸能力显著降低，且肌酸激酶反应动力学受损，导致三磷酸腺苷（ATP）转移效率下降。这意味着当神经活动需求增加时，脑细胞难以迅速提升能量供应以维持正常的生理功能。这种能量代谢障碍进而干扰了大脑的同步节律，特别是40赫兹的听觉节律，并降低了内在神经时间尺度（INTs）的适应性，从而导致患者出现时间感凝滞、空间脱节及自我感偏移等临床症状。该理论框架的提出具有重要的临床和科研意义。它不仅为精神分裂症复杂的临床表型提供了一个统一的神经生物学解释，也为未来靶向性治疗开辟了新途径。通过针对线粒体功能或ATP缓冲系统进行干预的代谢疗法，可能有助于恢复受损的细胞能量稳态，进而重同步大脑节律，改善患者的日常体验。这一发现将推动精神病学领域从神经递质失衡转向更深层的细胞能量代谢障碍进行研究，有望为精神分裂症的精准医疗和新药研发提供新的生物学靶点。

Credit: Pexels A minute can seem shorter when you are having fun, yet it feels painfully long when you're bored or stressed. Although this gap between actual time and perceived time is something we all experience, it can be much more pronounced in people with schizophrenia-spectrum conditions. They may experience time and space very differently from others, with time seeming fragmented, familiar surroundings feeling strange or disconnected, and even their sense of self shifting. A recent study published in Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews explores why people with schizophrenia often experience time, space and their sense of self differently. Drawing on evidence from cell studies, metabolic imaging, electrical recordings of brain activity and functional brain imaging, the author developed a unified model. The model proposes that an energy bottleneck in brain cells may limit the brain's ability to maintain synchronized rhythms, contributing to fragmented everyday experiences. To test this hypothesis, the author proposed experiments that combine real-time brain wave recordings (EEG) with metabolic brain imaging (fPET) as participants listen to a rhythmic 40-Hz sound. The tests would examine how the brain produces and uses energy, how it responds to changing demands, and the relationship between energy metabolism and neural activity. An energy bottleneck behind time fragmentation? The brain normally stitches the past, present and future into one seamless flow, but in people with schizophrenia, that continuity can break down, making time feel frozen or fragmented into disconnected snapshots. This disruption may have roots in how brain cells manage their energy. Laboratory stem-cell models show that cells in schizophrenia maintain normal baseline energy production but have reduced spare respiratory capacity, limiting their ability to increase energy production when demand rises. A 15% to 22% drop in creatine kinase reaction kinetics points to slower ATP transfer between brain cells. These energy-related changes may also disrupt brain rhythms. People with schizophrenia often show weaker power and phase synchronization of brain activity in 40-Hz auditory rhythms. Neuroimaging studies suggest that intrinsic neural timescales (INTs), the brain's internal memory windows, are also less adaptable in schizophrenia, making it harder to adjust them when switching from rest to active tasks. Small human studies have also linked altered brain activity to disturbances in the sense of self, although direct evidence connecting these changes to people's lived experiences of time and space remains limited. Together, these findings led the researcher to propose an energy adaptation bottleneck. Brain cells may struggle to boost ATP production when demand rises, making it harder to sustain synchronized 40-Hz rhythms and flexible timing windows, potentially fragmenting the experience of time, space and self. Theoretical to practical The researcher proposed a three-part strategy to turn the idea into testable parameters. The first was a multimodal experiment that paired PET scans of glucose uptake with high-speed brain wave recording. Participants listen to rhythmic 40-Hz sound clicks at low, medium and high demand levels. Running both scans at the same moment lets scientists watch how energy supply and wave synchronization respond together as mental workload climbs. Next is an analysis of relative demand-response scaling. It examines how energy use and wave synchronization rise in step with each other, then links those brain findings directly to clinical interviews. The aim is to find out whether schizophrenia involves a true biological mismatch, where energy fails to keep up with the demands of brain waves, and whether this metabolic-neural uncoupling directly explains a person's fragmented experience of time, space and selfhood. Finally, the researcher laid out clear benchmarks for disproving the idea, along with long-term testing models for future clinical studies. Among them is the falsifiable prediction: metabolic responses and neural synchronization should adapt differently together in schizophrenia as stimulation increases. The author stated that if future clinical trials find that energy production and brain-wave activity scale normally in schizophrenia, the hypothesis will be disproven. However, if the findings hold up, this framework could help test targeted metabolic treatments, such as mitochondrial or ATP-buffering therapies, to see whether restoring cellular energy can resynchronize brain rhythms and improve people's everyday experiences. The proposed framework still needs testing, either through small pilot studies with volunteers or laboratory simulations, as the current study offers a theoretical model rather than original experimental or computational evidence.
