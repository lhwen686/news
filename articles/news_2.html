Concept of body-coupled wireless power delivery for battery-free wearables. Credit: Science Advances

A research team led by Prof. Jerald Yoo from the Department of Electrical and Computer Engineering at Seoul National University (SNU) has developed a skin-conformal wearable health care system, "SkinECG," capable of measuring electrocardiogram (ECG) signals without a battery. By combining energy harvesting with human body–coupled power transfer, the study presents a new solution to one of the most critical challenges in wearable devices: power supply.

The findings are published in Science Advances.

Wearable health care systems are emerging as next-generation medical technologies that enable real-time monitoring of physiological signals through body-worn sensors, allowing early detection of disease-related abnormalities.

A representative example is the electrocardiography (ECG) sensor, which measures electrical signals generated by the heart and is essential for detecting cardiovascular conditions such as arrhythmia.

However, a major obstacle remains in the commercialization and long-term use of wearable devices: the battery. Batteries increase device size and weight, reducing wearability, and once depleted, they can interrupt the collection of physiological signals. In addition, the need for periodic charging and replacement poses inconvenience for users and makes continuous long-term health monitoring difficult.

To overcome this challenge, researchers have explored energy harvesting technologies that convert ambient energy sources—such as light, heat, and motion—into electricity. However, a fundamental limitation remains: the optimal location to harvest energy is often not the same as the best location to measure physiological signals.

For example, ECG sensors are typically placed on the chest, while solar cells operate most efficiently on body areas exposed to sunlight, such as the arms or legs. This creates what the research team calls a "location mismatch": the best place to harvest energy is often different from the best place to measure biosignals .

Solving the energy location mismatch

To overcome this location mismatch, Prof. Yoo's team proposed a novel power supply architecture that wirelessly delivers energy from one or more body-worn energy-harvesting devices to a remotely located ECG sensor.

The developed SkinECG system consists of a skin-adherent hydrocolloid patch integrating a flexible circuit board and a custom-designed semiconductor chip for ECG sensing, along with a multi-source wireless power supply network that transmits energy harvested from multiple devices to the sensor. This technology is termed an Orthogonal Energy Harvesting Network (O-EHN) in the study.

One or more energy-harvesting units convert ambient energy into electrical power, which is then wirelessly delivered to a chest-mounted ECG sensor via body-coupled power transfer. By transmitting power at orthogonal frequencies, the harvesters reduce interference between power signals, allowing their number and placement to be flexibly adjusted while maintaining stable power delivery to the ECG sensor.

Guiding wireless power along skin

Conventional wireless power transfer methods rely on electromagnetic radiation through the air. Near the human body, however, electromagnetic waves are often absorbed or scattered, leading to a significant drop in power-transfer efficiency.

To overcome this limitation, Prof. Yoo's team took a different approach: instead of radiating power over a distance, they guided the power along the surface of the skin.

Using body-coupled power transfer, the team wirelessly delivered power from on-body energy harvesters directly to the ECG sensor without wires. To prevent interference among multiple power sources, each harvester was assigned a distinct frequency channel, ensuring stable power reception at the sensor.

The system was also designed with human safety in mind. The power coupled to the body was kept at a level comparable to everyday exposure from ambient electromagnetic environments, ensuring safety for human use.

Toward broader battery-free health monitoring

The researchers demonstrated that the ECG sensor could be powered solely through energy harvesting, without batteries or wired connections. The system also reduces constraints on the number and placement of energy harvesters and is compatible with existing commercial energy harvesting technologies, indicating strong potential for expansion into a wide range of wearable health care devices.

This technology can be applied not only to ECG monitoring but also to long-term measurement of other physiological signals, such as electromyography (EMG) and electroencephalography (EEG). Furthermore, it holds promise as a foundational solution for addressing power supply challenges in both wearable electronics and implantable medical devices.

Prof. Jerald Yoo explained, "In wearable health care systems, there has been a fundamental limitation in that the optimal locations for harvesting ambient energy and for measuring physiological signals are different. This study resolves that issue by enabling wireless power transfer along the surface of the human body."

He added, "By limiting the power coupled to the human body to levels comparable to those encountered in daily life, we designed the system with human safety in mind while demonstrating stable power delivery to ECG sensors without bulky batteries.

"This approach can be extended to multimodal digital health care platforms capable of operating various biosignal sensors such as EMG and EEG, as well as to broader wearable power supply technologies."

This research was conducted as an international collaboration led by Prof. Yoo's team at Seoul National University, with participation from the University of Tokyo and the National University of Singapore. The team expects the results to mark an important step toward solving power-supply challenges for next-generation wearable health care systems.

身体耦合无线电力传输实现无电池可穿戴设备的理念。图片来源：Science Advances

由首尔国立大学（SNU）电气与计算机工程系 Jerald Yoo 教授领导的研究团队开发出一种皮肤贴合式可穿戴医疗保健系统“SkinECG”，该系统能够在无需电池的情况下测量心电图（ECG）信号。通过将能量采集与人体耦合电力传输相结合，这项研究为可穿戴设备中最关键的挑战之一——电源供应——提供了一种新颖的解决方案。

相关研究成果已发表在《科学进展》（Science Advances）期刊上。

可穿戴医疗保健系统正作为下一代医疗技术而兴起，它们通过佩戴在身体上的传感器实现对生理信号的实时监测，从而有助于早期发现与疾病相关的异常。

一个典型的例子是心电图（ECG）传感器，它测量心脏产生的电信号，对于检测心律失常等心血管疾病至关重要。

然而，可穿戴设备的商业化和长期使用面临一个主要障碍：电池。电池会增加设备的尺寸和重量，降低佩戴舒适度；一旦电量耗尽，它们还会中断生理信号的采集。此外，定期充电和更换的需求给用户带来不便，并使得连续的长期健康监测变得困难。

为克服这一挑战，研究人员探索了能量采集技术，该技术能将环境能源（如光、热和运动）转化为电能。然而，一个根本性的局限仍然存在：最佳的能量采集位置往往与最佳的生理信号测量位置不同。

例如，心电图传感器通常放置在胸部，而太阳能电池则在暴露于阳光的身体部位（如手臂或腿部）工作效率最高。这便产生了研究团队所称的“位置不匹配”问题：最佳的能量采集位置通常与最佳的生物信号测量位置不同。

解决能量位置不匹配问题

为克服这种位置不匹配问题，Yoo 教授的团队提出了一种新颖的电源架构，能够将一个或多个身体佩戴式能量采集设备所采集的能量无线传输到远程的心电图传感器。

所开发的 SkinECG 系统包括一个集成柔性电路板和定制设计的用于心电感测的半导体芯片的皮肤贴附式水胶体贴片，以及一个将从多个设备采集的能量传输给传感器的多源无线电源网络。这项技术在研究中被称为“正交能量采集网络”（O-EHN）。

一个或多个能量采集单元将环境能量转换为电能，然后通过身体耦合电力传输，将其无线传输到胸部佩戴式心电图传感器。通过以正交频率传输电力，采集器减少了电源信号之间的干扰，从而使其数量和位置能够灵活调整，同时保持向心电图传感器的稳定供电。

沿皮肤表面引导无线电力

传统的无线电力传输方法依赖于通过空气的电磁辐射。然而，在人体附近，电磁波常常被吸收或散射，导致电力传输效率显著下降。

为克服这一局限，Yoo 教授的团队采取了不同的方法：他们不是通过距离辐射电力，而是沿着皮肤表面引导电力。

利用身体耦合电力传输，该团队将来自佩戴在身体上的能量采集器的电力直接无线传输到心电图传感器，无需导线。为防止多个电源之间的干扰，每个采集器都被分配了一个独特的频率通道，从而确保传感器稳定接收电力。

该系统在设计时也考虑了人体安全。耦合到身体的功率被控制在与日常环境中电磁暴露水平相当的程度，确保了人体使用的安全性。

迈向更广泛的无电池健康监测

研究人员证明，心电图传感器可以完全通过能量采集供电，无需电池或有线连接。该系统还减少了能量采集器数量和放置位置的限制，并与现有商业能量采集技术兼容，这表明其在广泛的可穿戴医疗保健设备中具有强大的扩展潜力。

这项技术不仅可以应用于心电图监测，还可以用于肌电图（EMG）和脑电图（EEG）等其他生理信号的长期测量。此外，它有望成为解决可穿戴电子设备和植入式医疗设备电源供应挑战的基础性解决方案。

Jerald Yoo 教授解释说：“在可穿戴医疗保健系统中，一直存在一个根本性的局限性，即环境能量采集的最佳位置与生理信号测量的最佳位置不同。这项研究通过实现沿人体表面传输无线电力，解决了这个问题。”

他补充说：“通过将耦合到人体的功率限制在与日常生活中遇到的电磁暴露水平相当的水平，我们在设计系统时考虑了人体安全，同时展示了无需笨重电池即可向心电图传感器稳定供电的能力。”

“这种方法可以扩展到能够运行肌电图和脑电图等各种生物信号传感器的多模式数字医疗平台，以及更广泛的可穿戴电源供应技术。”

这项研究是Yoo教授团队在首尔国立大学领导下，与东京大学和新加坡国立大学合作进行的国际项目。该团队期望研究成果能为解决下一代可穿戴医疗保健系统的电源供应挑战迈出重要一步。
