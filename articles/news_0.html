深度解析

慢性阻塞性肺疾病（COPD）的核心病理机制在于气道塌陷和肺内空气滞留，导致严重呼吸困难（dyspnea）。文章探讨的噘嘴呼吸和笑声疗法均为非药物干预。噘嘴呼吸通过产生背压，有效预防气道塌陷，排出陈旧空气，并减缓呼吸频率，增强患者呼吸控制。 笑声疗法作为补充干预，其机制推测为通过锻炼呼吸肌、促进深呼气改善肺通气，并触发内啡肽释放，提供身心缓解，减轻慢性病心理压力。 该研究影响深远。它揭示了简单、低成本且居家可行的非药物干预，可有效补充现有药物及肺康复，显著改善COPD患者呼吸困难及整体健康。这为全球，尤其资源有限地区，提供了一种可行的、以患者为中心的慢性病管理方案。未来需更大规模研究阐明笑声疗法机制，并推动其纳入临床指南，促进COPD护理向更全面、个性化方向发展。

This article has been reviewed according to Science X's editorial process and policies . Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: Laughter therapy. Credit: Goncagul Aldan / European Respiratory Society A clinical trial for patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) shows that laughter therapy can reduce breathing difficulties and is as effective as more traditional breathing exercises. The research was presented at the European Respiratory Society (ERS) Congress in Barcelona, Spain. In the trial, patients were taught how to use deliberate laughter as a breathing exercise. This was directly compared with patients using traditional breathing exercises and a control group that did not do any breathing exercises. Researchers say laughter therapy offers an effective, low-cost treatment that patients can practice at home without equipment or specialist support, alongside their other treatment. Results of the trial were presented by Dr. Goncagul Aldan, a lecturer and nurse who graduated from Hacettepe University Faculty of Nursing, Ankara, Türkiye. She said, "COPD is a serious lung disease that makes it hard to breathe. It affects millions of people worldwide and is a leading cause of disability and death. People with COPD often feel breathless, even during simple daily activities. This reduces their quality of life and makes them increasingly dependent on others for care. Managing COPD requires more than medication alone. Patients need a holistic approach to help them cope with their symptoms, the physical burden of the disease and its emotional and psychological impact. "With this research, we wanted to explore whether additional simple, low-cost techniques could complement existing care. Laughter therapy had already shown promising results in a number of other chronic conditions. Its accessible, enjoyable nature suggests it could also benefit COPD patients, not only in coping with the daily stress of living with a chronic illness, but also in directly supporting their respiratory health." How the trial was set up The study included 63 people with COPD being treated at Ankara Bilkent City Hospital, Türkiye. One-third of patients were randomly assigned to laughter therapy, one-third to pursed-lip breathing exercises and one-third to no breathing exercises. Pursed-lip breathing exercises, in which patients are taught to breathe in through the nose and out slowly through pursed lips, are an established technique for COPD patients that helps release stale air that can become trapped in diseased lungs. For laughter therapy, patients were taught a series of exercises including rhythmic clapping with vocal sounds; playful laughter exercises, such as mimicking a motorcycle starting or a lion's roar; laughter meditation with spontaneous, unforced laughter; and relaxation exercises with calm breathing and smiling. In both cases, patients were taught in person how to do their assigned exercises and were then asked to practice for 30 minutes, three times per week for eight weeks. They were supported with a video call and text messages. Before, during and after treatment, patients were assessed for breathing difficulties, overall health and how much care they needed day to day. Breathlessness eased in both groups After the treatment period, patients receiving laughter therapy and those using pursed-lip breathing exercises had less difficulty breathing compared with patients who had not been doing either breathing exercise. Patients in the two treatment groups also had better overall health, but there was no difference in how much care they needed. Aldan said, "The results of this study show that both pursed-lip breathing exercises and laughter therapy can significantly reduce the severity of breathlessness in COPD patients, and this effect was sustained even a month after the program ended. Both interventions also showed promising results in improving overall health, suggesting that laughter therapy may be a worthwhile area for further investigation alongside pursed-lip breathing in COPD care. "In COPD, the airways tend to collapse during exhalation, trapping stale air inside the lungs. Pursed-lip breathing creates a gentle back pressure that allows this trapped air to escape, slows the breathing rate and helps patients feel more in control of their breathing. Laughter may exercise the breathing muscles, promote deeper exhalation and trigger the release of endorphins, offering both physical and emotional relief. Laughter therapy aims to evoke the effects of genuine laughter in the body. However, the mechanisms behind laughter therapy in COPD remain poorly understood, and more comprehensive research is needed. "These findings offer hope that simple, low-cost techniques that patients can practice at home—without equipment or specialist support—may reduce breathlessness and improve daily well-being. Both pursed-lip breathing exercises and laughter therapy could be valuable additions to existing COPD care, complementing medication and pulmonary rehabilitation programs." Calls for larger studies The researchers now hope to study laughter therapy on a larger scale with more patients and collaborate internationally to study these approaches in clinical settings across different countries. Dr. Marc Miravitlles, vice president of the European Respiratory Society and a senior researcher and consultant at Vall d'Hebron University Hospital in Barcelona, Spain, who was not involved in the research, said, "COPD is a common, serious and long-term condition with no cure. It's vital that we help patients manage their condition as well as possible, so they can continue to live a normal life. Treatment and care usually involve inhaler treatment, exercise and vaccinations against respiratory infections. "This is a small but well-run study looking at the potential benefits of laughter therapy or breathing exercises for people with COPD. Patients were taught how to do these exercises and then given support to continue at home. These results, showing that patients can breathe more easily and feel better in general, are promising. Laughter therapy is simple and low-cost, meaning it could be used by anyone, anywhere in the world. We look forward to further research in this area." Key medical concepts COPDDyspnea
