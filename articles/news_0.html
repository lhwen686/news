People who speak more than one language seem to have younger brains, according to research presented at the Federation of European Neuroscience Societies (FENS) Forum 2026. Our brains are made up of billions of nerve cells that need to communicate with one another. As we age, connectivity in our brains tends to deteriorate and, as a result, our memory and the speed of our thinking also decline.

The new research found that the more languages people speak, the younger their brains appear. Learning an extra language at a younger age and becoming highly fluent in another language also seem to slow brain aging.

The research was presented by Dr. Lucia Amoruso from the Basque Center on Cognition, Brain and Language, San Sebastián, Spain, who worked with a team from the Latin American Brain Health Institute at the Universidad Adolfo Ibañez, Chile, the Cognitive Neuroscience Center at the Universidad de San Andres, Argentina, and the Global Brain Health Institute at Trinity College Dublin, Ireland.

The researchers recently published a study showing that in countries where people typically speak more than one language, people seem to age more slowly. In the new study, the researchers carried out a detailed analysis of a group of people from the Basque region of Spain who spoke between one and four different languages, including combinations of Spanish, Basque, French and English.

They began with a group of 728 people to create a "brain aging clock." They used a technique called magnetoencephalography, which measures brain activity by the faint magnetic fields produced when brain cells are active. The researchers used artificial intelligence to process data on brain activity in people of different ages to show what is a normal level of brain connectivity at any given age.

Then the team used this clock to gauge the "brain age" of a second group of 144 people.

When they compared people's real age with the age of their brain, they found that those who spoke two languages had brains that appeared around 6 years younger than those who spoke only one language. For people who spoke three languages, their brains were around 7 years younger, and for those who spoke four languages, their brains were around 13 years younger.

Amoruso said, "In simple terms, people who spoke more languages tended to have brains that looked younger than expected for their chronological age. The effect was not only related to the number of languages spoken. Higher language proficiency and earlier acquisition of a second language were also associated with more delayed brain aging.

"This suggests that multilingual experience matters as a gradient: It is not simply about being bilingual or not, but about the depth and duration of language experience."

The researchers took account of factors such as people's age, sex and education, but caution that they cannot rule out the potential influence of other factors that may have an impact on the brain, such as lifestyle and social engagement.

Amoruso and the team now hope to carry out similar work in people with neurodegenerative conditions, such as Alzheimer's disease, where brain aging and resilience are especially important. They also plan to look at whether speaking two or more languages that are very similar could have a bigger effect on the brain, as managing closely related languages may require greater language control.

Professor Christina Dalla from the National and Kapodistrian University of Athens, Greece, is chair of the FENS Forum communication committee and was not involved in the research.

She said, "We know that many factors can influence our brain health and mental abilities as we age. For example, we know that not smoking, eating well, social and artistic engagement, as well as being active, can help. How we use our brains throughout life can also have an impact, especially if we engage in effortful learning that activates our brain.

"This study suggests that learning a second, third or fourth language could help our brains stay younger for longer, and the earlier we start, the better. There are many good reasons for learning another language at any age—social, cultural and for the health of your brain—so we should support language learning at school and throughout life, even if it's hard."

根据欧洲神经科学学会联合会（FENS）2026年论坛上发表的研究，说多种语言的人似乎拥有更年轻的大脑。我们的大脑由数十亿个需要相互交流的神经细胞组成。随着年龄增长，我们大脑的连接性趋于恶化，结果是我们的记忆力和思维速度也随之下降。

这项新研究发现，人们说的语言越多，他们的大脑就显得越年轻。在更年轻的时候学习一门额外的语言，并流利掌握另一种语言，似乎也能减缓大脑衰老。

这项研究由西班牙圣塞巴斯蒂安巴斯克认知、大脑与语言中心的路西亚·阿莫鲁索博士（Dr. Lucia Amoruso）发表，她与来自智利阿道夫·伊瓦涅斯大学拉丁美洲大脑健康研究所、阿根廷圣安德烈斯大学认知神经科学中心以及爱尔兰都柏林圣三一学院全球大脑健康研究所的团队合作。

研究人员最近发表的一项研究表明，在那些人们通常说不止一种语言的国家，人们的衰老速度似乎更慢。在这项新研究中，研究人员对来自西班牙巴斯克地区的一群人进行了详细分析，这些人说一到四种不同的语言，包括西班牙语、巴斯克语、法语和英语的组合。

他们首先对一组728人进行了研究，以创建一个“大脑衰老时钟”。他们使用了一种名为脑磁图（magnetoencephalography）的技术，该技术通过测量脑细胞活跃时产生的微弱磁场来评估大脑活动。研究人员利用人工智能处理不同年龄人群的大脑活动数据，以展示在任何给定年龄下大脑连接性的正常水平。

随后，该团队利用这个时钟来评估第二组144人的“大脑年龄”。

当他们将人们的实际年龄与他们的大脑年龄进行比较时，发现说两种语言的人，他们的大脑比只说一种语言的人年轻约6岁。对于说三种语言的人，他们的大脑年轻约7岁；而说四种语言的人，他们的大脑年轻约13岁。

阿莫鲁索表示：“简单来说，说更多语言的人，他们的大脑看起来比其实际年龄预期要年轻。这种效应不仅与所说的语言数量有关。更高的语言熟练度和更早掌握第二语言也与大脑衰老延迟更显著相关。”

“这表明多语种经验的重要性呈现出一种梯度：它不仅仅是关于是否双语，而是关于语言经验的深度和持续时间。”

研究人员考虑了年龄、性别和教育等因素，但同时警告称，他们无法排除其他可能影响大脑的因素，如生活方式和社会参与。

阿莫鲁索及其团队现在希望在患有神经退行性疾病（如阿尔茨海默病）的人群中开展类似研究，因为在大脑衰老和恢复力方面，这些人群的状况尤为重要。他们还计划探讨说两种或两种以上非常相似的语言是否会对大脑产生更大的影响，因为处理密切相关的语言可能需要更强的语言控制能力。

希腊雅典国立卡波迪斯特里安大学的克里斯蒂娜·达拉教授（Professor Christina Dalla）是FENS论坛传播委员会主席，并未参与这项研究。

她表示：“我们知道许多因素会影响我们随着年龄增长的大脑健康和心智能力。例如，我们知道不吸烟、健康饮食、社交和艺术活动以及保持活跃都有帮助。”

“我们在一生中如何使用大脑也会产生影响，特别是如果我们从事需要努力学习以激活大脑的活动。”

“这项研究表明，学习第二、第三或第四种语言可能有助于我们的大脑保持更长时间的年轻，而且我们开始得越早越好。在任何年龄学习另一种语言都有很多充分的理由——为了社交、文化和大脑健康——所以我们应该在学校和终身支持语言学习，即使这很困难。”
