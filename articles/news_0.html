New vaccination approach turns mosquito bites into immunity boosters

深度解析

核心专业概念是创新的化学疫苗接种策略，其医学机制在于精准靶向疟疾寄生虫的肝脏晚期发育阶段。通过使用WM382和MK-7602等天门冬氨酸蛋白酶IX和X的双重抑制剂，研究人员能够在寄生虫进入血液并引发临床疾病前，将其阻滞在肝细胞内，这被称为“免疫学最佳时机”。这种方法不仅有效预防了疾病进展，更关键的是，它能高效预先调动宿主免疫系统，诱导产生强大的多层次免疫应答，包括抗体、CD8+ T细胞以及特异性的肝脏驻留记忆T细胞。这些记忆T细胞对未来的感染具有快速响应和清除能力，提供了持久保护。 这项技术革新解决了传统基因减毒寄生虫疫苗生产复杂、剂量高和难以规模化的问题。由于所用药物靶向在不同疟疾病种中高度保守的酶，该策略有望对多样化的疟疾变体提供更广泛、持久的免疫力。其行业影响深远：它开辟了一条利用自然蚊虫叮咬作为免疫增强剂的“自然疫苗接种和增强”新路径，简化了疫苗接种流程，有望显著提升疫区人群的保护水平，并为应对日益严峻的疟疾耐药性挑战提供了强有力的新工具。这对于全球疟疾防治，特别是脆弱人群的健康，具有革命性意义。

英文原文

Original
Researchers from WEHI (Melbourne, Australia) have discovered a new way to help prevent malaria that could one day transform mosquito bites from a source of deadly infection into immunity boosters against the disease. The Australian team demonstrated a new vaccination approach that can prime the immune system to fight malaria parasites before they cause disease, with subsequent mosquito bites acting as boosters to strengthen immunity over time. This approach protected mice against malaria for the study period – a rare outcome that could inform the development of next-generation prevention strategies for one of the world's deadliest infectious diseases. At a glance WEHI researchers develop a new vaccination approach that turned mosquito bites into ongoing immune boosters, in landmark research published in Science. The preclinical study is the first to target malaria parasites at the late liver stage using an antimalarial drug candidate discovered by WEHI and global biopharmaceutical company MSD, (tradename of Merck & Co., Inc., Rahway, N.J., USA), triggering an unusually strong and multi-layered immune response. This approach protected mice from developing disease for up to two years – a timeline that covers two malaria seasons in real-world settings. The 'sweet spot' for malaria intervention More than 600,000 people – predominantly pregnant women and children under the age of five – die from malaria every year. According to the World Health Organization, one child in Africa dies from malaria every two minutes. With drug resistance continuing to undermine malaria control, there is an urgent need for new drugs and strategies that can stop infections before they have a chance to take hold. In the preclinical study published in Science, researchers developed a novel immunisation strategy that paired mosquito-delivered malaria parasites with an investigational class of antimalarial drug compounds, developed by WEHI and MSD. The compounds blocked the parasite's development at a critical stage of the malaria lifecycle, preventing disease and triggering a robust immune response that provided durable protection against malaria. Subsequent mosquito bites then reinforced this immunity and protection. Corresponding author and WEHI laboratory head Associate Professor Justin Boddey said the findings are an example of a drug candidate potently priming the immune system before disease could begin. Using this new drug compound, we've found a way to turn mosquito bites – the very thing that spreads malaria – into vaccination events in mice. This represents a shift in the way drugs could be employed to prevent malaria." Associate Professor Justin Boddey, WEHI The compounds were able to trap malaria parasites at the very end of liver-stage development – just before they enter the bloodstream and cause disease. To date, there had been no drug treatment available that could arrest the parasite at this immunological sweet spot. "This means the parasite was stopped just before it could cause illness, while giving the immune system a fuller preview of the potential threats," Prof Boddey said. "The immune response generated required only a very small dose of parasites but was broader and longer lasting than most current vaccine approaches. "This is because our approach allowed parasites to amplify and then triggered both antibodies and CD8+ T cells to protect against reinfection. "Importantly, this included liver‑resident memory T cells, which have the potential to respond rapidly to future infections and eliminate them before disease develops." Researchers hope the drug compounds may enable a 'vaccinate and boost naturally' approach, where progressive long-term immunity is learned and sustained through repeated natural mosquito bites in endemic areas. Critical industry collaboration The antimalarial drug candidates used in the study, WM382 and MK-7602, are both dual inhibitors of plasmepsin IX and X – two 'master regulators' that are crucial for parasite survival. The drug candidates are the result of a decade-long research collaboration between WEHI and MSD. WEHI researchers were able to achieve the strong immunity seen in the preclinical study by combining the drug compounds with small doses of malaria parasites transmitted by mosquito bites. John A. McCauley, Senior Director, Discovery Chemistry at MSD, said: "Current approaches often rely on genetically attenuated parasites, which can provide strong protection but require high doses and are difficult to produce, scale and administer in real‑world settings. "By using a drug to arrest parasites at the late liver stage, we've enabled the immune system to recognize a broader range of malaria antigens using a smaller parasite dose. "This approach may provide a broader response against the diversity of malaria parasites seen in the real-world and go beyond what genetically attenuated laboratory strains can achieve." "Despite using smaller parasite doses, we were able to generate strong and durable protective response in this study." As WM382 and MK-7602 target enzymes that are highly conserved across malaria species, researchers hope this will enable their approach to provide protection against a wide range of malaria 'variants' in the future – potentially allowing people in endemic areas to build immunity from natural mosquito bites over time. A Long-Acting Injectable based on the compounds is in preclinical development. If developed, this could prevent malaria by targeting the late liver stage, with the potential upside of vaccinating and boosting people with each mosquito bite. This work is supported by MSD, The Wellcome Trust, National Health and Medical Research Council of Australia (NHMRC) and Victorian State Government. The study, "Chemovaccination with a late liver-stage antimalarial induces durable immunity against malaria", with first author Dr Ryan Steel, is published in Science (DOI: 10.1126/science.aea7605).

澳大利亚墨尔本的WEHI研究人员发现了一种预防疟疾的新方法，有朝一日可能将蚊虫叮咬从致命感染源转变为对抗该疾病的免疫增强剂。 这个澳大利亚团队展示了一种新型疫苗接种方法，该方法能够预先调动免疫系统来对抗疟疾寄生虫，使其在致病前被消灭，而随后的蚊虫叮咬则作为增强剂，随着时间的推移不断强化免疫力。 这种方法在研究期间保护了小鼠免受疟疾侵害——这是一个罕见的成果，可能为开发针对全球最致命传染病之一的下一代预防策略提供信息。 概览 WEHI研究人员开发了一种新的疫苗接种方法，将蚊虫叮咬转化为持续的免疫增强剂，这项具有里程碑意义的研究已发表在《科学》杂志上。 这项临床前研究首次利用WEHI和全球生物制药公司MSD（美国新泽西州拉威市默克公司的商品名）发现的一种抗疟疾药物候选物，在疟疾寄生虫的肝脏晚期对其进行靶向，从而触发了异常强大且多层次的免疫应答。 这种方法保护了小鼠长达两年未患病——这一时间跨度涵盖了真实世界环境中的两个疟疾流行季节。 疟疾干预的“最佳时机” 每年有超过60万人死于疟疾，其中大部分是孕妇和五岁以下儿童。根据世界卫生组织的数据，非洲每两分钟就有一名儿童死于疟疾。 随着耐药性持续削弱疟疾控制，迫切需要新的药物和策略，以便在感染有机会站稳脚跟之前将其阻止。 在发表于《科学》杂志的临床前研究中，研究人员开发了一种新型免疫策略，该策略将蚊媒传播的疟疾寄生虫与WEHI和MSD开发的一类处于研究阶段的抗疟疾药物化合物相结合。 这些化合物在疟疾生命周期的关键阶段阻断了寄生虫的发育，从而预防了疾病，并触发了强大的免疫应答，提供了持久的疟疾保护。 随后的蚊虫叮咬则进一步强化了这种免疫力和保护作用。 论文通讯作者兼WEHI实验室负责人贾斯汀·博迪副教授表示，这些发现是一个药物候选物在疾病发生前有效预先调动免疫系统的例子。 “通过使用这种新型药物化合物，我们找到了一种方法，将蚊虫叮咬——正是传播疟疾的媒介——转化为小鼠体内的疫苗接种事件。” “这代表了药物用于预防疟疾方式的转变。” WEHI贾斯汀·博迪副教授说。 这些化合物能够在肝脏发育的末期——即寄生虫进入血液并导致疾病之前——捕获疟疾寄生虫。 迄今为止，还没有任何药物治疗能够在这个免疫学“最佳时机”阻止寄生虫。 “这意味着寄生虫在引发疾病前被阻止，同时给予免疫系统更全面的潜在威胁预警，”博迪教授说。 “所产生的免疫应答仅需要非常小剂量的寄生虫，但其广度和持久性都优于目前大多数疫苗方法。” “这是因为我们的方法允许寄生虫扩增，然后同时触发抗体和CD8+ T细胞，以防止再次感染。” “重要的是，这包括肝脏驻留记忆T细胞，它们有潜力对未来的感染迅速作出反应，并在疾病发生前将其清除。” 研究人员希望这些药物化合物能够实现一种“自然疫苗接种和增强”的方法，即在疟疾流行地区通过反复的自然蚊虫叮咬，学习并维持渐进的长期免疫力。 关键的行业合作 研究中使用的抗疟疾药物候选物WM382和MK-7602都是天门冬氨酸蛋白酶IX和X的双重抑制剂——这两种“主调控因子”对寄生虫的存活至关重要。 这些药物候选物是WEHI与MSD之间长达十年的研究合作成果。 WEHI研究人员通过将这些药物化合物与蚊虫叮咬传播的小剂量疟疾寄生虫相结合，在临床前研究中实现了强大的免疫力。 MSD发现化学高级总监约翰·A·麦考利表示：“目前的策略通常依赖基因减毒寄生虫，它们可以提供强大的保护，但需要高剂量，并且在真实世界环境中难以生产、规模化和给药。” “通过使用药物在肝脏晚期阶段阻止寄生虫，我们使免疫系统能够以更小的寄生虫剂量识别更广泛的疟疾抗原。” “这种方法可能会对真实世界中观察到的多样化疟疾寄生虫产生更广泛的应答，并超越基因减毒实验室菌株所能达到的效果。” “尽管使用了较小的寄生虫剂量，我们在这项研究中仍能产生强大而持久的保护性应答。” 由于WM382和MK-7602靶向在不同疟疾病种中高度保守的酶，研究人员希望这种方法未来能够提供针对各种疟疾“变体”的保护——从而可能使疫区人群随着时间推移通过自然蚊虫叮咬建立免疫力。 一种基于这些化合物的长效注射剂正在进行临床前开发。 如果成功开发，这可以通过靶向肝脏晚期来预防疟疾，并具有通过每次蚊虫叮咬为人们提供疫苗接种和免疫增强的潜在优势。 这项工作得到了MSD、惠康信托基金、澳大利亚国家健康与医学研究理事会（NHMRC）以及维多利亚州政府的支持。 这项题为“晚期肝脏阶段抗疟疾药物化学疫苗接种诱导持久性疟疾免疫力”的研究，由第一作者瑞安·斯蒂尔博士完成，已发表在《科学》杂志（DOI: 10.1126/science.aea7605）上。
