KIT researchers develop contact-free laser technique to rotate microscopic samples

Original Text
Until now, it has been technically nearly impossible to rotate highly sensitive samples in all directions under a microscope without making contact. Researchers at the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology (KIT) have developed a new laser-based technique that allows microscopic samples such as cells to be rotated contact-free in all three spatial directions. The laser creates tiny temperature differences in the liquid, which trigger gentle fluid flows that move the sample. This protects delicate samples and enables more accurate three-dimensional images—an important step for basic medical research.

Modern optical microscopes can produce extremely sharp images in a single plane, comparable to a photograph, but depth information is often imprecise. To overcome this limitation, samples must be imaged from multiple viewing angles and the images combined into a three-dimensional model. This requires rotating the object under investigation. The new method makes it possible to do this in an exceptionally gentle manner.

Rotation without physical contact

The research team led by Professor Moritz Kreysing and Dr. Fan Nan at the KIT's Institute of Biological and Chemical Systems uses a laser to locally heat the liquid in which the sample is suspended. This creates subtle fluid flows that can be used to precisely move freely floating microscopic objects—entirely without mechanical microtools such as tiny pipettes, needles, or grippers.

"We do not manipulate the sample directly," says Nan. "Instead, we control the movement of the surrounding liquid so that the object aligns itself."

Laser-driven flows have been known for some time, but previously only enabled motion within a single plane. Now, controlled rotation in three-dimensional space is also possible: by rapidly scanning the laser, the researchers generate a spiral flow that gently rotates objects—similar to a small paper boat spinning on its own in a tiny whirlpool.

Benefits for medicine and technology

Three-dimensional control allows cellular structures to be captured more effectively from different perspectives.

When samples can be aligned more precisely, we see more details. This is a key prerequisite for better understanding biological structures and processes." Professor Moritz Kreysing, KIT's Institute of Biological and Chemical Systems

In the long term, the method could also become relevant for contact-free micromanipulation, microscopic robotics, or highly precise manufacturing at the smallest scales, according to Kreysing.

现代光学显微镜能够在单一平面上产生极其清晰的图像，类似于照片，但深度信息往往不精确。为了克服这一局限，样本必须从多个视角进行成像，并将这些图像组合成一个三维模型。这要求对被研究物体进行旋转。新方法使得以极其温和的方式实现这一点成为可能。

无物理接触旋转

由莫里茨·克雷辛教授和范楠博士领导的卡尔斯鲁厄理工学院生物与化学系统研究所研究团队，利用激光局部加热样本悬浮其中的液体。这产生了微妙的流体流动，可用于精确移动自由漂浮的微观物体——完全无需微型移液管、针头或夹具等机械微工具。

“我们不直接操纵样本，”范博士说。“相反，我们控制周围液体的运动，使物体自行对齐。”

激光驱动的流体流动已有一段时间为人所知，但此前仅能实现单一平面内的运动。现在，三维空间中的受控旋转也成为可能：通过快速扫描激光，研究人员产生了一种螺旋流，轻柔地旋转物体——这类似于一艘小纸船在微型漩涡中自行旋转。

对医学和技术的益处

三维控制使得能够从不同视角更有效地捕捉细胞结构。

“当样本能够更精确地对齐时，我们就能看到更多细节。这是更好地理解生物结构和过程的关键前提。”——卡尔斯鲁厄理工学院生物与化学系统研究所莫里茨·克雷辛教授

克雷辛教授表示，从长远来看，该方法还可能对无接触微操纵、微观机器人技术或在最小尺度上的高精度制造具有重要意义。
