Young adults with cancer report putting off appointments, getting dismissed by doctors

深度解析

文章揭示了年轻癌症患者普遍存在的诊断延迟问题，这在临床实践中是一个严峻挑战。核心机制在于多重因素交织：首先是患者层面的症状评估偏差。年轻人常将身体不适归因于压力或个体差异，缺乏对癌症早期警示信号（如异常肿块、持续出血、不明原因疼痛）的认知。这种误判，结合在线信息泛滥但指导不明，导致了长期的求医拖延。其次是医疗提供者层面的专业响应。尽管癌症在年轻人中相对罕见，但当患者反复报告典型症状时，医生的初步诊察若过于简化或倾向于排除严重病因，未能及时进行充分的临床检测（如影像学检查、实验室检查），则可能延误确诊。这种“临床盲点”可能源于对年轻患者病情的固有假设或系统性压力。诊断延迟的后果是严重的，可能导致疾病进展至更晚期，从而降低治愈率、增加治疗难度和并发症风险。对睾丸癌、宫颈癌等在年轻人中相对高发的肿瘤而言，早期诊断是改善预后的关键。因此，行业影响层面，公共卫生宣教需更精准地触达年轻群体，提升其健康素养与自我倡导能力；同时，临床医生需加强针对年轻患者的鉴别诊断意识和沟通技巧培训，确保每一位患者的症状都能得到严谨对待。此外，探索人工智能辅助患者记录症状、整理病史，可作为辅助工具，提高就诊效率和准确性，而非替代专业诊疗。

Credit: Douglas Levere When Natasha Allard's close friend Dave was diagnosed with stage 4 testicular cancer at age 26, the University at Buffalo postdoctoral associate started paying more attention to how cancer was upending the lives of young people. What she saw would eventually change the course of her career. At the time, Allard, Ph.D. '26, was working in marketing and communications at Roswell Park Comprehensive Cancer Center. She learned that cancer is increasingly being diagnosed in young adults, while holding steady or declining in older adults. This knowledge spurred Allard, who earned her MBA from UB in 2014, to pursue a doctorate in community health and health behavior. She wanted to focus on educating young adults about paying attention to their bodies, listening to their instincts and getting symptoms checked out sooner. Under the mentorship of Heather Orom, Ph.D., associate professor of community health and health behavior in the School of Public Health and Health Professions, Allard completed her dissertation. She found many young adults waited months or even years after noticing symptoms to see a physician. She developed that study into an article published Aug. 22 in the journal Cancer Causes & Control. Orom served as co-author, along with Thomas Feeley, Ph.D., SUNY distinguished professor in the UB Department of Communication, two researchers with Roswell Park and one with Hunter College's Department of Psychology. According to the American Cancer Society, cancer now ranks among the five leading causes of death for adults ages 20 to 39. For young adult females, it's the leading cause of disease-related death. "There is a lot of research on cancer treatment and prevention, as there should be," Allard says. "But there's much less on what people do between noticing a symptom and getting a diagnosis, and that time matters. With young adults, I wanted to know: how did they decide whether their symptoms were serious? And how did doctors respond?" At the beginning of 2023, Allard interviewed 30 individuals between the ages of 20 and 39 nationwide who had been diagnosed with cancer. She asked the interviewees to take her back to that first symptom they remembered or first gut feeling that something was wrong. On average, participants were 28 when diagnosed and about four years past their diagnosis. She found that many put off seeking care because they blamed their symptoms on stress. They also found information online that downplayed symptoms or offered unclear guidance. And when they did see a doctor, their concerns were often dismissed. The delays in seeking medical care didn't surprise Allard. She notes that young adulthood is often marked by many concurrent, sometimes stressful, changes—new jobs, new living arrangements and new friendships. Some participants also didn't know which changes in their body warranted concern. One young woman, for instance, had abnormal bleeding for a year but figured her body was just "built differently." She was later diagnosed with cervical cancer at age 35. What did surprise Allard was how often doctors brushed off patients' concerns, even when they reported classic warning signs. One provider suggested a gluten-free diet for a patient with rectal bleeding who was later diagnosed with colorectal cancer at 39. Of two patients later diagnosed with brain cancer, a 23-year-old was sent home with a sleep aid, and a 27-year-old had to make three or four trips to doctors before anyone ordered an MRI. Allard acknowledges that doctors face constraints, from overloaded schedules to insurance limitations. And despite rising rates, cancer is still rare in young adults, and symptoms like fatigue and headaches usually have less serious causes. "But a symptom that won't go away still deserves a closer look," she says. "And young adults should feel empowered to speak up and push for testing." Speaking up was easier for some of the interviewees than others. Those who felt comfortable navigating the health system kept pushing for answers, while others often accepted their doctor's first explanation and waited longer for a diagnosis. Allard says the findings point to opportunities for public health communicators and educators. She would like to see campaigns on social media and in public places where young adults spend time to help them recognize warning signs and feel more confident seeking care. "Not everyone knows a lump or abnormal bleeding means you should go to the doctor," she says. Young adults also need to hear that physical symptoms caused by stress shouldn't be normalized, she adds. A doctor should help find the cause or, if it is stress, connect them with resources to manage it. And because so many young adults already turn to the internet and increasingly to artificial intelligence when something feels off, Allard sees a chance to use those tools to prepare for appointments rather than replace them. "Instead of using AI solely for self-diagnosis, why not use it to make a list of questions and a timeline of the symptoms you've had?" she says. Doctors have a role to play, too. Allard recommends communication training focused on listening to young adults' concerns and giving clear follow-up instructions. She also points to the need for system-level fixes, from insurance coverage to access to care. "If you're not going to order any clinical testing for a patient who has had severe headaches for months, then you need to ask the right questions and explain the plan clearly, so they don't walk away feeling completely dismissed or completely terrified," she says. Allard's friend Dave, whose diagnosis at 26 first set her on this path, died at age 31. She dedicated her dissertation to him. More information Natasha C.
