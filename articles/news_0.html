Behavioral therapy can be just as effective as medication for Tourette syndrome, says researcher

Behavioral therapy is just as effective as medication for Tourette syndrome. This is the finding of doctoral research conducted by clinical psychologist Jolande van de Griendt, who will receive her Ph.D. from Radboud University on July 3, 2026. "Medication is still often the first step in treating a tic disorder. But for many people, it's a great relief to be able to do something about their symptoms without taking pills."

During behavioral therapy, people with Tourette syndrome learn to perform competing movements to counteract their tics, or they learn to suppress their tics for increasingly longer periods. This latter form of treatment is called Exposure and Response Prevention (ERP).

Behavioral therapy

In ERP, patients practice suppressing their tics for as long as possible. Many patients feel a strong urge to perform a tic and initially delay it for only a few seconds. Over time, they progress to a minute and, after a few sessions, perhaps even 15 minutes. "In treatment, we focus on that feeling," says Van de Griendt. "We make it harder by, for example, talking about the tic—then that tic really wants to come out."

Van de Griendt observed that, for most people, the urge to perform a tic grew stronger during the first 15 minutes of the treatment session and then stabilized rather than subsided. "The trick is to delay the tics for longer and longer periods when that urge is strongest," she explains. "That way, you get used to the feeling, just like when you jump into cold water. At first it's unpleasant, but your body gradually learns to tolerate the cold. Once you're used to it, you can still swim just fine. In the same way, you can learn to tolerate the urge, and by practicing every day, the tics often become less frequent."

No pills

The psychologist compared the effectiveness of this type of behavioral therapy with that of medication (antipsychotics, which were previously the standard first-line treatment). The effects turned out to be more or less the same.

"I was slightly disappointed," says Van de Griendt, who has run her own practice specializing in behavioral therapy for tics and Tourette syndrome since 2016. "I actually expected behavioral therapy to be more effective than medication, but that wasn't the case."

It did, however, prove difficult to recruit enough participants because of the mandatory random assignment. Many people preferred behavioral therapy and did not want to risk being assigned to the medication group. "Medication isn't very popular among patients anymore," van de Griendt says. "Especially for children, side effects such as fatigue and weight gain can be quite severe. On the other hand, behavioral therapy naturally requires more effort from the patient. That's why it's important that people can choose for themselves."

She also concluded that patients benefited just as much from one-hour behavioral therapy sessions as they did from two-hour sessions. "That's less tiring for the patient, and if we shorten those long sessions by an hour, we could treat twice as many people," says the Ph.D. candidate. "That's important because specialized treatment for tic disorders isn't available everywhere."

Treatment is not always necessary

Tourette syndrome is the best-known tic disorder, in which people experience involuntary movements and vocalizations for more than a year. It usually begins between ages 6 and 8, with symptoms peaking between ages 10 and 12. After puberty, one in three children outgrows the condition.

Tourette syndrome affects about one in every 100 people, but not everyone experiences symptoms that require treatment. For example, some people may have a mild cough, frequently shake their head, or blink excessively without finding these behaviors bothersome. Temporary tics are even more common, occurring in one in every five to 10 children. In most cases, these resolve on their own.

"Sometimes parents come to my practice because they want their child's tics treated, even though the child isn't bothered by them," van de Griendt continues. "That's a shame. Tics shouldn't be something to be ashamed of. But some people experience muscle pain because of their tics or become very tired as a result. In those cases, treatment is appropriate."

行为疗法对妥瑞氏综合征的疗效与药物治疗不相上下。这是临床心理学家Jolande van de Griendt博士研究的发现，她将于2026年7月3日从拉德堡德大学获得博士学位。“药物仍然常常是治疗抽动障碍的第一步。但对许多人来说，能够不用服药就能改善症状，这是一种极大的解脱。”

在行为疗法中，妥瑞氏综合征患者学习进行竞争性动作来对抗他们的抽动，或者学习抑制抽动的时间越来越长。后一种治疗形式被称为暴露与反应预防（ERP）。

行为疗法

在ERP中，患者练习尽可能长时间地抑制抽动。许多患者感到强烈的抽动冲动，最初只能延迟几秒钟。随着时间的推移，他们会进步到一分钟，在几次治疗后，甚至可能达到15分钟。“在治疗中，我们关注那种感觉，”Van de Griendt说，“我们通过例如谈论抽动来增加难度——那样抽动就真的想表现出来。”

Van de Griendt观察到，对大多数人而言，在治疗会议的前15分钟内，进行抽动的冲动会变得更强，然后趋于稳定而非消退。“诀窍在于，当这种冲动最强烈时，将抽动延迟更长的时间，”她解释道，“这样，你就会习惯这种感觉，就像跳入冷水一样。起初会不舒服，但你的身体会逐渐学会忍受寒冷。一旦你适应了，你仍然可以很好地游泳。同样地，你可以学会忍受这种冲动，通过每天练习，抽动往往会变得不那么频繁。”

无需药物

这位心理学家比较了这种行为疗法与药物（抗精神病药物，曾是标准的一线治疗方案）的疗效。结果发现，两者的效果大体相同。

“我略感失望，”Van de Griendt说，她自2016年以来一直经营着自己的诊所，专门从事抽动症和妥瑞氏综合征的行为疗法。“我本来期望行为疗法比药物更有效，但事实并非如此。”

然而，由于强制性的随机分配，招募到足够多的参与者被证明是困难的。许多人更倾向于行为疗法，不愿冒被分配到药物组的风险。“药物在患者中不再很受欢迎，”van de Griendt说，“特别是对儿童而言，疲劳和体重增加等副作用可能相当严重。另一方面，行为疗法自然需要患者付出更多努力。因此，重要的是人们可以自己选择。”

她还得出结论，患者从一小时的行为治疗疗程中获得的益处与两小时的疗程相同。“这让患者更不疲惫，如果我们把那些长时间的疗程缩短一小时，我们就能治疗两倍的人数，”这位博士研究生说，“这很重要，因为抽动障碍的专业治疗并非随处可得。”

并非总是需要治疗

妥瑞氏综合征是最广为人知的抽动障碍，患者会出现持续一年以上的不自主动作和发声。它通常在6到8岁之间开始，症状在10到12岁之间达到高峰。青春期后，三分之一的儿童会摆脱这种状况。

妥瑞氏综合征影响大约每100人中的一人，但并非所有人都出现需要治疗的症状。例如，有些人可能只有轻微咳嗽、频繁摇头或过度眨眼，而这些行为并不让他们感到困扰。暂时性抽动更为常见，每5到10名儿童中就有一人出现。在大多数情况下，这些抽动会自行消退。

“有时家长来到我的诊所，因为他们想治疗孩子的抽动，即使孩子自己并不为此困扰，”van de Griendt继续说，“这很可惜。抽动不应该成为羞耻的事情。但有些人因为抽动而感到肌肉疼痛，或者因此变得非常疲惫。在这些情况下，治疗是适当的。”
