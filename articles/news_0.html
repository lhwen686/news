新研究揭示耳念珠菌如何规避抗真菌药物

New study reveals how Candida auris evades antifungal drugs

深度解析

这项研究揭示了致命真菌耳念珠菌（Candida auris）产生抗真菌药物耐药性的一种意想不到的新机制：它通过复制其整个基因组的多个版本，并伴随“Crazy-one”（CRZ1）等关键基因的突变。传统抗真菌药物通常靶向细胞壁合成的关键酶，以破坏真菌结构。然而，耳念珠菌采用“风险对冲”策略，在高浓度药物压力下，通过基因组多拷贝和CRZ1基因突变（该基因在细胞壁完整性中发挥作用），使其细胞壁结构未受药物影响，从而逃避药效并持续生长。 这一发现对全球公共卫生和制药行业具有深远影响。耳念珠菌已被世卫组织列为关键优先病原体，其独特的进化耐药机制（不同于常见的FKS1/2变异）削弱了现有抗真菌药物的有效性。这凸显了开发新型诊断工具和治疗策略的紧迫性，如探索联合用药或靶向新机制的药物。此研究将指导未来的抗真菌药物研发，改进医院感染控制措施，并降低重症监护病房的致死率，以应对这种日益严峻的临床挑战。

英文原文

Original
New research has revealed an unexpected new mechanism through which the killer fungus Candida auris is resisting antifungal drug treatment. A team led by the University of Exeter and City St George's, University of London, discovered that the fungus creates multiple versions of its entire genome, including mutating a gene which plays a role in cell wall integrity, called "Crazy-one" (CRZ1). The study is now published in Nature Communications. The research was primarily funded by the Medical Research Council, with support from the NIHR Biomedical Research Centre: Exeter. Study authors say their findings unlock a brand new mechanism that allows Candida auris to adapt to antifungal treatment and survive, which could lead to new ways to tackle the fungus. Candida auris was only described for the first time in 2008, and its origins remain a mystery. Since it emerged, outbreaks have been reported on every continent, in more than 60 countries in total, including the UK. It has been named a global public health threat, and is on the World Health Organization's critical priority fungal pathogens list. Study first author Dr Hugh Gifford, Clinical lecturer at the University of Exeter's MRC Centre for Medical Mycology, is also an intensive care physician. He said: "Candida auris is rare in the general population, but it's a deadly threat to patients in intensive care units. It clings to patient skin and surrounding equipment, and can be transmitted from person to person – it's incredibly hard to eradicate. If infection enters a vulnerable patient's blood stream, there is a 45 per cent death rate. "Outbreaks in intensive care units have been costly and highly disruptive. One unit in the UK had to close and undergo complete refurbishment to stop the outbreak, while another outbreak was estimated to cost £1 million to control. " The research team was initially testing for drug resistance when a researcher accidentally left plates of cells incubating for longer than the required test period. The researcher returned to find that cells had unexpectedly grown during this additional time period. They were surprised to find that further testing showed the deadly fungus had survived antifungal treatment by creating multiple versions of its own genome, thereby increasing its chances of mutating to evade the drug and survive. We initially set out to investigate why antifungal drugs weren't working to kill Candida auris, despite laboratory tests showing that they were not resistant and that the drugs should have been effective. We were surprised to discover an entirely new mechanism for this fungus to survive – particularly with luck playing a big part of the discovery. We tested the fungus against very high concentrations of antifungal drugs. We were amazed to find there was a tiny population of cells that appeared over several days and continued to grow. Our discovery means we need to now look at new ways of treating people and eradicating this deadly fungus from hospitals." Dr. Sarah Murphy, former postdoctoral researcher at City St George's University of London and co-first author Expanding further on the finding, Dr Hugh Gifford said: "This study had a number of surprising elements. Firstly, we did not identify any of the DNA mutations that we might have expected. Antifungal drugs target the enzymes that make cell walls, so that fungi die by shriveling, exploding, or not being able to grow. Somehow, these fungi had survived antifungal drugs, and we needed to find out why. We were surprised to find that surviving cells had made multiple copies of its entire genome. This is a trick that other fungi have used in the past, but not one that is well known in C. auris. In fact, there were two populations, some growing slowly, others growing more rapidly. We call it 'bet-hedging' when a bug uses multiple tactics to increases its chances of developing resistance and evading treatment." The team found that the more rapidly growing cells contained a wide range of mutations in a gene that has been studied in other organisms for its role in governing cell wall synthesis, called "Crazy-one" (CRZ1). Further tests confirmed the rapid growth of CRZ1 mutants, and that their cell walls appeared to be unaffected by antifungal drugs. Researchers are now looking at options including the use of antifungal drugs in combination, which is being addressed via the Exeter-led UKRI funded Fungi-CARE programme, also in partnership with colleagues at City St George's. Senior author Professor Tihana Bicanic, Professor of Infectious Diseases and Mycology at the School of Health & Medical Sciences at City St George's, University of London, said: "We now believe that fungal infections are more complicated than previously thought. Multiple populations may exist during infection. New and emerging human fungal infections, like Candida auris, essentially have a Swiss Army Knife of innovation with their own genome, leading to stepwise adaptation and drug resistance. The implication of these results are that we now know a new mechanism of evolution that weakens our antifungal drug arsenal. So, we are sounding the alarm for the need to make better antifungal drugs and urgently address our ability to prevent fungal deaths." The study is titled 'FKS1/2-variant independent mechanisms underlying the 1 emergence of resistance in echinocandin-refractory 2 Candida auris infections' and is published in Nature Communications.

新研究揭示了杀手真菌耳念珠菌抵抗抗真菌药物治疗的一种意想不到的新机制。 由埃克塞特大学和伦敦大学圣乔治学院共同领导的一个团队发现，这种真菌会产生其整个基因组的多个版本，包括一个在细胞壁完整性中发挥作用的基因发生突变，该基因被称为“Crazy-one”（CRZ1）。 该研究现已发表在《自然通讯》上。这项研究主要由医学研究委员会资助，并得到了NIHR埃克塞特生物医学研究中心的支持。 研究作者表示，他们的发现揭示了一种全新的机制，使耳念珠菌能够适应抗真菌治疗并存活，这可能为应对这种真菌提供新方法。 耳念珠菌于2008年首次被描述，其起源至今仍是个谜。自其出现以来，全球各大洲已有超过60个国家报告了疫情，其中包括英国。它已被列为全球公共卫生威胁，并位列世界卫生组织的关键优先真菌病原体清单。 该研究的第一作者、埃克塞特大学医学真菌学MRC中心临床讲师休·吉福德博士，同时也是一名重症监护医师。他说：“耳念珠菌在普通人群中很少见，但对重症监护病房的患者来说，它是一种致命的威胁。它附着在患者皮肤和周围设备上，并可在人际间传播——极难根除。如果感染进入脆弱患者的血液，死亡率高达45%。” “重症监护病房的疫情代价高昂且极具破坏性。英国有一个病房不得不关闭并进行彻底翻新以阻止疫情，而另一起疫情的控制成本估计为100万英镑。” 研究团队最初正在测试耐药性，当时一名研究人员意外地让细胞培养皿孵育时间超过了所需的测试周期。研究人员回来后发现，在额外的这段时间里细胞意外地生长了。他们惊讶地发现，进一步测试显示这种致命真菌通过创建自身基因组的多个版本来抵抗抗真菌治疗并存活，从而增加了其变异以逃避药物和生存的机会。 “我们最初着手调查为什么抗真菌药物未能杀死耳念珠菌，尽管实验室测试显示它们并未产生耐药性，且药物应该有效。我们惊讶地发现这种真菌存活的全新机制——尤其是在这次发现中运气起到了很大作用。我们用非常高浓度的抗真菌药物测试了这种真菌。我们惊奇地发现，在几天内出现了一小部分细胞并持续生长。我们的发现意味着我们现在需要寻找新的方法来治疗患者并从医院中根除这种致命真菌。”伦敦大学圣乔治学院前博士后研究员、共同第一作者莎拉·墨菲博士说。 吉福德博士进一步阐述了这一发现：“这项研究有许多令人惊讶的元素。首先，我们没有发现任何我们可能预期的DNA突变。抗真菌药物通过靶向构成细胞壁的酶来发挥作用，从而使真菌因萎缩、爆裂或无法生长而死亡。然而，这些真菌却以某种方式在抗真菌药物作用下存活了下来，我们需要找出原因。我们惊讶地发现，存活下来的细胞复制了其整个基因组的多个副本。这是一种其他真菌在过去使用过的策略，但在耳念珠菌中并不广为人知。事实上，存在两个群体，一些生长缓慢，另一些生长更快。当病原体利用多种策略来增加其产生耐药性和逃避治疗的机会时，我们称之为‘风险对冲’。” 该团队发现，生长较快的细胞中，一个在其他生物体中因其在调控细胞壁合成中的作用而被研究过的基因——“Crazy-one”（CRZ1）——存在广泛的突变。进一步的测试证实了CRZ1突变体的快速生长，并且它们的细胞壁似乎未受抗真菌药物的影响。 研究人员现在正在研究包括联合使用抗真菌药物在内的方案，这项工作正在通过埃克塞特大学主导、UKRI资助的Fungi-CARE项目进行，并与伦敦大学圣乔治学院的同事合作。 资深作者、伦敦大学圣乔治学院健康与医学科学学院传染病学和真菌学教授蒂哈娜·比卡尼奇教授表示：“我们现在认为真菌感染比以前认为的更为复杂。感染过程中可能存在多个真菌群体。新出现的和正在流行的人类真菌感染，如耳念珠菌，实质上拥有一个基因组的‘瑞士军刀’式创新能力，导致逐步适应和耐药性。这些结果的意义在于，我们现在知道了削弱我们抗真菌药物储备的一种新的进化机制。因此，我们正在敲响警钟，呼吁开发更好的抗真菌药物，并紧急提升我们预防真菌感染致死的能力。” 该研究的标题是《FKS1/2变异非依赖性机制在棘白菌素难治性耳念珠菌感染耐药性出现中的作用》，并发表在《自然通讯》上。
