该文章揭示了未经监管的合成肽在“健康养生”和健美领域日益泛滥的严峻公共健康问题。肽是氨基酸的短链，既天然存在于体内，参与组织修复等关键生理过程，也是胰岛素、GLP-1减肥药等临床药物的活性成分。然而，文中提及的GHK-Cu、瑞他鲁肽（Retatrutide）、塞兰克（Selank）和BPC-157等未经授权的合成肽，其在非临床用途中的作用机制及安全性均缺乏充分的人体研究和证据支持。例如，瑞他鲁肽作为一种多靶点（GLP-1/GIP/GCG）受体激动剂，在严格的临床试验中展现出治疗肥胖和糖尿病的潜力，但在非法市场中，其未经批准的使用已与急性肝损伤等严重不良事件相关。BPC-157虽在动物研究中显示出组织修复的希望，但人体数据却极为匮乏。 这种现象对行业产生了深远影响。首先，未经监管的肽市场充斥着虚假宣传和严重的健康风险，对公众生命安全构成威胁。其次，社交媒体和网红的推波助澜，加速了错误信息的传播，挑战了传统医疗的专业性和科学循证原则。第三，监管机构面临巨大压力，如何在创新和安全之间取得平衡，以及有效打击非法销售是当前亟待解决的难题。最终，这种对“生物学优化”的非理性追求，不仅可能扭曲个体对健康的认知，也暴露出医药产品研发、审批与市场乱象之间的巨大鸿沟。

Thomas's phone alarm buzzes at 9:30 p.m.—it's time for the aspiring bodybuilder to start injecting several banned peptides into his stomach. He takes vials of GHK-Cu, which he tells AFP will give him "beautiful skin," and Retatrutide, which will help him "feel less hungry," from his refrigerator. After adding a liquid to reconstitute the synthetic peptides, he disinfects the syringes and injects both into his lower abdomen. Thomas, whose name has been changed, takes GHK-Cu daily and Retatrutide three times a week. "It takes a little organization, but it's become a routine," said Thomas, who goes to the gym almost every day. He is one of a growing number of people attempting to "optimize" themselves by using peptides, a class of substances widely promoted online by influencers, celebrities and the wellness industry. Viral social media posts claim peptides can offer a range of seemingly miraculous benefits, including glowing skin, stronger muscles, deeper sleep and a more alert mind—and even provide an avenue to prevent aging. However, experts warn there is little evidence to support these claims and that, because these peptides have not been studied properly, they can pose serious health risks. That is why these peptides remain banned in many countries, including France, where Thomas lives. In the United States, however, a panel recommended loosening restrictions on a range of peptides last month. The decision outraged many medical experts, who accused Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.—an avowed fan and user of peptides—of packing the panel with people who have strong ties to the peptide industry. Very real health risks Peptides are short chains of amino acids that assemble to form proteins, which are the body's building blocks. They occur naturally in the body, helping dictate processes such as repairing tissue. Peptides reproduced in a lab are the active ingredients in many common medications, including insulin and GLP-1 weight-loss drugs. However, unauthorized forms of synthetic peptides sold online have increasingly been promoted by influencers and gyms promising a more effective workout. Thomas was already using steroids when he was introduced to peptides six months ago. Along with GHK-Cu and Retatrutide, he sometimes injects himself with Selank for help with anxiety and BPC-157, which promises to repair soft tissue, such as ligaments, more quickly. Because these peptides are not authorized for sale in France, there are no official guidelines for how to use them. In online discussion forums such as Reddit and Discord, communities of mostly men exchange advice on how much and how often they should inject themselves. "You read the studies, you look at the lab tests, and you see all the positive effects," said Thomas, who believes that the risks are "rare and not serious." Self-experimentation 'worthless' However, neuroscientist Armelle Rancillac at the French research institute INSERM warned that knowledge of how to use these peptides remains limited because they "have often not been tested on humans." Earlier this year, INSERM said that "no large-scale, randomized controlled clinical trial has confirmed" the promised benefits of peptides, warning that some pose "very real health risks." "We are not giving research enough time to prove its worth," Rancillac emphasized, adding that "scientifically worthless" accounts of self-experimentation were being given too much credence. Numerous nations have reported people being hospitalized after using peptides. In June, Australia's government reported six cases of acute liver injury linked to products labeled Retatrutide. It is difficult to determine how many people are using peptides, but the recent increase in exposure is enough to "raise alarm," Fabien Ohl, a sports sociologist at the University of Lausanne in Switzerland, told AFP. Ohl criticized the "biological view" being pushed online that the human body is "capital" to be exploited by taking chemical substances to improve one's physical appearance. For example, one website selling peptides promised to provide "the chemical architecture for those seeking absolute biological optimization." Self-proclaimed experts online also sell advice vowing to help men "optimize" their physique. Ohl criticized this view of masculinity as problematic, warning it normalized an idea of "the biological male who is potentially more violent and aggressive."
