深度解析

这项研究揭示了全氟和多氟烷基物质（PFAS，特别是PFOA和PFOS）与雌激素受体阴性（ER阴性）乳腺癌风险增加的关联，具有重要的医学和公共卫生意义。ER阴性乳腺癌因缺乏雌激素受体，无法通过内分泌治疗干预，通常表现出更强的侵袭性、更高的转移风险和更差的预后，且已知风险因素较少，治疗选择相对有限。本研究提出的PFAS可能通过非激素生物学途径（如基因活性或免疫系统改变）影响癌症风险，而非传统的内分泌干扰机制，这为ER阴性乳腺癌的发病机制研究开辟了新方向。 从行业影响来看，“永久性化学品”PFAS的广泛存在及其在人体内的持久性，凸显了环境毒理学在疾病预防中的关键作用。该研究基于大规模队列研究“姐妹研究”并结合荟萃分析，增强了结果的可靠性。鉴于传统PFAS正被新型化合物取代，未来研究需紧急评估这些替代品对乳腺癌及其他健康风险的影响。这要求监管机构和化学工业对新化学品的安全性进行更严格的审查，同时促使公众提高对日常环境中潜在致癌物的认知，并采取措施减少个人暴露，以期开辟新的疾病预防途径。

英文原文

Original
Women with higher blood levels of two common per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, also familiarly known as PFAS, were more likely to develop estrogen receptor-negative, or ER-negative, breast cancer, according to a new study by the National Institutes of Health (NIH), part of the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS). PFAS are a large group of chemicals used in common consumer products. Often referred to as "forever chemicals," PFAS do not break down easily in the environment and can remain in the body for years. The study found that women with higher blood plasma levels of two of these substances, perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) and perfluorooctanesulfonic acid (PFOS), were associated with a higher incidence of ER-negative breast cancer. No clear association was observed between the two chemicals and breast cancer risk overall. Women deserve clear answers about how chemicals in their environment may affect their health. This NIH study links two forever chemicals to a higher risk of ER-negative breast cancer and demands further investigation. Under President Trump's leadership, we will pursue the evidence, confront environmental health risks, and give women the information they need to protect their health." Robert F. Kennedy, Jr., HHS Secretary ER-negative tumors lack receptors that allow hormone estrogen to help cancer cells grow. Compared with breast cancers that respond to hormone estrogen, ER-negative breast cancers tend to be more aggressive and have worse outcomes. Scientists know less about what raises the risk of this type of breast cancer. "Our findings, which we confirmed using a meta-analysis of prior research, indicate that certain legacy PFAS, including PFOA and PFOS, are associated with a higher risk of ER-negative breast cancer, a more aggressive tumor type with few known risk factors," explained NIH Stadtman Investigator Alexandra White, Ph.D., who led the study. The findings are reported in the Journal of Clinical Oncology. NIH researchers and their collaborators measured 17 PFAS in blood, collected years before women were diagnosed with cancer. Women in the group with the highest PFOA levels had a 67% higher rate of ER-negative breast cancer than those in the lowest group. For PFOS, the rate was 93% higher, or nearly twice as high. These numbers describe relative differences between groups, not an individual woman's chance of getting breast cancer. The research drew on the Sister Study, led by NIH's National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences (NIEHS). The larger study enrolled 50,884 women ages 35–74 in the United States and Puerto Rico from 2003 to 2009. None had breast cancer when they joined the Sister Study, but each had a full or half-sister who had been previously diagnosed with breast cancer. For the PFAS study, researchers included 1,609 women who developed breast cancer and a comparison sample of 2,269 women. "These findings matter for women's health broadly," said first author, Che-Jung Chang, Ph.D., a former research fellow at NIH and a current researcher at DLH, a scientific research organization. "Although PFAS are persistent in the environment and in our bodies, exposure to them is still potentially modifiable, meaning people can take steps to reduce their exposure, and these findings could open new avenues for prevention." Because the strongest associations involved tumors without hormone receptors, the authors suggest that PFAS may affect cancer risk through other non-hormonal biologic pathways. Possible pathways include changes in gene activity or immune system. However, this study did not test those explanations. The authors say future studies should evaluate people with higher PFAS exposure and in groups more affected by ER-negative breast cancer. Although most individuals have measurable levels of PFOA and PFOS in their bodies, these compounds have been largely phased out and replaced with new PFAS compounds. "More research is needed, particularly evaluating the risk of breast cancer associated with newer replacement PFAS chemicals," White said.
