新结构在大脑连接图谱中显现

New structures come into view in brain wiring maps

深度解析

本文介绍的“可扩展电子断层扫描”技术，是连接组学研究的重大突破，首次实现了对大体积脑组织进行纳米尺度（约15纳米）的高效高精度绘制。该技术通过优化成像流程，解决了传统方法在速度与分辨率上的局限，能够揭示传统连接组图谱中缺失的关键精细结构，例如电突触（间隙连接）以及神经元与胶质细胞间进行物质交换的微小突起。 这项创新对神经科学和神经内科领域影响深远。电突触的精确观测对于理解神经网络的快速同步和高级认知功能至关重要，其异常可能与癫痫、自闭症等神经系统疾病的病理生理机制密切相关。神经元“啃食”胶质细胞的发现，则提示了神经元与支持细胞间存在更为复杂的代谢、信号或免疫调节交互，为神经发育障碍和神经退行性疾病的研究提供了新视角和潜在靶点。更完整的脑连接组图谱将为深入解析疾病的结构基础和功能异常提供有力工具，从而推动新型诊断生物标志物的开发和精准治疗策略的制定，有望革新临床对复杂脑部疾病的理解与干预，尤其在神经内科的诊断与治疗中具有潜在的突破性意义。

Credit: Kuan A, et al., Nature Methods (November 2026) Researchers at Harvard Medical School and the University of California San Diego have sped up a powerful imaging technique enough to map, for the first time, large volumes of brain tissue in nanoscale detail. The approach reveals fine structures that brain wiring maps, known as connectomes, can miss. The work, described in Nature Methods, promises to help scientists build more complete diagrams of how cells in the brain are connected. This, in turn, could deepen understanding of nervous-system development and certain brain diseases. "We can now see brain structures that we haven't been able to see before in connectomics data," said co-senior author Wei-Chung Allen Lee, associate professor of neurobiology at HMS and HMS associate professor of neurology at Boston Children's Hospital. "We hope this work will help us understand how nervous systems are built; what changes with age; and what goes awry in neurodevelopmental, cognitive, and neurodegenerative disorders," Lee said. The gaps in brain wiring maps Neuroscientists build connectomes to understand how neurons work together to produce thought and behavior. Some research efforts chart the brain's major highways using MRI scans. Others zoom in, using electron microscopy to trace individual neurons and the synapses where they meet, as in a recently completed connectome of the fruit fly brain and nerve cord that Lee co-led with researchers from around the world. However, even these fine-grained maps give up some detail when imaging large amounts of tissue. Electrical synapses, also called gap junctions, are among the key structures they can miss. Fewer images, thicker tissue samples To capture that detail, the team turned to electron tomography, a technique that builds 3D images by viewing a sample from multiple angles. Electron tomography is usually slow because it requires taking hundreds of images of the same area. The researchers found that they could use about 50 times fewer images than standard electron tomography, on tissue sections roughly 20 times thicker than usual, and still achieve 3D resolution of about 15 nanometers or finer. Hidden connections and cellular exchanges Applying the technique to tissue from the mouse cerebellum, the team saw electrical synapses, which let signals pass almost instantly between neurons and are thought to help synchronize network activity. They also saw tiny protrusions extending between neurons and neighboring support cells called glia. The researchers say these structures appear to be involved in one cell taking up material from another. "We're seeing uncharacterized structures, suggesting that neurons are 'nibbling' their neighboring cells," Lee said. In addition, the team traced nerve fibers through a 3D block of tissue to the points where those fibers connect with other neurons. Scaling the approach to an entire mammalian brain will take more work, including automating how tissue samples are handled, the researchers said. That is a long-term goal for the team. Publication details Aaron T. Kuan et al., Scalable electron tomography for connectomics, Nature Methods (2026).
