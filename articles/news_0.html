Original
An Upper-South region of Texas including San Antonio and Bexar County had the state's highest hysterectomy-corrected incidence of endometrial cancer and the largest increase over time, a study led by scientists at The University of Texas at San Antonio Health Science Center and the National Cancer Institute showed. Endometrial cancer is the most common gynecologic cancer in the United States. And while several types of cancer are declining across the country, endometrial cancer is on the rise, especially in South Texas. This type of cancer often is diagnosed early and is highly survivable, but both endometrial cancer incidence and mortality rates have increased sharply over the past 20 years. The recent study published in the journal Cancer Epidemiology provides a comprehensive examination of regional differences in endometrial cancer incidence and survival across Texas. Principal investigator Edward R. Kost, MD, professor of gynecologic oncology in the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology at the Long School of Medicine and gynecologic oncologist at the Mays Cancer Center at UT Health San Antonio, said the goal is to figure out why endometrial cancer rates are rising and which populations are most affected. The mission of the Mays Cancer Center is to reduce the burden of cancer in San Antonio, South Texas and beyond. To do that, we need to understand the cancers that affect our local population." Edward R. Kost, MD, professor of gynecologic oncology, Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, Long School of Medicine and gynecologic oncologist, Mays Cancer Center at UT Health San Antonio Rates highest in Upper-South Texas The research team analyzed population-based data from 42,571 Texas women ages 20 to 79 with endometrial cancer who were diagnosed between 2000 and 2019 using the National Cancer Institute's Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program. The analysis compared eight Texas Health Service Regions. It showed an Upper-South region including San Antonio and Bexar County with the highest hysterectomy-corrected incidence in the state and the largest increase over time, rising from 42.6 per 100,000 women in 2000-2009 to 53.8 per 100,000 in 2010-2019. The study found that among women under age 50, Hispanic women had the highest incidence of endometrial cancer compared to other groups included in the study. Among women under age 50, the statewide hysterectomy-corrected incidence rate was 12.4 per 100,000 among Hispanic women, compared with 8.9 among non-Hispanic white women and 5.6 among non-Hispanic Black women. In the Upper-South region, the early-onset incidence among Hispanic women reached 17.2 per 100,000. The study also found that non-Hispanic Black women had higher rates of non-endometrioid tumors – the more aggressive form of this cancer – and poorer five-year relative survival. "Part of our responsibility is to understand the cancer burden of the population we serve, and we found cancer disparities that are affecting our community," Kost said. Increased cancer rates in younger women A major concern is the high rates of endometrial cancer in younger women in Texas. "Our institutional tumor registry data from Bexar County suggest that approximately 25% of endometrial cancer patients were younger than 50 years at diagnosis, compared with approximately 15% nationally," Kost said. The most common treatment for endometrial cancer is hysterectomy, the surgical removal of the uterus. For carefully selected women with early endometrial cancer who want to preserve fertility, more conservative treatment options are available. These can include progestin therapy, often with an intrauterine device, close endometrial surveillance with repeat biopsies and efforts to address obesity and other metabolic risk factors, diet modification, exercise and consideration of weight reduction with GLP-1 medications or bariatric surgery when appropriate. Even when the cancer is successfully controlled and fertility is preserved, achieving pregnancy can present challenges. Many women may need to see a reproductive endocrinologist. The process can be grueling, expensive and not always successful. "Wanting to have a child is one of the strongest desires a human can have," Kost said. "We do everything we can to preserve a woman's ability to have children, but there are so many hurdles, and the primary goal is to get the cancer into remission." Finding the risk factors Kost said the research team hopes to study population-level differences at a more granular level to better understand factors that may contribute to the observed disparities. One of the greatest risk factors for endometrial cancer is increased body mass index (BMI). While scientists are still working to understand why endometrial cancer rates are increasing, rates of early onset endometrioid cancer have risen in parallel with increasing rates of obesity. BMI and individual-level metabolic factors, such as obesity, diabetes, hypertension and fatty liver disease, were not available variables in the SEER data used for this study. This initial study identifies the problem, and the next step in the research is a closer look at metabolic factors, social determinants of health, geographic differences and other known risk factors in the Upper-South region to see where there is overlap with endometrial cancer rates. Geospatial analysis to connect the dots Kost said the team plans to use geospatial analysis to examine how cancer rates overlap with various risk factors across populations. "We want to understand how cancer incidence and potential risk factors overlap at a much more local level, including census-tract-level geography," he said. "That would allow us to focus community interventions in the areas where they may be needed most." One of the strengths of the study is the collaborative team that brought together expertise in cancer epidemiology, population health, cancer biology and the gynecologic oncology group. "Ultimately, the purpose is to move from identifying disparities to understanding their causes and developing better prevention, early detection, outreach and care," Kost said.

Kost医学博士（朗医学院妇产科妇科肿瘤学教授，德克萨斯大学圣安东尼奥健康中心梅斯癌症中心的妇科肿瘤学家）表示，研究目标是弄清子宫内膜癌发病率上升的原因以及哪些人群受影响最大。 “梅斯癌症中心的使命是减轻圣安东尼奥、南德克萨斯及周边地区的癌症负担。为此，我们需要了解影响我们当地人群的癌症。”——Edward R. Kost医学博士，朗医学院妇产科妇科肿瘤学教授，德克萨斯大学圣安东尼奥健康中心梅斯癌症中心的妇科肿瘤学家。 上南德克萨斯州发病率最高 研究团队利用国家癌症研究所的监测、流行病学和最终结果（SEER）项目，分析了2000年至2019年间诊断为子宫内膜癌的42,571名年龄在20至79岁德克萨斯州女性的人口数据。该分析比较了德克萨斯州的八个卫生服务区。 结果显示，包括圣安东尼奥和贝尔县在内的上南部地区，其子宫切除术校正后的发病率在全州最高，且随着时间推移增幅最大，从2000-2009年的每10万女性42.6例上升至2010-2019年的每10万女性53.8例。 研究发现，在50岁以下女性中，西班牙裔女性的子宫内膜癌发病率高于研究中包含的其他群体。 在50岁以下女性中，全州西班牙裔女性的子宫切除术校正发病率为每10万女性12.4例，而非西班牙裔白人女性为8.9例，非西班牙裔黑人女性为5.6例。在上南部地区，西班牙裔女性的早发性发病率达到每10万女性17.2例。 研究还发现，非西班牙裔黑人女性的非子宫内膜样肿瘤（这种癌症更具侵袭性的形式）发病率更高，且五年相对生存率更差。 科斯特表示：“我们部分职责是了解我们所服务人群的癌症负担，我们发现了正在影响我们社区的癌症健康不平等现象。” 年轻女性癌症发病率增加 子宫内膜癌在德克萨斯州年轻女性中的高发病率是一个主要担忧。 科斯特表示：“我们来自贝尔县的机构肿瘤登记数据显示，大约25%的子宫内膜癌患者在诊断时年龄小于50岁，而全国范围内的这一比例约为15%。” 子宫内膜癌最常见的治疗方法是子宫切除术，即通过手术切除子宫。 对于经仔细筛选、希望保留生育能力的早期子宫内膜癌女性，有更保守的治疗选择。这些选择可能包括孕激素治疗（常与宫内节育器结合使用）、通过反复活检进行严密子宫内膜监测，以及努力解决肥胖和其他代谢风险因素、调整饮食、增加运动，并在适当时考虑使用GLP-1类药物或进行减重手术来减轻体重。 即使癌症成功得到控制并保留了生育能力，实现怀孕仍可能面临挑战。许多女性可能需要咨询生殖内分泌学家。这一过程可能漫长、昂贵，且并非总能成功。 科斯特说：“想要孩子是人类最强烈的愿望之一。我们竭尽所能保留女性生育孩子的能力，但其中存在诸多障碍，首要目标是使癌症获得缓解。” 寻找风险因素 科斯特表示，研究团队希望在更细致的层面研究人群差异，以更好地理解可能导致观察到的健康不平等现象的因素。 子宫内膜癌最大的风险因素之一是体重指数（BMI）升高。尽管科学家仍在努力理解子宫内膜癌发病率上升的原因，但早发性子宫内膜样癌的发病率与肥胖率的上升同步增加。 体重指数和个体层面的代谢因素，如肥胖、糖尿病、高血压和脂肪肝，在本研究使用的SEER数据中并非可用变量。 这项初步研究指出了问题，下一步研究将更深入地审视上南部地区的代谢因素、健康社会决定因素、地理差异以及其他已知风险因素，以查明它们与子宫内膜癌发病率的重叠情况。 地理空间分析以连接各项数据 科斯特表示，研究团队计划利用地理空间分析来检查不同人群中癌症发病率与各种风险因素的重叠情况。 他说：“我们希望在更本地化的层面，包括普查区级别的地理范围，了解癌症发病率和潜在风险因素如何重叠。这将使我们能够将社区干预措施集中在最需要的区域。” 这项研究的优势之一是其协作团队，汇集了癌症流行病学、人群健康、癌症生物学和妇科肿瘤学领域的专业知识。 科斯特表示：“最终，我们的目的是从识别健康不平等现象，转向理解其成因，并制定更好的预防、早期发现、宣传和护理措施。”
