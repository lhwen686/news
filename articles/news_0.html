Protein buildup mechanism shapes response to PROTAC drugs

深度解析

PROTAC（蛋白水解靶向嵌合体）代表了一种革新性的癌症治疗策略。与传统小分子药物抑制蛋白质活性不同，PROTACs通过劫持细胞自身的泛素-蛋白酶体系统，靶向性地诱导致病蛋白质的泛素化修饰，进而导致其被蛋白酶体识别、降解和清除。这项研究的核心发现揭示了肿瘤中致癌蛋白积累的根本原因，对PROTAC疗效具有决定性影响。当致癌蛋白因降解障碍而积累时，PROTACs能高效将其水平降低；但若积累是由于癌细胞过量生产新蛋白，PROTACs的清除效果则受限，因为新的蛋白质仍在持续生成。 这一发现的专业意义在于，它将对靶向蛋白降解疗法的理解从单纯的“蛋白质表达量”提升到“蛋白质代谢动力学”的深层机制。它强调了肿瘤内蛋白质合成与降解平衡的关键作用，对精准医学领域影响深远。从行业角度看，该研究成果有助于优化PROTACs的临床应用。医生能更准确地筛选出最有可能受益的患者，避免无效治疗。同时，它也为新型PROTAC药物的研发指明了方向，例如结合抑制蛋白合成的药物，以应对过量生产的情况。这无疑推动了癌症治疗向更高效、更个性化的方向发展，最终提升患者的治疗反应率和生活质量。

英文原文

Original
The way cancer-causing proteins accumulate in tumors may influence how effectively a promising new generation of medicines can remove them, a study suggests. Researchers found that PROTAC (Proteolysis Targeting Chimeras) drugs were more effective at removing proteins that build up because they are not broken down properly than proteins that cancer cells continually produce in large amounts. Experts say the findings could help identify which tumors are most likely to respond to these therapies, supporting the development of more personalized cancer treatments. PROTACs are a new class of medicines that destroy harmful proteins by harnessing the cell's natural waste-disposal system. Unlike conventional drugs, which typically block the activity of disease-causing proteins, PROTACs are designed to remove them altogether. Many cancers depend on high levels of proteins that drive uncontrolled cell growth. These proteins can accumulate either because they are not broken down properly or because cancer cells produce them in excess, but it was unclear whether this affects how well PROTACs work. Researchers from the University of Edinburgh used β-catenin, a protein involved in many cancers, to investigate this question. The team developed methods to make β-catenin either harder for cells to break down or quicker for cells to produce. They then tested how effectively a PROTAC could remove it. The results revealed a clear difference between the two mechanisms. When β-catenin accumulated because it was not being broken down properly, the PROTAC was able to reduce protein levels to a similar minimum level regardless of how much had built up. However, when cells were producing β-catenin at a higher rate, protein levels remained elevated after treatment because new protein continued to be made. Researchers say the findings suggest that the success of PROTAC therapies may depend not only on how much of a cancer-driving protein is present, but also on why it has accumulated in the first place. By improving understanding of how tumor genetics shapes responses to targeted protein degradation, this study could help researchers identify which patients are most likely to benefit from these therapies and support the development of more personalized approaches to cancer treatment." Andrew Wood, Principal Investigator, University of Edinburgh's Institute of Genetics and Cancer Professor Matthew Walters, Chair of Medical Research Scotland, said: "Medical Research Scotland is proud to have supported this important study, which we hope will help in the development of more targeted therapies for cancer. This research reflects our commitment to backing high-quality work with the potential to improve lives in Scotland and beyond."
