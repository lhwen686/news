Aspirin does not prevent recurrence after treatment for colorectal cancer liver metastases

深度解析

这篇新闻深入探讨了阿司匹林在预防结直肠癌肝转移根治性治疗后复发方面的疗效，通过 ASAC 这项关键的随机、双盲、安慰剂对照试验，提供了强有力的临床证据。文章的核心专业概念在于对不同研究设计证据等级的区分：既往的观察性研究虽曾提示阿司匹林可能与较低的癌症复发风险和更高的生存率相关，但这类研究只能揭示关联性，无法确证因果关系。相比之下，ASAC 试验作为一项高标准的随机对照试验（RCT），旨在通过严格的对照组和双盲设计，明确阿司匹林对肝转移患者复发率的真实影响，其结果对临床实践具有决定性的指导意义。 文章还触及了肿瘤生物学的复杂性及其对治疗反应的影响。虽然另一项 ALASCCA 试验发现阿司匹林在肿瘤携带特定 PI3K 信号通路分子变异的局部结直肠癌患者中显示出益处，但 ASAC 试验却未能在结直肠癌肝转移患者中观察到类似效果，即使是那些具有相似分子变异的患者。这突显了疾病阶段（如局部肿瘤与远处转移）和肿瘤分子特征的异质性在指导个性化治疗中的重要性。研究结果明确指出，不应常规推荐阿司匹林预防结直肠癌肝转移根治性治疗后的复发，并强调了现有服用阿司匹林患者应在医生指导下调整用药，避免潜在的风险，这直接影响了肿瘤学和肝胆外科的临床决策。

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: Credit: Pixabay/CC0 Public Domain Colorectal cancer is one of the most common cancers in Norway, which also has one of the highest incidence rates in the world. Approximately 30% of patients with colorectal cancer develop liver metastases during the course of their disease. Once the disease has spread, it is much more difficult to treat than primary colorectal cancer confined to the bowel. "Patients rarely die from the primary tumor itself. It is the metastatic disease that is usually life-threatening," says Sheraz Yaqub, professor at the University of Oslo and consultant hepatobiliary surgeon at Oslo University Hospital. For many patients, surgery offers the best chance of a cure. "But even when we remove the tumors surgically, the cancer returns in more than half of patients," says Yaqub. Previous studies have suggested a protective effect of aspirin Previous observational studies have suggested that aspirin use after colorectal cancer treatment may be associated with a lower risk of recurrence and improved survival. More recent randomized trials have investigated whether aspirin can provide such benefits, particularly in patients with specific molecular alterations in their tumors. Yaqub and his colleagues wanted to find out whether aspirin could have the same preventive effect in patients whose colorectal cancer had spread to the liver. "We wanted to know whether we could prevent the cancer from returning by giving aspirin to these patients as well," Yaqub says. The study has now been published in The Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology. An inexpensive and widely available medicine Aspirin has long been used to treat pain, fever and inflammation. Its potential to prevent cancer recurrence has therefore attracted considerable interest. "Aspirin is inexpensive, widely available and has been used for more than a century. Its effects and side effects are well known, which made it an attractive candidate for preventing cancer recurrence," says Yaqub. Could aspirin prevent recurrence after treatment for liver metastases? The researchers conducted ASAC, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial investigating whether aspirin could reduce the risk of recurrence after curative-intent treatment of colorectal cancer liver metastases. A total of 466 patients were randomized at 14 hospitals in Norway, Sweden and Denmark. All had undergone treatment (surgery or local ablation) for colorectal cancer liver metastases. The participants were randomly assigned to receive either aspirin 160 mg daily or an identical placebo for up to three years. "For most patients, the cancer returns within the first two years after surgery. That is why we followed participants for three years," says Yaqub. No reduction in recurrence with aspirin The researchers found that aspirin did not reduce the risk of the cancer returning, compared with placebo. Patients who received aspirin also had a lower overall survival rate, but this finding should be interpreted with caution. Serious adverse events were more common in the aspirin group. "We found no benefit from aspirin in preventing cancer recurrence in these patients. The finding of lower overall survival, together with a higher frequency of serious adverse events, also raises concerns," says Yaqub. "Our results show that aspirin should not be routinely recommended to prevent recurrence after curative-intent treatment of colorectal cancer liver metastases," he adds. Why randomized trials matter In an earlier study, Yaqub and colleagues linked information from the Norwegian Cancer Registry and the Norwegian Prescription Database. That registry-based study suggested that patients with colorectal cancer who were taking aspirin lived longer than those who were not. However, studies based on health registries cannot show with certainty whether a treatment was the reason for the difference. "Registry studies can identify associations, but they cannot establish whether aspirin itself is responsible for improved survival. Randomized, placebo-controlled trials provide much stronger evidence about the effects of treatment," he explains. Why might aspirin benefit some patients but not others? The reasons are not yet clear, but differences in disease stage and tumor biology may play a role. The ASAC trial was conducted alongside the Scandinavian ALASCCA trial. Both studies examined molecular changes in tumor tissue. The ALASCCA study found that aspirin reduced recurrence in some patients with localized colorectal cancer whose tumors carried specific alterations in the PI3K signaling pathway. In contrast, the ASAC trial found no benefit for patients who had been treated for colorectal cancer that had spread to the liver, including those with the molecular alterations examined in the study. "What works for one group of patients does not necessarily work for another. We cannot simply transfer results from patients with primary colorectal cancer to patients who have been treated for liver metastases," says Yaqub. Do not stop taking aspirin without medical advice The ASAC trial investigated the effect of starting aspirin after cancer treatment. Patients who were already taking aspirin were not eligible to take part. The findings therefore do not mean that people who already take aspirin for other medical reasons should stop treatment. "Patients who are already taking aspirin for cardiovascular disease or other medical indications should not stop treatment because of these findings. Any changes should be discussed with their treating physician," Yaqub says.
