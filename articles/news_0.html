Spaceflight research opens new avenues for studying rare pediatric diseases

深度解析

这篇综述深入探讨了利用太空环境作为研究平台，以克服地基人诱导多能干细胞（hiPSC）模型在细胞不成熟和代谢偏倚等方面的局限性。核心机制在于，微重力和空间辐射作为独特的生物物理应激源，能够靶向线粒体和心血管通路，进而影响细胞的基因表达、三磷酸腺苷（ATP）产生、活性氧（ROS）积累及钙稳态。这些变化不仅模拟了许多罕见代谢疾病和先天性心脏病（CHDs）的病理生理特征，甚至能促进细胞加速成熟，从而提供更接近生理状态的疾病模型。通过在太空研究患者来源的hiPSC，研究人员旨在揭示在常规培养条件下难以捕捉的疾病相关脆弱性和分子机制。这对于线粒体疾病和CHDs尤为关键，因为这些疾病常涉及能量代谢紊乱和结构发育异常。这种“应激测试”模式有望加速药物靶点识别和现有药物的再利用，例如通过测试干预措施能否挽救线粒体呼吸和ATP生成。同时，太空研究还能为地球上的类器官和芯片器官系统提供验证数据，并为宇航员健康提供预防和干预策略，真正实现罕见病研究与人类太空探索的双向转化医学突破。

英文原文

Original
Earth-based hiPSC models have transformed disease research but remain limited by cellular immaturity, a strong reliance on glycolysis rather than oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), and incomplete tissue architecture. Animal models often fail to recapitulate human phenotypes, and primary patient tissues are difficult to obtain, especially for rare pediatric conditions. Spaceflight exposes cells to microgravity and space radiation, stress conditions that may unmask or amplify disease-relevant vulnerabilities. Because these stressors converge on mitochondrial and cardiac pathways, they offer a biologically distinct context for studying rare metabolic and congenital disorders. Conventional culture also differs in oxygen tension. Based on these challenges, deeper investigation is needed into how patient-derived human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) models respond to the space environment. A review published (DOI: 10.1093/pcmedi/pbag028) in Precision Clinical Medicine in 2026 by researchers affiliated with IRCCS Policlinico San Donato, the University of Milan, and Weill Cornell Medicine, with collaborators from multiple institutions, examines current space-based hiPSC studies and their relevance to mitochondrial diseases and congenital heart disorders (CHDs), and their potential to inform rare pediatric disease research. It summarizes reported effects on mitochondrial function, cytoskeletal organization, oxidative stress, calcium handling, and gene expression, and outlines experimental and mechanistic gaps that must be addressed before patient-derived models can be deployed in orbit. The authors report that spaceflight and simulated microgravity can produce both adaptive and pathological responses in hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes and neurons. Studies aboard the International Space Station (ISS) and ground-based simulators have shown altered mitochondrial gene expression, reduced adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production, increased reactive oxygen species (ROS), and changes in calcium handling and contractility. Some experiments also suggest accelerated cellular maturation under microgravity, a potentially valuable effect because immature phenotypes remain a major limitation of Earth-based models. However, results vary with exposure duration, cell type, and culture format, and no study has yet examined patient-derived hiPSCs carrying pathogenic mitochondrial disease or CHD variants in space. The review highlights the need to distinguish the individual and combined effects of microgravity and space radiation, and to determine whether pre-existing metabolic defects impair adaptation or exacerbate damage. It also notes that most space studies rely on transcriptional data with limited functional validation. The authors emphasize that adaptive maturation signals and pathological stress responses may coexist and must be separated experimentally. Key genetic examples include SURF1, MT-ATP6, POLG, TAZ, NKX2-5, GATA4, TBX5, HAND2, MYH7, MYBPC3, RYR2, and CALM1-3. The authors said the space environment could serve as a discovery platform that reveals disease-relevant mechanisms difficult to detect under conventional culture conditions. They said patient-derived hiPSC models of mitochondrial diseases or CHDs have not yet been tested in space, representing an important gap, and that future experiments should compare microgravity, radiation, and combined exposure with appropriate ground and in-flight controls. They also said promising findings would need validation in terrestrial disease models, including organoids and organ-on-chip systems, before any clinical translation. The proposed framework positions space as a stress-testing environment and Earth as the validation ground. Shared molecular signatures between spaceflight stress and disease could guide drug repurposing and target identification, with candidate interventions tested for rescue of mitochondrial respiration, ATP production, ROS accumulation, and calcium handling. Microgravity-based biomanufacturing could also support tissue engineering and regenerative medicine, provided that cells and tissues retain their desired properties after return to Earth. The authors note that spaceflight-associated cardiovascular and neurological changes may inform astronaut health, creating a two-way translational link between rare pediatric disease research and human space exploration. This framework could accelerate translational discovery for rare pediatric disorders while informing countermeasures for astronauts. Human induced pluripotent stem cells and organoid models in space for investigating severe pediatric metabolic and congenital disorders. Precision Clinical Medicine.
