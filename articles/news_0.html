A new Europe-wide survey launched during the 42nd Annual Meeting of the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE) suggests broad public support for fertility treatment and several areas of reproductive research, while highlighting key areas where public understanding and opinion continue to develop.

The report, "Fertility, Embryo Research and Genome Editing: Public Attitudes in Europe," was commissioned by the charity Progress Educational Trust (PET), supported by ESHRE and conducted by Ipsos. It explored public attitudes toward fertility treatment, embryo research, genome editing, surrogacy and related topics across the UK, the Netherlands, Spain and Italy.

The survey included 8,688 participants ages 16–75, with more than 2,000 respondents in each country. Samples were designed to be representative of national populations according to age, gender, region and working status.

Across all four countries, a majority of respondents supported state-funded fertility treatment for people experiencing infertility and wishing to conceive, ranging from 54% in the Netherlands to 64% in Italy.

The survey also found public backing for the use of human embryos in research to better understand and develop treatments for congenital diseases. Support ranged from 41% in Italy to 48% in the Netherlands and Spain, exceeding opposition in all four countries surveyed, including Italy, where research uses of human embryos are currently prohibited.

Views became more nuanced when respondents were asked about emerging applications and future developments. Support for genome editing varied depending on its intended use, with the strongest support seen for eliminating severe or life-threatening conditions in embryos intended for pregnancy, ranging from 46% in Italy to 55% in the Netherlands.

A majority of respondents in the UK, the Netherlands and Spain supported this use, despite such applications not currently being permitted in any of the four countries surveyed.

Professor Karen Sermon, immediate past chair of ESHRE, said, "Reproductive medicine and embryo research are advancing rapidly, and these findings show the importance of understanding how the public views those developments. It is particularly striking that support for some applications extends beyond what is currently permitted in certain countries.

"As science advances, it is essential that public awareness keeps pace, so that decisions about future treatments are informed by both evidence and societal values."

The survey also highlighted areas where many people had not yet formed firm opinions. Among respondents who supported, or neither supported nor opposed, the use of human embryos in research, the most common view was that the current 14-day limit on embryo research is "about right."

However, more than a quarter (27%) of respondents asked about the 14-day limit said they did not know whether it was too long, too short or about right.

Respondents were also asked what circumstances, if any, might justify extending the 14-day limit to 28 days. Among those who supported, or neither supported nor opposed, embryo research, the most popular justification for extending the limit was finding new treatments for congenital diseases.

Taken together, these findings suggest that many people have not yet reached a settled view on this issue and that understanding the potential medical benefits of embryo research may play an important role in shaping future attitudes.

Generational differences in attitudes were observed across several topics explored in the survey, including sex selection, fertility treatment access and embryo research. Across the survey as a whole, only a small proportion of respondents selected "Prefer not to answer," suggesting a willingness to engage with these issues even where views had not yet been fully formed.

"This report is filled with rich insights into the public's views on scientifically, legally and ethically complex issues," said Sarah Norcross, director of the Progress Educational Trust.

"There are some surprising findings, such as younger respondents in all four countries being more likely than older respondents to say that sex selection should be permitted. It is also striking that in each of the four countries, the number of respondents saying that surrogacy should be permitted exceeds the number who say that surrogacy should be prohibited."

在欧洲人类生殖与胚胎学会（ESHRE）第42届年会上发布的一项新的欧洲范围调查显示，公众对生育治疗和多项生殖研究领域普遍支持，同时也指出了公众理解和看法仍在发展的关键领域。

这份名为《生育、胚胎研究和基因组编辑：欧洲公众态度》的报告由进步教育信托基金（PET）委托，欧洲人类生殖与胚胎学会（ESHRE）支持，并由益普索（Ipsos）执行。它调查了英国、荷兰、西班牙和意大利公众对生育治疗、胚胎研究、基因组编辑、代孕及相关议题的态度。

该调查涵盖了8,688名16至75岁的参与者，每个国家有超过2,000名受访者。样本设计旨在根据年龄、性别、地区和就业状况，代表各自国家的整体人口。

在这四个国家中，大多数受访者支持为不孕不育并希望怀孕的人提供国家资助的生育治疗，支持率从荷兰的54%到意大利的64%不等。

调查还发现，公众支持在研究中使用人类胚胎，以更好地理解和开发先天性疾病的治疗方法。支持率从意大利的41%到荷兰和西班牙的48%不等，在所有四个受调查国家中都超过了反对率，包括目前禁止人类胚胎用于研究的意大利。

当被问及新兴应用和未来发展时，公众的看法变得更加细致。对基因组编辑的支持因其预期用途而异，其中支持率最高的是消除拟用于怀孕的胚胎中的严重或危及生命的疾病，支持率从意大利的46%到荷兰的55%不等。

尽管在所有四个受调查国家中，此类应用目前均不被允许，但英国、荷兰和西班牙的大多数受访者仍支持这种用途。

欧洲人类生殖与胚胎学会（ESHRE）前任主席凯伦·瑟蒙教授表示：“生殖医学和胚胎研究正在迅速发展，这些发现表明了了解公众如何看待这些发展的重要性。尤其引人注目的是，对某些应用的支持范围超出了某些国家目前允许的范畴。”

“随着科学的进步，公众认知同步发展至关重要，这样才能确保未来治疗的决策既有科学依据，又符合社会价值观。”

该调查还强调了一些公众尚未形成明确意见的领域。在支持或既不支持也不反对人类胚胎研究的受访者中，最普遍的看法是当前胚胎研究的14天限制“恰到好处”。

然而，在被问及14天限制的受访者中，超过四分之一（27%）的人表示，他们不确定这个期限是太长、太短还是恰到好处。

受访者还被问及，在何种情况下（如果有的话）可以证明将14天限制延长至28天是合理的。在支持或既不支持也不反对胚胎研究的人群中，延长限制最普遍的理由是寻找先天性疾病的新治疗方法。

总而言之，这些发现表明许多人尚未就此问题达成一致看法，而理解胚胎研究的潜在医疗益处可能在塑造未来态度方面发挥重要作用。

调查中探讨的多个议题都观察到态度的代际差异，包括性别选择、生育治疗的可及性以及胚胎研究。就整个调查而言，只有一小部分受访者选择了“不愿回答”，这表明即使观点尚未完全形成，公众也愿意参与讨论这些问题。

进步教育信托基金主任莎拉·诺克罗斯表示：“这份报告充满了对公众在科学、法律和伦理复杂问题上观点的深刻见解。”

“有一些令人惊讶的发现，例如在所有四个国家中，年轻受访者比年长受访者更有可能表示应允许性别选择。同样引人注目的是，在这四个国家中的每一个，认为代孕应被允许的受访者数量都超过了认为应被禁止的受访者数量。”
