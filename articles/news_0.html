宾夕法尼亚州老化的监狱未考虑当今极端天气而建造，使妇女和老年人面临风险

Pennsylvania's aging prisons weren't built for today's extreme weather—leaving women and seniors at risk

深度解析

本文通过流行病学研究揭示了极端温度对宾夕法尼亚州监狱内被监禁者健康与死亡率的严重影响。核心专业概念“加速衰老”在此背景下尤为突出：由于监禁前的创伤、监禁期间的压力、营养不良、医疗资源有限等因素，被监禁者的生理年龄早于实际年龄，导致他们更早出现更年期、癌症和慢性病，使其对环境极端温度的耐受性大幅下降。例如，被监禁女性的潮热、盗汗等更年期症状在高温下若缺乏降温、补水或激素替代疗法，可能引发危及生命的脱水或热射病。 这种脆弱性对行业产生了深远影响。首先，它加剧了本已不堪重负的监狱医疗系统压力，导致死亡率、医务室就诊和外部转院率增加，增加了医疗资源消耗和行政负担。其次，这凸显了矫正医疗领域在应对气候变化时的挑战，要求监狱设施进行气候韧性规划，包括改善基础设施（如空调）、强化医疗服务。从公共卫生角度看，被监禁群体的健康问题并非孤立存在，其慢性病负担和极端环境下的健康风险，在释放后可能进一步影响社区健康，因此改善监狱健康照护是广义公共卫生的重要组成部分。此外，未能有效应对这些风险还可能引发法律和伦理争议，涉及对囚犯医疗需求“故意漠视”的宪法禁止。

Editors have highlighted the following attributes while ensuring the content's credibility: Credit: Unsplash/CC0 Public Domain When outdoor temperatures spike to 103 degrees Fahrenheit (39 degrees Celsius), or drop to -9 F (-23 C), like they did across Pennsylvania last year, people locked up in jails and prisons cannot simply turn on the air conditioning or find a public warming center. For several years, people in custody, advocates and local media have sounded the alarm about the dangers posed by extreme temperatures in Pennsylvania's state prisons. Conditions inside Pennsylvania's prisons have been described as a sauna during a summer heat wave and bitterly cold in the winter, especially when heating systems have malfunctioned. Amy McBride, a woman in her early 60s incarcerated at MCI Muncy, told the Prison Journalism Project, "I tend to wear two to three layers of pants and four to six layers of shirts (in winter), sometimes with my jacket and coat over them." I am an epidemiologist who studies the health of incarcerated people. My colleagues and I put numbers to these warnings in a recent study. We found that extremely hot and cold temperatures were associated with higher rates of deaths, infirmary admissions and transfers to external medical facilities among imprisoned people in Pennsylvania over the past 20 years. The paper is published in the journal Nature Communications. While these temperature hazards are not unique to Pennsylvania, several factors make this issue a particular concern for the state. Pennsylvania has the nation's sixth-largest prison population among U.S. states. Nearly 40,000 people are confined in Pennsylvania prisons. Furthermore, most of Pennsylvania's state prison facilities were built in the 1990s or earlier, when weather patterns were not so extreme. Some, including the state correctional institutions in Cambridge Springs, Dallas, Laurel Highlands, Muncy, Waymart and Huntingdon, the oldest continuously running correctional facility in Pennsylvania, have partial or very limited air conditioning. This means they have air conditioning in some buildings or units, such as nursing care areas, and not others. Previous research also suggests that while hot temperatures pose risks to incarcerated people across the country, those incarcerated in the Northeast have experienced some of the greatest increases in heat-related mortality risk since 2001. Prison health care systems under strain Even under normal weather conditions, prison health care systems are highly strained. People in prison are more likely than the general population to have chronic medical conditions such as heart disease, and the state's prison population is getting older, which increases its medical needs and expenses. The average age at prison admission has increased from 31 in 2000 to 38 in 2023. The average cost for medications alone for adults over 50 in the state's prisons is $3,357 per person per year. That's 53% more than costs for younger incarcerated adults. The state prison system experiences chronic staffing shortages of corrections officers, nurses and physicians. Some facilities have experienced vacancy rates of medical staff of nearly 40%. Shortages of corrections officers result in greater restrictions on the movement of incarcerated people, who then have less access to health appointments, group therapy and other programming. This can be especially difficult for older incarcerated people and those who have been incarcerated for long periods of time, because they have greater medical needs and are often more isolated from loved ones. Constitutional protections against cruel and unusual punishment prohibit deliberate indifference to prisoners' medical needs. But in addition to staffing shortages and other administrative challenges with health care delivery in prisons, incarcerated people also face financial barriers. In Pennsylvania, they must pay a $5 copay per visit to receive medical treatment that is determined by the Department of Correction to not be an emergency. This may sound low but is substantial in the context of wages that range from 23 cents to 50 cents per hour. Incarcerated women may face higher heat risks One unexpected finding from our analysis was that women in Pennsylvania state prisons experienced greater increases in rates of infirmary visits and transfers to external medical facilities on and after extremely hot days than men. This was unexpected because previous studies haven't consistently shown that women are more vulnerable to heat-related health risks. But these studies were conducted in the general population, not among people in prison. Incarcerated people experience what medical researchers call "accelerated aging" because of the stressors they experience during and prior to incarceration. This means their biologic age is ahead of their chronological age. They are more likely to experience menopause, cancer and chronic disease at a younger age than would typically be expected. Hot flashes and night sweats are common symptoms of menopause that can become life-threatening during extreme heat events and when cooling, hydration and hormone therapy are limited or unavailable. Preparing for a warmer climate Our research found that extreme heat and extreme cold increase the demands on an already strained system of prison operations and health care delivery. These demands will likely only get worse as extreme temperatures continue to intensify. We believe climate resilience planning will require improvements in prison conditions, improvements in prison health care systems and policy efforts to reduce the number of people—particularly older adults—who are incarcerated. Publication details Jaquelyn L. Jahn et al., Mortality and healthcare services in Pennsylvania state prisons following extreme ambient temperatures, Nature Communications (2026).
